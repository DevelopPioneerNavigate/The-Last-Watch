**THE TWELVE TRACKS — 0936 HOURS**

A dozen red marks appeared on the display, and for one terrible instant I wanted them to stay still.

They did not. A tactical picture is a living thing when missiles are flying, and every second changes it. The watch team called out bearings, classifications and updates in disciplined bursts. The marks moved toward the convoy through an ocean that had already swallowed one ship and put nineteen survivors aboard another.

Twelve weapons. Three merchant ships. Two escorts that could not afford to be wrong.

Hassan Idris stood over the console with both hands braced against its edge. He was not shouting. The softer he spoke, the more carefully his operators listened.

"Track correlation?"

"Updating."

"Confirm independently."

"Working."

I could see his lips moving through a count that was not audible to the rest of us.

"You have a recommendation?" I asked.

"Yes, Captain."

He gave it in the form I'd demanded throughout the passage: what was known, what remained uncertain, and which defensive actions he considered justified. The recommendations were grave. Our limited remaining weapons were not a margin that could simply be replenished when the next wave came.

I authorized the necessary defense.

The launches struck the ship like blows delivered from beneath the deck. Northstar trembled, recovered, and kept moving. Over the command circuit I heard the bridge acknowledge the maneuver and the weapons teams confirm their work.

I knew there were men and women in the forward compartments feeling the launches as pressure through their boots. They had no tactical display. They were judging the danger by sound.

An operator called, "Two tracks merging."

Another said, "No, one is dropping."

For an instant both voices spoke at once.

Idris lifted one hand.

"One at a time. We don't improve the picture by shouting over each other."

The first operator repeated the observation. The second corrected her earlier report. Nobody lost face. Nobody wasted time apologizing.

I thought of Chief Ramirez and her training drills. The habits we'd practiced during quieter months were now holding the room together.

"Captain," Farouk said, stepping alongside me, "we have a discrepancy between local and shared information. The common picture is lagging."

"By how much?"

"Enough that I'd prefer the local assessments for the immediate engagement."

I looked at Idris.

"Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Then use what we can verify."

The ship rolled. One of the young operators grabbed the edge of his chair without looking away from his screen. Sweat glistened under his headset. Somewhere beyond the steel walls an enemy pilot or sailor had launched weapons toward people he'd never met.

No one in Combat could see the faces aboard Aurora. I had made the mistake of picturing them too often, and now the images came without invitation: a little girl with a red crayon, a teacher folding paper boats, a mother trying to make a child believe a ferry was a safe place.

I forced my attention back to Idris.

"What about the British?"

"Cutlass is engaging on the western side."

The allied circuit came alive.

"Northstar, Cutlass. We have the western approach."

Mallory's voice carried a faint metallic distortion. But it was unmistakably Edward—my friend, my equal in this strange duty, and a captain with sailors of his own to protect.

"Understood," I answered. "Keep us informed."

"Wouldn't dream of withholding a good story."

He sounded infuriatingly cheerful.

I nearly smiled.

Then our operator called a new track on a course that might threaten Aurora.

It appeared late enough to be frightening. The room tightened around it.

"Threat classification?"

Idris gave his judgment. Not a speech. A few necessary words.

"Defensive action recommended."

"Authorized."

The console's lights shifted. A timer advanced. Somewhere on our decks a weapon departed into the freezing air.

The seconds that followed became immense.

I could hear my own breathing.

The operator reported that the defensive engagement had taken place. Then the hostile track disappeared.

"Threat no longer detected on the expected course," she said.

"Confirm," Idris answered.

The next report supported defeat of that immediate threat, but there was no celebration. Another track remained. Another might still appear.

I turned toward Mara.

"How's the bridge?"

"Maintaining control. Mercer has the helm team steady. Bishop is watching the merchant positions."

"Any casualty reports?"

"None so far."

So far.

It was a phrase I had begun to hate.

Cross arrived at the edge of the room with a message from a repair locker. A sailor had been knocked against a fitting during the earlier maneuver and was receiving medical attention. Another had reported a temporary equipment fault. Neither was catastrophic.

Both mattered.

"Get medical updates when there's time," I said. "No unnecessary interruptions during the engagement."

"Understood."

He lingered.

"What do you need from me, Captain?"

The answer came before I could think.

"Keep them doing the ordinary things correctly."

Cross nodded.

"That I can manage."

He disappeared toward the passageway.

The attack continued.

On the shared display, one hostile mark split into conflicting interpretations. Idris demanded independent confirmation before treating it as two weapons. For five long seconds we could not establish which interpretation was right.

"Captain, we may be looking at an error in correlation," he said.

"Then don't count an error as a weapon."

"But maintain the threat assessment," he replied.

"Exactly."

I knew how maddening it was to make decisions while the instruments themselves seemed to debate reality. A missile could not be persuaded to wait while we resolved an argument between two displays.

Yet mistaking the display for the world was another way to die.

At 0939 Mallory came onto the circuit again.

"Northstar, Cutlass. Two inbound on our sector. We're defending."

"Copy."

I wanted to tell him to turn away, to save his ship. But he knew the duty he had accepted. He was in the best position to cover that approach. If he abandoned it, a vulnerable merchant would inherit the threat.

"Edward," I said.

"Yes, Daniel?"

"Watch yourself."

A pause.

"Always."

The radio clicked.

I looked at the British symbol. It moved slightly as Cutlass maneuvered. Beyond it the threatening marks kept coming.

There are moments when a commander knows the decision is correct and still wishes somebody else had made it. This was one. I had not ordered Edward into danger. The mission had. But I could feel the weight of his answer as though I'd placed it on his deck myself.

The watch called another defensive result.

One threat was gone.

One remained.

I moved closer to the display.

For an instant I imagined that if I stared hard enough I might see the British frigate, her sailors and my friend all moving through the same seconds that were passing aboard Northstar.

But there was only a symbol.

Idris said, "Captain—"

I turned.

He was watching the western approach.

The next few seconds belonged to Cutlass.

And there was nothing I could do except keep my ship ready for what followed.
