**THE DARK BETWEEN THE ALARMS — 2251 HOURS**

There is a kind of quiet that follows combat, when every ordinary sound seems like an approaching threat.

At 2251 the sonar contact remained uncertain. Bell's team had tracked faint machinery noise for more than half an hour and could not say whether it belonged to the submarine that had shadowed the convoy earlier. The sea outside had become a wall of darkness interrupted by whitecaps. Even the bridge cameras showed little more than spray.

I stepped into the passageway outside Combat and listened to the ship.

A pump started somewhere below. The ventilation changed pitch. A deck plate rattled. I knew all three sounds, but after the fire each made me pause.

Cross was waiting at the mess-deck ladder.

"Captain, I need to show you something."

"Can it wait?"

"Probably. But I don't think it should."

He led me into the mess decks.

There were fewer sailors than usual for that hour. Most of the crew were asleep or trying to sleep. Several watchstanders ate in silence near the galley. At the far end of the room a small group had gathered around a notebook.

I recognized Rios's handwriting on the open page, though Rios himself was in medical.

"What is this?" I asked.

One of the sailors rose.

"Chief Ramirez's qualification book, sir. We found it in her locker. She kept notes on what each of us was working on."

He turned several pages.

Beside every name was a short observation.

*Good with machinery. Needs confidence on oral boards.*

*Asks the right questions when nobody else wants to.*

*Can lead a team if given the chance.*

There were no grand declarations. No speeches about honor or sacrifice. Just a chief who had been paying attention to people.

"What are you going to do with it?" I asked.

"We want to finish the things she was helping us with."

I looked at Cross.

He said nothing. He didn't need to.

The sailor turned to another page. There was a note beside Rios's name.

*Strong instincts. Remind him not to confuse speed with competence. Could be a very good chief someday.*

I stared at the words.

Rios had been aboard less than a year. Ramirez had already imagined a future for him that he could not yet see.

The sailor carefully closed the notebook.

"We thought you should know."

I nodded.

"Keep the records with her effects. Take a copy of the training notes if the family and command arrangements permit. And when Rios is ready, let him read what she wrote."

"Yes, Captain."

I left the mess with Cross beside me.

"You knew about the notebook?"

"I knew she was keeping one. Didn't know what she wrote."

We climbed a ladder slowly because the ship was rolling and neither of us trusted our tired legs.

"She would have made a fine command master chief," he said.

"She already did half your job."

"More than half on some days."

We both laughed.

Then he grew serious.

"Captain, everybody's waiting for you to say the worst is over."

"I can't."

"I know. I don't want you to lie. I just want you to understand why they keep looking at you."

I stopped at the top of the ladder.

"Why?"

"Because you still look like somebody who believes there is a way home."

I leaned against the bulkhead. There had been hours when I believed nothing of the kind. I had simply kept making the next decision because refusing to decide was also a decision.

"How do I help them?" I asked.

"Keep telling the truth. Give them permission to rest when they can. And don't let the ship become a place where people apologize for grieving."

I nodded.

"You sound like Okafor."

"She's smarter than either of us."

That was difficult to argue.

We entered the medical space quietly. Ochoa remained under close observation. Price was sleeping. Rios had finally fallen asleep with his bandaged hands on top of a blanket.

I stood near the doorway and looked at him.

Nineteen years old.

A few days earlier, his greatest concern had been whether he could pass an engineering qualification. Now he would wake with memories that no amount of training could fully explain.

Okafor approached.

"Captain, he needs sleep."

"I won't wake him."

She looked at my face.

"You do too."

"Apparently that's become the ship's official position."

"It passed unanimously."

I smiled faintly.

She checked an instrument, then leaned against the counter.

"Do you know what he's been asking about?"

"Ramirez?"

"Yes. And Ochoa. He's convinced the next update will be his fault somehow."

I looked at the sleeping boy.

"Can you help him?"

"We can listen. We can tell him what happened as accurately as we know it. We can help him recover. But we can't erase what he saw."

I thought of the notebook in the mess.

"There's something Ramirez wrote about him. I want him to see it when he's ready."

Okafor nodded.

"That might help."

When I left, the passageway seemed longer than it had been before.

I returned to Combat. Bell was still listening. Idris was checking our limited defensive status. Farouk was reviewing communications integrity. Nobody had asked how tired I was. They already knew.

"Any change?" I asked.

"Contact intermittent," Bell said. "Still no confident classification."

I looked at her hands. She had a small photograph tucked beside her console: a dog standing on a beach with its ears turned backward in the wind.

"Your dog?"

"Yes, Captain. Her name's Admiral."

"Why Admiral?"

"Because she expects everyone to feed her and doesn't understand why we need to sleep."

I laughed.

"That sounds accurate."

Bell smiled for a second.

Then she tilted her head and listened again.

The room became quiet.

I could hear somebody adjusting a headset. A cooling fan. A distant footstep in a passageway.

At that hour the entire ship seemed to be holding its breath.

I remembered the first time I stood a midwatch as a young ensign. My commanding officer had told me the job was boring until it wasn't. I thought he was joking. Years later I understood he had been describing the psychology of watching for things you hope will never happen.

A ship's life depends on people who can concentrate when nothing changes.

At 2302, the contact weakened.

At 2307, it returned briefly on a slightly altered bearing.

Bell refused to pronounce it gone.

"Could be moving away," she said. "Could be the conditions. I need more evidence."

"Take the time you need."

I looked toward the bridge feed. Darkness. Spray. A thin line of foam where our bow cut the water.

Somewhere ahead, unseen by us, lay Iceland.

Somewhere behind, Caledonia rested on the seafloor.

And between those places moved the men and women of Northstar, holding the same watch they had held through fire and missiles and grief.

I went to the command position and sat down for the first time in what felt like a year.

Mara glanced at me.

"Anything wrong, Daniel?"

"No."

She looked suspicious.

"You're sitting."

"Apparently I'm experimenting with leadership."

"Promising development."

I smiled.

Then we both returned to the screens.

We continued watching through the last minutes of the hour. Bell made no grand declaration about the contact. She waited for the evidence to become strong enough to say what had happened. I understood that patience better than I had at the beginning of the war.
