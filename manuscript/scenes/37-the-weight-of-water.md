**THE WEIGHT OF WATER — 1326 HOURS**

Engineering reported the fire contained.

I had never heard a sentence so welcome or so incomplete.

Containment meant the ship could begin to assess its losses. It did not mean the danger had passed. Heat remained in spaces that should have been cool. Electrical power had been rerouted through unfamiliar paths. The port propulsion plant was unavailable, and the surviving machinery carried the responsibility for bringing the convoy home.

Most of all, Chief Ramirez was dead.

I went below with Mara after the engineering watch had given permission for a limited inspection.

The air tasted of soot, metal, and chemical residue. Portable lights cast hard shadows across the passageways. Sailors stood beside equipment whose labels had been scorched until they could scarcely be read. A temporary line carried power across a section of deck that looked almost ordinary, except for the dark marks climbing the bulkhead.

Navarro met us outside a protected space.

"Captain, you can look from here. I won't take either of you farther in until the safety checks are complete."

"That's fine."

He sounded as though he expected me to argue.

I had learned enough that day not to confuse proximity with leadership.

"How's the remaining plant?"

"Stable, within strict limits. We're watching temperatures and vibration. I can give you a safe operating range, not a promise of unlimited maneuvering."

"I'll take the range."

He handed Mara a written summary.

I studied the damage visible from the doorway. A section of cable tray hung at an angle where supports had failed. Insulation had been stripped from several surfaces by heat. A red inspection tag moved in the airflow.

"What do you need most?" I asked.

"Time. Replacement material. A dry dock, eventually. For tonight I need enough people who aren't exhausted to keep this ship within limits."

"What can we give you?"

"Rest rotations for the teams who aren't actively repairing something."

Mara made a note.

"Approved. As soon as your supervisors say a team can stand down."

Navarro nodded.

"Thank you."

I looked at him more closely. His face was gray from fatigue, and there was a fresh scrape above one eyebrow.

"What happened?"

"Hit a fitting during the casualty."

"Medical seen it?"

"Yes."

"How long have you been awake?"

He looked at Mara for rescue.

She folded her arms.

"That answer isn't going to help you."

"Long enough, Captain."

"Put another qualified engineer on the supervisory circuit for twenty minutes. Eat something. Then come back."

"I can't leave this."

"You have a trained department, Luis. They need you to make decisions, not collapse before the next one."

He considered arguing again, then nodded.

"Yes, sir."

He left us beside the scorched bulkhead.

Mara walked a few steps down the passageway, then stopped at a small brass plate bearing the name of an equipment compartment. Its letters were discolored by heat, but still legible.

"That's where Elena's team entered," she said.

"I know."

"You want to go inside."

"Yes."

"Would it change anything?"

I looked toward the door.

"No."

We stood there longer than we needed to.

I remembered Ramirez the week before departure, showing Rios the correct way to secure equipment during a drill. She had been impatient with shortcuts but generous with mistakes honestly admitted. She could make a sailor feel very small about a careless act and remarkably capable five minutes later.

A good chief builds confidence without making people think danger has disappeared.

Mara leaned against the cool side of the passage.

"We need to decide how to tell the crew."

"Cross is arranging the first announcements."

"Then what will you say?"

I did not have an answer.

I had commanded men and women under fire. I had made judgments about weapons that could travel farther than I could see. But I did not know how to tell a ship that one of its strongest people was gone because she had done exactly what she had taught others to do.

"That she died saving her shipmates," I said at last.

"True."

"That she was a good chief."

"Also true."

"That I wish I had found another way."

Mara looked at me.

"Careful."

"Why?"

"Because if you say that publicly, they may hear that you think the rescue teams failed."

"I don't."

"Then be precise. We can grieve without turning grief into a verdict."

I stared at the deck.

That was one of the things Mara had always done for me: protect the truth from the language of my own pain.

"You're right."

She nodded, not pleased, merely relieved.

A sailor approached from the repair locker and saluted. His eyes were bloodshot behind his protective glasses.

"Captain, may I speak?"

"Of course."

"I'm Petty Officer Calder. I was on the second rescue team."

I knew his name from the reports.

"How are you doing?"

He stared at his gloved hands.

"We got Price out, sir. Then Rios. Then Ochoa. Ramirez was right behind us, and—"

His voice stopped.

He tried again.

"I keep thinking I should've gone back faster."

I looked at him.

"What did your team leader direct?"

"We followed the route when it was cleared. We checked the safety conditions. We went back when the leader said it was possible."

"Did you follow the plan you were trained to follow?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then don't decide alone that your work was a failure. There will be a careful review. It will tell us what we could improve. Until then, you did your job under terrible conditions and brought three shipmates out."

He swallowed.

"But not her."

"No," I said. "Not her."

I did not add that the others were alive because she had remained.

He already knew.

"Take your rest period when your supervisor releases you," I said. "Talk to the people who were there. Don't try to make sense of it by yourself."

"Yes, Captain."

He left.

I watched him walk away.

A ship's damage can be measured in failed components and twisted steel. The other damage is harder. It appears in the way a petty officer hesitates before walking through a passage. In a young sailor who has trouble sleeping. In an experienced engineer who keeps returning to the same diagram because the last time he looked at it, someone was still alive.

Navarro returned after a few minutes with half a sandwich in his hand. I pretended not to notice that he had obeyed my order only partially.

"Captain," he said, "I have a recommendation."

"Go ahead."

"We should make a permanent record of the damage-control teams' actions while memories are fresh. Not just an engineering log. We need witness accounts, times, obstacles, and what each team believed it knew."

"Agreed."

"And I want Ramirez's people involved in reviewing it when they're ready. She trained them. They have the experience to tell us what the diagrams don't show."

"Make sure none of them feel required to relive the worst minutes before they can manage it."

He nodded.

"We'll do it right."

Outside the damaged compartment a small pool of water had collected along the deck. It came from the firefighting effort, not from the sea, and it moved slightly as Northstar rolled. A sailor stepped over it and continued toward the ladder.

I watched the water shift.

That was the strange thing about the fire. It had happened inside a ship surrounded by water. We had fought the flames with tools and discipline and more water than I could imagine. But once the danger was under control, the water itself had become another thing to manage—something to remove, to measure, to keep from reaching the wrong equipment.

Every solution made another duty.

I thanked Navarro and turned toward the ladder.

Mara followed.

At the top of the passage I stopped and looked back.

The compartment door stood closed. A warning tag moved in the air. Somewhere beyond it, sailors were beginning the slow work of making damaged machinery safe.

I imagined Ramirez standing beside her clipboard, asking whether our people had checked the boundaries and counted the team members.

We had.

We would keep doing so.

I put a hand against the bulkhead.

"All right," I whispered.

I wasn't speaking to the machinery.

I wasn't speaking to Mara.

I was speaking to the ship and to the woman who would never walk these passageways again.

Then I went back to Combat.

The convoy was still moving west.
