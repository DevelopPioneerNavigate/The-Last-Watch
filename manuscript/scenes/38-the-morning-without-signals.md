**THE MORNING WITHOUT SIGNALS — 19 NOVEMBER, 0610 HOURS**

The war did not announce a new day.

At 0610, the North Atlantic remained a dark mass of heaving water, streaked with rain. The light over the horizon was barely enough to distinguish sky from sea. From Northstar's bridge, Aurora looked like an exhausted animal struggling through the waves.

I had been asleep for ninety minutes when Mara sent for me.

She had not sent for me because of an attack.

She had sent for me because the communications watch could no longer verify an expected update from the regional command, and a weather report suggested our planned transit corridor might become less safe than we'd estimated.

I arrived in Combat with my uniform jacket half-fastened and a taste of stale coffee in my mouth.

"Tell me what we know," I said.

Farouk stood beside the communications plot.

"We've lost reliable confirmation of the latest regional shipping advisory. Two circuits are delayed, one has intermittent reception, and the last message we received is now outside the time window we'd normally consider current."

"Does that mean hostile interference?"

"No confirmed evidence of that."

"Good."

It was not good, of course. It was simply honest.

Bishop had come down from the bridge with a chart and the latest local observations. He spread them across a console and pointed toward the corridor ahead.

"We have a narrower weather margin than yesterday's forecast suggested. The sea is trending worse along the southern part of the route. The alternatives are longer and may take us closer to uncertain hazards."

"Merchant limitations?"

"Meridian's master is asking that we avoid abrupt changes. Atlas Mariner is holding steady. Aurora is reporting more passenger injuries from the motion."

Mara watched me.

"We could continue on the existing track for a while and gather more information," she said.

"Risk?"

"Conditions could deteriorate before we have the better picture."

"And the alternate?"

"Not automatically safer. It trades one uncertainty for another."

I rubbed my eyes.

The ocean was not presenting us with a correct answer. It was asking us to choose a risk we could manage.

"Get the merchant masters on a conference circuit," I said.

Lindholm joined first, sounding hoarse. He had not slept much more than I had. Captain Reed from Meridian spoke next. Then Captain Solberg of Atlas Mariner. Cutlass's executive officer, Shaw, came onto the line to represent the British ship.

I explained the assessment plainly.

"We have deteriorating weather information and a delayed regional advisory. We're comparing routes. I want each of you to tell me what your ship can safely do, and what limits you cannot exceed."

Reed began.

"My tanker is manageable at the present heading, but the rolling will become harder if we turn farther into the swell. I can reduce speed. I would rather not make sudden course changes until I know what sea I'm turning into."

Solberg agreed that the worst rolling was affecting his crew's ability to move safely. He was monitoring loose equipment and had kept people away from exposed areas.

Lindholm said Aurora could continue but that the passengers were frightened by every new change of motion.

Shaw gave a brief account of Cutlass's damaged capabilities and the limited maneuvers she could tolerate.

I listened and let each captain finish.

They did not need me to explain their ships to them. They needed an escort commander willing to treat their judgment as part of the operation.

When the conference ended, Mara said, "You could have decided without asking."

"Yes."

"Would it have been faster?"

"Maybe."

"Would it have been better?"

"Probably not."

She smiled slightly.

The final choice was not dramatic. We maintained the current safe leg while the navigators refined a weather-limited alternate and made sure every captain knew the conditions that would trigger a change. We kept our options open instead of committing early to a route that looked elegant on a map.

The danger remained.

But nobody had been ignored.

At 0650, Bishop returned to the bridge. Farouk stayed in Combat to work with Alvarez on the missing advisory. Their teams had a list of verified sources and a clear requirement not to let uncorrelated reports become an invented enemy picture.

I stood by the plotting table and watched the icons move slowly west.

Three merchant ships.

One wounded British frigate.

One wounded American destroyer.

That was the world we had left to defend.

"Captain," Idris said, "can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"When do we stop thinking of the convoy as a combat problem and start thinking of it as a navigation problem?"

I considered that.

"When we're tied up," I said.

He nodded.

"That's what I thought."

"Doesn't mean we can't take the defensive burden down when the evidence supports it. It means we don't stop being responsible because we're tired of the job."

"I understand."

He looked at his screens again.

I knew he was exhausted. Everybody was. The difference between discipline and stubbornness could become dangerously small after two days without proper rest.

I asked him to turn the watch over to the next qualified officer when the relief was ready.

He began to object.

"That's an order, Hassan."

He nodded.

"Yes, Captain."

"Eat something. Sleep. Come back able to think."

For a moment he seemed younger than his rank.

"Understood."

When he left, the room felt different. Not because its systems had changed, but because it was no longer being held together by one man refusing to admit he needed rest.

I tried to remember when I'd last had a conversation with Hassan that wasn't about weapons.

There had been one aboard ship before deployment. He had told me his father ran a small grocery store and still thought naval officers spent most of their days polishing brass. The old man had been proud of his son but baffled by what he did at sea.

I hoped he would never understand it too well.

At 0714 the first promising update arrived from a verified friendly aircraft operating farther west. Its crew had encountered better visibility along part of the route and passed observations that helped Bishop refine the hazard assessment.

It was not a rescue.

It was not an assurance.

It was one more piece of good information.

I thanked the aircrew through the available circuit.

The young communications operator beside Alvarez looked at me.

"Captain, does this mean we're almost clear?"

I looked at the plot.

"We're closer than we were yesterday."

He nodded, looking almost disappointed.

"That's all?"

"That's a great deal."

The ship pitched heavily and settled again.

On the bridge camera, Aurora emerged from a wall of spray, her bow lifting into the swell. A few lights were still visible behind the windows. Somewhere aboard her, children were probably sleeping on the floor of a lounge. Some might be dreaming of their old homes.

I thought of Liv and the red crayon.

I wanted to promise her a harbor by evening.

I didn't know enough to do that.

Instead I asked the watch officer for the latest weather and the safest possible course.

That would have to be my promise for another hour.

At 0730, Alvarez reported the delayed regional advisory had been restored through a verified channel. Its content confirmed broad warnings we already knew, added one uncertainty about distant hostile activity, and offered nothing that demanded an immediate change in our maneuver.

"Any reason to alter the current plan?" I asked Bishop.

"Not yet, Captain."

"Then maintain."

The room settled into work.

I looked toward Mara.

"Did we accomplish anything this morning?"

She considered.

"We didn't make a bad situation worse."

I smiled.

"That's not the kind of victory they put in books."

"Perhaps they should."

Outside, dawn began to find the edges of the sea.

The clouds remained low.

The ship kept moving.

And after a night in which almost everything had seemed uncertain, our people had managed one more small, essential thing: they had refused to pretend that the missing information didn't matter.

That was enough to carry us into the next watch.
