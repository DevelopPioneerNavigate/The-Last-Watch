**THE FIRST CROSSING — THREE DAYS BEFORE THE ATTACK**

The convoy did not leave Norway as a formation. It left as an argument.

At dawn, with low cloud clinging to the cranes, Aurora cast off first because Lindholm insisted he needed the room to swing clear of the pier. Meridian's master wanted the tanker to wait until the tug traffic thinned. Atlas Mariner had a steering-system alarm that was probably nothing but could not be treated as nothing. Caledonia was still reconciling a container count with the shore authorities.

And there was a war going on.

From Northstar's bridge, the harbor looked like a box of moving pieces whose owners had stopped speaking the same language.

"Captain, Atlas Mariner asks to delay fifteen minutes," Mercer reported.

"Reason?"

"Intermittent steering indication."

"Tell her master we want the system checked before departure. We will adjust."

"That pushes Caledonia's departure."

"Then it pushes her departure."

Mercer picked up the telephone. Behind her, the navigator leaned over the chart table and amended the sequence of movements.

Idris called from Combat. "Sir, intelligence is asking when we'll clear coastal waters."

"When all our ships can sail safely."

"That's not the time they wanted."

"It's the time they've got."

I had been given an operational schedule, but the sea does not read schedules and machinery has no respect for admirals. A convoy of merchants is not a squadron of warships. You do not make four very different vessels behave as one through the force of your voice.

You do it by earning their captains' confidence, then refusing to abandon their limitations when danger makes those limitations inconvenient.

By 0710, Atlas Mariner's crew had isolated a faulty indication and verified that the steering gear was responding correctly. Aurora was outside the inner harbor. Meridian moved slowly toward the breakwater with her tug. Caledonia followed at a distance, the last of her medical cargo finally secured.

I stood on the port bridge wing and watched people waving from the pier.

There were perhaps twenty of them. Not a crowd large enough to fill a stadium or make a headline. Just a few soldiers, dockworkers, and relatives who had made it through the night to see the ships depart.

One woman raised a cardboard sign. Through binoculars I could just read two words written in thick black letters.

*COME BACK.*

The wind almost tore it from her hands.

I lowered the binoculars.

"Captain?" Mercer said from the doorway. "Convoy departure sequence is complete."

"Very well. Let's take them to sea."

The breakwater passed slowly along our starboard side. Beyond it, the ocean opened as gray and restless as a sleeping animal. Northstar's bow rose into the first swell, and I felt the shift from harbor motion to the long pulse of deep water.

We were on our own.

Not completely, of course. The naval network reached toward ships and aircraft beyond the horizon. Commanders ashore knew where we were supposed to be. Intelligence analysts were comparing reports from sources I would never see. But the nearest reliable assistance was already hours away, and the distance would grow with every mile.

In the evening we held the first convoy captains' conference by radio.

Lindholm spoke first. Aurora was rolling more than expected, and several passengers had become ill. Ward reported that Caledonia had handled the first swells well but that her engineering department had found a vibration they wanted to monitor. Reed aboard Meridian asked for better advance warning of larger course changes.

The freighter's master, Captain Henrik Solberg of Atlas Mariner, was a man of few words. He listened through most of the call, then said, "Are we being hunted?"

There was no respectful way to pretend I hadn't heard.

"We have no confirmed hostile contact," I answered.

"That's not my question."

"No. But it's the answer I can defend."

He waited.

"The wider area is contested," I continued. "We are using the best available information to minimize exposure. Cutlass and Northstar will remain with the convoy."

Ward spoke.

"Daniel, I'd prefer you told us when you're worried. We know you won't be able to tell us everything."

I looked at Mara, who stood beside the bridge console listening.

"Then let me be clear," I said. "I am worried. I'm not asking any of you to pretend otherwise. I'm asking you to keep your ships safe and follow coordinated instructions when the threat changes."

For a moment there was only static.

Then Lindholm said, "That I can work with."

The conference ended.

Mara folded her arms.

"Good speech."

"Not intended as one."

"Those are your better ones."

She studied the convoy on the navigation display.

"We still have an opportunity to turn back."

"Only until we don't."

"Precisely."

I looked at her. "Do you think we should?"

She thought for several seconds.

"I think Caledonia's cargo matters. I think Aurora's people need a harbor where the lights stay on. I think the tanker and freighter are carrying supplies that cannot wait for an ideal moment. And I think we are the escort assigned because there isn't a better one close enough."

"That sounds like an argument to continue."

"It is. But don't mistake an argument for certainty."

That was Mara's gift. She never let me use conviction as a substitute for thinking.

The next morning, the wind eased briefly and the merchants began to settle into a routine. The escort ships took turns repositioning within the protective arrangement. We updated the weather picture and checked convoy communications. I received reports about fresh-water consumption, minor mechanical problems, and a dispute aboard Meridian over overtime pay.

The first two were our business. The third was not, although I admired the sailor willing to argue about wages while crossing a war zone.

At lunch I ate with the junior officers.

They were trying to speak normally. I could hear it in their voices.

Bishop had brought a chart showing the expected sea conditions. Mercer was complaining that the bridge coffee tasted like burnt cable insulation. Lieutenant Farouk asked whether anyone knew why an old television program playing in the wardroom was stuck on the same episode. The laughter that followed was genuine, and I held onto it.

When I was a junior officer, I believed senior leaders occupied a different emotional world. A captain seemed insulated from ordinary uncertainty by rank and experience. I imagined my own commanding officer went to sleep knowing exactly what tomorrow would bring.

Now I understood he had probably spent those nights listening to a ship settle around him, trying not to think about the weight of every sailor inside it.

After lunch, Cross joined me outside the wardroom.

"Captain, the crew's nervous."

"I'd be more concerned if they weren't."

"They need to see that nervousness isn't the same as being unready."

"What would help?"

"Something ordinary. Inspections without theater. Training without announcing that we're about to die. Make it clear they can keep being sailors instead of acting like characters in a war film."

I considered that.

"Schedule the damage-control drills we were already due to conduct. Keep them controlled. I don't want exhausted people hurt because somebody tried to make a point."

"Yes, sir."

"And Cross?"

He stopped.

"Talk to the younger ones. Don't tell them the danger isn't real."

"I wouldn't insult them."

Two days before the attack we ran a controlled fire-response exercise in an equipment compartment that had never burned and probably never would. Ramirez supervised. She moved from team to team with her clipboard and her severe expression, asking questions in a voice loud enough to overcome the ventilation.

I watched her correct a sailor's positioning without humiliating him.

"You're not here to make me happy," she said. "You're here to make sure the person beside you comes home."

The sailor adjusted his stance.

"Better?"

"Much."

She stepped back and let him repeat the task.

At the end of the exercise, Rios was frustrated.

"I knew that answer, Chief. I just froze."

"Then you learned what happens when your brain meets pressure," Ramirez said.

"What if I freeze when it matters?"

"You won't know until the day comes. That's why we practice now. And if you do freeze, your shipmate will help you move. Then someday you'll do the same for somebody else."

I stood close enough to hear.

Rios noticed me and straightened.

"Captain."

"Carry on."

He looked embarrassed.

I said, "Chief Ramirez has a point. Nobody aboard this ship succeeds alone."

His shoulders eased a little.

When I left the compartment, I told myself the training had done what it was supposed to do. I had no idea how precisely those words would return to us in the fire.

That evening I stood on the bridge after sunset. Northstar moved through a long, steady swell under a sky the color of wet slate. Cutlass was a dark shape on the horizon. Behind us, Aurora's lights glowed against the gathering night.

Mercer offered me coffee.

I accepted it.

"Sir, does it always feel this quiet before something happens?"

I looked out over the ocean.

"Sometimes nothing happens."

"Do you believe that will be our story?"

I wanted to tell her yes.

Instead, I sipped the coffee and listened to the wind.

"I hope so, Lieutenant."

For the first time in years, I found myself wishing for an uneventful deployment with all the desperation of a prayer.
