**THE LAST MANIFEST — 0548 HOURS**

After Caledonia disappeared, the sea looked unchanged.

That is something I have never forgiven it for.

The freighter had gone down with all the finality a ship can possess. Her cargo of generators and medicines was scattered or lost. People who had expected to wake at the end of their watches would never wake again. And the ocean resumed the same gray motion it had made before any of us arrived.

Cutlass's searchlights swept the water.

Mallory's crew had recovered nineteen survivors, but the count was still being verified. Some needed urgent medical treatment. Four remained unaccounted for. The reports arrived as partial phrases, names repeated over radios, and a sequence of questions that had no satisfactory answers.

I asked communications to record the known manifest status beside the last confirmed position.

"Captain," Alvarez said, "the port authority's loading list includes some cargo additions that were never reflected in the summary."

"Can we reconcile it?"

"We're trying. The manifests were transmitted through a damaged network."

"Don't rush. We need an accurate record."

He nodded.

The work sounded clerical. It was anything but.

Those manifests represented operating rooms that would remain without generators, medicines that could not reach injured people, and a chain of promises broken beneath the sea. They also listed crew positions that would someday become names in official notifications.

A few minutes later Alvarez brought me a personal message received from Cutlass's medical team. One of Caledonia's rescued sailors had asked whether he could send an account to his family before he received treatment. He wanted them to know he had helped another man into a life raft. The medical team had recorded the message but would not transmit it until the correct notification channels allowed.

I read it once.

It described a man whose face the sailor could barely remember, a cold hand gripping his wrist, and a promise that someone would come back.

The message ended with a single sentence.

*I didn't leave him on purpose.*

I stared at it until the words became difficult to see.

Mara was watching.

"What's wrong?"

I handed her the report.

She read it silently, then returned it.

"Every survivor is going to wonder about somebody they couldn't save."

"I know."

"We can't answer those questions for them."

"No."

She leaned against the console.

"You can't answer them for yourself either."

I looked away.

On the display, Cutlass remained near the sinking position, still searching as the weather allowed. Every few minutes a rescue boat moved through the same patch of sea where a human life might be present or might already have slipped beneath the waves.

Our remaining ships were farther west, moving slowly under our protection.

"Captain," Idris said, "the convoy masters are requesting an updated route."

"What are they asking?"

"Whether we're diverting to avoid the sinking area."

"We should. Keep the maneuver within safe limits and avoid disturbing the rescue operations."

He nodded.

I looked at the positions of Aurora, Atlas Mariner and Meridian. Three ships. Three symbols. I felt an irrational need to count them repeatedly, as though they might vanish if I stopped.

I contacted Lindholm.

"Peter, we're adjusting the track. How are your passengers?"

"Shaken. Some saw Caledonia go down."

"Any injuries?"

"A few people fell when we changed course. Nothing serious. A doctor is tending them."

He hesitated.

"I need to tell you about a boy."

I waited.

"He keeps asking whether the cargo ship's sailors had mothers."

My throat tightened.

"What did you say?"

"I told him they did. He asked whether somebody was going to tell them what happened."

I stared at my hands.

"Tell him yes. Tell him people are working on it."

"Thank you."

"Peter, if any of the children need something that can be provided safely, let us know."

A pause.

"We need to reach Iceland, Daniel."

I looked out at the rain-streaked video from the bridge.

"I know."

When I finished the call, I found that the communications team had received a fragment of a message from the Norwegian port authority. A medical director ashore was asking whether the generators aboard Caledonia would still arrive. The hospital had started rationing power to certain parts of the building.

I drafted the reply myself.

*Caledonia lost at sea. Cargo not recoverable. Alternate supply route urgently required.*

I deleted the phrase *not recoverable*.

It sounded too much like the human beings who had gone down with her.

I rewrote the message:

*Caledonia was sunk during the crossing. Her cargo will not reach you. We request immediate priority for replacement generators and medical supplies via the next safe route.*

Then I added:

*Her crew attempted the mission despite severe danger. Please remember them.*

I sent it.

There was nothing more I could do for the lost cargo.

But I could tell the truth about the people who had tried to deliver it.

Cross arrived in Combat with a report from the mess decks.

"Captain, a few of our sailors are having trouble concentrating. They watched the sinking on the internal cameras."

"I want them checked and rotated where possible."

"Already started. One asked me how many people died."

"What did you tell him?"

"The confirmed number. And that we don't know about the missing."

"Good."

Cross remained beside me.

"He also asked whether the Navy was going to win."

I looked at him.

"And?"

"I told him winning is a question above my pay grade. Getting the next watch right isn't."

I laughed, a sound so unexpected that Idris glanced over.

"That's good advice, Master Chief."

"Cost me twenty-nine years to learn it."

The ship rolled hard. Someone caught the edge of a console. The motion felt rougher after the stillness of my thoughts.

I asked Idris for another look at the weather and air picture. The distant hostile aircraft remained unidentified, and we were not yet certain which contacts would become threats.

The hours ahead held a brutality we could not predict.

But at 0548, my responsibility remained painfully simple: record the dead accurately, defend the living, and never confuse the two jobs.

In the corner of a tactical display, Caledonia's sinking position remained a small symbol.

I told the watch team not to remove it.

Not yet.

I needed to see where our promise had broken.
