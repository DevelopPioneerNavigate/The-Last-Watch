**THE PEOPLE WHO WERE NOT ON THE SCREEN — 1257 HOURS**

When I left medical, I found the passageway outside filled with the things combat leaves behind: a helmet with its chin strap broken, an empty stretcher, a pair of wet gloves, somebody's coffee cup resting upright against the deck because the ship had finally stopped rolling quite so violently.

I bent to pick up the gloves. They were labeled with Chief Ramirez's name.

For a moment, I could not move.

A voice spoke behind me.

"Captain?"

It was Leah Okafor. Her sleeves were rolled up and her face had the exhausted concentration I had come to associate with every truly good corpsman.

"Those are hers," she said.

"I know."

"Her team brought them out. Nobody wanted to decide what to do with them."

I handed them to her.

"Make sure they go with her effects. Not in a storage box where they'll disappear."

"Yes, sir."

She waited. "You should eat something."

"I will."

"That's what everybody says when they aren't going to."

I glanced at her.

"Leah, you have a ship full of patients. You don't have time to worry about me."

"You're one of the patients, in the broadest possible sense."

I smiled despite myself.

"That's a dangerous definition."

"It's a medically useful one."

Then she grew serious.

"Captain, when we reach port, some of the crew will be surprised by how bad they feel. They did their jobs while the danger was immediate. When it stops, the body doesn't always stop with it."

"How do I help?"

"Make room for them to talk. Make room for them not to. Keep people from pretending a good sailor never needs help."

I looked toward the treatment room.

"And you?"

She hesitated.

"Ask me again after we've got everyone ashore."

That was as honest as any answer I had heard all day.

In the mess decks the cooks had managed to produce soup, bread, and a tray of sandwiches despite the engineering casualty. I stood watching two sailors carry food toward the watch stations.

The senior cook, Chief Petty Officer Arturo Mendoza, had a burn on his forearm covered by a fresh dressing.

"Chief, what happened?"

"Pan slipped during that turn, sir."

"Have medical take another look."

"Already did."

He was stirring a pot. The galley smelled like onions and pepper and something richer than our situation deserved.

"How long have you been cooking?"

"Since before the first missile attack. The watch teams needed something hot."

"Did you sleep?"

"Not yet."

"Then put somebody else on the pot."

He shook his head.

"My cooks are just as tired. We'll rotate when everyone gets fed."

"That's an order, Chief. Rotate."

He sighed. "Aye, Captain."

As I turned away he added, "Sir, I set aside a sandwich for you."

"How'd you know I was coming?"

"Your executive officer has been calling every thirty minutes asking whether you ate."

I looked down at the sandwich.

Mara was going to outlive every one of us through sheer stubbornness.

At the next table, several sailors were writing down small details they remembered from the fire. Not official reports yet; notes for themselves while memories were fresh. One drew a rough passageway and labeled the places where people had been standing. Another wrote the sequence of voices he had heard on the radio.

I paused beside them.

"Doing the damage-control reconstruction?"

"A rough version, sir," one answered. "We don't want to forget anything."

"Good. Preserve it for the investigation. And don't turn it into a game of finding somebody to blame before we have evidence."

"No, sir."

They looked relieved.

There was a sudden burst of laughter at the far end of the room. I looked over, startled by how normal it sounded. Two sailors were arguing about whether the cook's soup had more pepper than water. An older petty officer told them to be grateful it had either.

Life insisted on continuing aboard Northstar. I had spent the morning thinking of the ship as a fighting machine. In the mess decks I saw it was also a home, patched together from hundreds of people who could not escape one another even when they wanted to.

Near the doorway, a young signalman was reading a folded letter. He looked up as I passed and quickly tried to hide the paper.

"You don't need to put it away for me."

"It's from my wife, Captain."

"Good news?"

"She says our daughter walked for the first time. She recorded it."

I knew that look. Pride folded around grief.

"You're going to see it when you get home."

"Yes, sir. It's just..." He looked at the letter again. "She didn't wait for me."

Children never do.

I thought of Ethan at sixteen, and the thousands of ordinary moments I had missed while other men and women carried out the same duty ashore.

"No, she didn't," I told him. "But she'll walk toward you. I promise you that much."

He smiled and nodded.

I went back to Combat with half a sandwich in my hand. The tactical display still showed an enemy force beyond the horizon. The war had not changed. But I had been reminded what we were protecting aboard our own ship: soup, letters, arguments about pepper, daughters learning to walk, people brave enough to talk about fear.

None of those things appeared on Idris's screen.

All of them mattered.
