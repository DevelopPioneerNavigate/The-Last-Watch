**WHEN CUTLASS WENT DARK — 0946 HOURS**

The British frigate's silence lasted less than ten minutes. I have lived with those minutes much longer than I should.

In the first seconds after the impact, I tried to reach Mallory by every available established channel. We had known each other for years. During one exercise near Scotland he had tried to convince me that American destroyer captains feared two things: bad weather and British tea. I told him he had the order wrong.

Now his ship had been hit, and he wasn't answering.

"Captain," Idris said, "we have to assume Cutlass's local picture is impaired."

"I know."

"What can we do for her without leaving our defensive station?"

"Pass our confirmed tracks. Keep all rescue options open. Don't let her lack of communication become a second attack."

I heard my voice give the orders. It sounded practiced and unemotional. Inside, I was imagining the impact. A flash at the forward superstructure. Fire racing through a passageway. Sailors thrown into ladders. Someone reaching for a radio that no longer worked.

The little voice that eventually came through belonged to Lieutenant Thomas Armitage. He sounded painfully young.

"Sir, we're fighting a serious fire. The captain is injured."

I asked whether they had an executive officer still available.

"Yes, sir. She's directing from central control."

"Good. Stay inside your chain. Tell her we'll pass information as our systems permit. Your first priorities are the ship's safety and personnel."

He acknowledged, then hesitated.

"Captain Vale, some of Caledonia's survivors are aboard us. We have patients in the sick bay."

That changed the picture again. The ship we had relied upon for rescue was now itself a casualty, carrying wounded people from an earlier disaster. Every ship, I was beginning to learn, could become both rescuer and victim within the same hour.

"Are they safe?"

"They're being moved where necessary, sir."

"Tell the medical staff we can accept casualties at the first safe opportunity. Don't attempt a hazardous transfer just because we're offering."

"Understood."

The transmission faded into a low hiss.

I turned to Cross. "Have our rescue and medical teams assess what can be prepared without taking us out of the battle."

"Already moving, Captain."

Of course they were. The whole ship seemed to be anticipating needs before I could give them names.

An internal medical report arrived. Leah Okafor was preparing supplies for additional casualties. She had asked for volunteers to move equipment and set up overflow treatment space. Every qualified sailor who could be spared had offered to help.

I called her.

"Leah, what do you need?"

"The same thing every corpsman needs, Captain."

"Which is?"

"Enough time and enough hands."

"We'll give you what we can."

Her voice softened. "I know you will."

There was another update from Cutlass. Their propulsion remained available. Several compartments had lost power. Their executive officer had assumed tactical responsibility while Mallory was treated.

I wondered whether he was conscious. Whether he could hear the alarms. Whether he was telling someone not to waste time worrying about him.

The thought made me angry in a way that had no useful target.

"Captain, fresh hostile-surface assessment," Idris said.

I returned to the main display.

Beyond our horizon the enemy group was still maneuvering, difficult to track in the weather. They had just hit one escort badly enough to remove much of her defensive contribution. If they attacked again immediately, Northstar would have to face a burden the two escorts had previously shared.

I looked at the remaining civilian vessels and at our own ammunition state.

Mara approached.

"If Cutlass loses propulsion, she'll be in real trouble."

"Yes."

"And if we turn to her, Aurora will be exposed."

"Yes."

"You know that's going to be the next decision."

"I do."

She waited while I studied the chart. I wanted to find a piece of geometry that would let us be everywhere at once.

"Maybe we won't have to make it," I said.

"Maybe."

We both knew that hope was not a plan.

Several minutes later Armitage returned.

"Captain Vale. Cutlass. We're still maneuvering. The fires are isolated. Captain Mallory is alive."

For the first time since the impact, I could breathe normally.

"Thank you, Lieutenant."

"Sir, our captain wanted you to know something."

"What?"

"He said to tell you not to come over here and make a nuisance of yourself."

I laughed before I could stop myself.

"That sounds like Edward."

"Yes, sir."

"Tell him he owes me a cup of terrible tea."

The young lieutenant laughed too, once, despite the alarms behind him.

Then he went back to saving his ship.

Later, in the official record, these minutes would become a series of contact reports and casualty updates. Nobody reading them would know what it meant to hear an injured friend make a joke through someone else's radio.

I had not yet learned whether we would survive the day.

But for a moment I was grateful that Edward Mallory still possessed enough of himself to annoy me.
