**THE LIEUTENANT'S BRIDGE — 0958 HOURS**

I knew Cutlass was alive because somebody aboard her was still arguing about the wording of a report.

"Northstar, Cutlass. Be advised our forward fire is isolated."

The voice belonged to Lieutenant Armitage, who had been trying to make himself heard over damaged equipment for the better part of ten minutes.

"Understood," I said. "Confirm isolated, not extinguished."

A pause.

"Correct, sir. Isolated. Still being fought."

"Thank you."

He was young. Younger than the officer who had spoken to me at the exercise the previous spring. Or perhaps his voice sounded younger because he was holding a bridge together while the men who ordinarily stood over him were somewhere below it, injured or occupied with the damage.

I could picture the scene too clearly: a dim compartment with one screen dark, somebody trying to wipe blood from a console, a sailor checking a telephone that no longer worked, and a junior officer looking at the captain's empty position as though it were a cliff.

"Lieutenant, who has the watch?" I asked.

"Commander Shaw has the ship, sir. She's coordinating repair and casualty reports. I'm at the bridge while we restore the primary circuit."

"Good. Tell her we have your position and will keep you informed of confirmed threats."

"Thank you, Captain."

Before he released the circuit, he added, almost too quietly for me to hear, "Sir, I don't know if I'm doing this correctly."

I looked at Idris.

He had heard.

"Armitage, are you keeping your people clear about what you know and what you need?"

"Yes, sir."

"Are you following your executive officer's direction?"

"Yes."

"Are you asking for help when you need it?"

There was a longer pause.

"I'm trying."

"Then keep trying. That's command for the next five minutes."

He let out a breath.

"Aye, sir."

The radio clicked off.

Idris returned to the display. I knew he wanted to say something. Eventually he did.

"Do you remember our first casualty-control exercise after you took command?"

"Unfortunately."

"You stopped the entire drill because a lieutenant announced a compartment clear without checking."

"Yes."

"He was furious with you."

"He was wrong."

"He knows that now."

I looked at him.

"What's your point?"

"Armitage just corrected his own report in front of everyone. He's learning the right lesson at a terrible time."

I nodded.

At 1001 a second transmission came from Cutlass, this one through a functioning alternate circuit. Commander Eleanor Shaw had taken command of the ship's immediate recovery. Her voice was controlled but thin with exhaustion.

"Northstar, Cutlass. We can maintain steerage. Captain Mallory is receiving treatment. We have wounded from the earlier rescue as well as our own."

I stared at the casualty notation on the display.

"Your surviving Caledonia sailors?"

"Being accounted for. Some were moved during the strike. We're checking everyone against a handwritten roster."

"Do you have enough medical staff?"

"Enough to keep working. No more than that."

She paused.

"Daniel, I need to ask you something difficult."

"Go ahead."

"Can we keep moving west while we finish the casualty accounting? If we remain in the present area, I may have to choose between getting accurate names and keeping the ship safe."

I thought about the people waiting aboard Aurora and Meridian. I thought about a damaged frigate moving through hostile water, carrying the survivors of another vessel.

"You should continue safe movement within your damaged capability. We can reconcile the names as information allows. Nobody will treat an uncertain casualty count as permission to invent one."

"Thank you."

"How is Edward?"

"Still arguing with the doctor. I regard that as encouraging."

I almost laughed.

"I do too."

Then a muffled alarm sounded behind Shaw's voice.

She turned away from the radio.

"Repeat that."

I couldn't hear the response.

She came back.

"We have a possible water-ingress indication in an affected space. Engineering is checking whether it is new or a consequence of the existing damage."

"Do you need assistance?"

"Not yet. We need a confirmed assessment, and I won't have the crew chasing a phantom fault while the actual fire is still being managed."

"Understood."

She disconnected.

The next four minutes were worse than the attack in a peculiar way. The missiles had forced decisions. Now we could only listen for the ship's next report.

I went to the bridge display.

Cutlass was moving slowly through rough water, smoke still staining part of her upper structure. Behind her was the place where she'd saved the sailors from Caledonia. Ahead of her were three civilian ships that were depending on us both.

Mercer stood beside the chart table.

"Sir, do you think they'll make it?"

"Yes," I said.

It was too quick an answer, and we both knew it.

I corrected myself.

"I think they've got a good crew, and they're still making progress."

She accepted that.

For the first time since the strike, I wondered about the person at the other end of the radio who might be making the worst mistake of his life without realizing it. Armitage was on a bridge that had changed beneath him. Shaw was carrying decisions normally shared with a captain. A dozen frightened sailors were performing jobs whose last practice exercise had ended with somebody laughing.

"Alvarez," I called, "do we have any unnecessary traffic directed to Cutlass?"

He checked.

"Some repeat requests for damage status from different commands."

"Consolidate through the approved channels. Their people need to repair a ship, not answer every version of the same question."

"Aye."

I looked toward Farouk.

"Are we forwarding unverified reports about Cutlass?"

"No. We're separating confirmed status from our assumptions."

"Keep doing it."

At 1006 Shaw called again.

"Northstar. The water-ingress indication appears to reflect known damage and residual water in an affected compartment. No new flooding confirmed. We remain under control."

I let out a breath.

"Good."

"And Armitage wants to know whether he gave you a satisfactory report."

I smiled.

"Tell him he told me the truth. That's more than satisfactory."

She laughed once.

"I will."

The room around me returned to its work. The attack hadn't ended our mission. It had broken some of the equipment and some of the people carrying it out, then asked them to continue.

I thought about the young lieutenant on the British bridge. Someday an investigation might decide which procedures had succeeded and which needed changing. None of those documents would show what it meant to be twenty-four, standing where your captain should be, knowing the next sentence you spoke might change the lives of a hundred sailors.

I knew because once I had been twenty-four.

I had never stood on a damaged British frigate. But I had stood on a bridge and wished someone older would come through the door and tell me what to do.

Armitage had nobody coming through his.

Instead, he had a crew who needed him to be the officer he had trained to become.

I looked at the symbol of Cutlass continuing west.

The British ship's lights did not appear on our main tactical screen. Her courage was represented by a shape and a heading.

But I knew now that somewhere inside that shape, a young lieutenant had learned how to make a report more accurate while his hands were shaking.

That, too, was a kind of victory.
