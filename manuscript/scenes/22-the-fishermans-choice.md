**THE FISHERMAN'S CHOICE — 0801 HOURS**

For seventeen minutes after the Sigrid volunteered to help Kestrel, I kept expecting Anders Nygaard to change his mind.

He was a civilian. He had no armor, no defensive missiles, and no navy issuing orders. He had a working boat, a handful of fishermen, and the same North Atlantic that was punishing us. His willingness to go toward the damaged frigate seemed almost unreasonable.

I listened to the radio while Idris watched the wider surface picture.

"Northstar, Sigrid," Nygaard called. "We have received the last known position."

"Understood. Conditions in that area remain dangerous."

"Conditions everywhere are dangerous, Commander."

His tone was factual, not defiant.

"Have you informed your crew of the risk?"

"They informed me first."

That stopped me.

"What did they say?"

"My deck boss said we should go because there are people there. My youngest sailor asked how long a man can live in this water. I told him we should spend less time guessing and more time working."

I glanced at Mara. Her expression was unreadable.

"Captain Nygaard, your judgment of your vessel's safety takes priority. If the circumstances become unsafe, I expect you to withdraw."

"Yes. But I will make that decision from the place where I can see."

The radio clicked off.

For the next few minutes, I stared at the dark area of ocean where the enemy frigate was believed to be drifting.

We knew almost nothing about Kestrel beyond her reported damage and the fact that she had been operating with hostile forces. She might have carried weapons that had been used against another Allied ship. Her sailors might have played a part in the attacks against our shipping. Every assumption I made about her was an invitation to turn a human emergency into a story that justified my feelings.

Alvarez approached with an updated distress transmission.

"Kestrel reports medical stores compromised and a growing list of wounded. They're asking whether any rescue vessel is responding."

"Tell them assistance has been offered, but no arrival can be guaranteed. Do not give our convoy position."

"Aye."

I watched him write the response.

"Chief, how's your family?" I asked unexpectedly.

He looked surprised.

"My mother is with my sister in Texas, sir. They're safe, last I heard."

"Good."

He hesitated.

"Captain, may I ask something?"

"Of course."

"Are we helping Kestrel because we have to, or because you want to?"

I thought about the difference.

"Both, in a sense. We have obligations toward people in distress when assistance can be provided safely. But beyond that, I don't want our crew to learn that compassion is something we put away when we go to war."

He considered the answer.

"What if they attack another ship?"

"Then we'll defend ourselves and the people we're protecting. The danger of future combat doesn't change the fact that somebody is drowning now."

Alvarez nodded slowly.

"I think I understand."

"I hope I do too," I said.

He returned to his station.

Mara was still watching me.

"You were careful with that answer," she said.

"I had to be."

"Why?"

"Because the moment I sound certain of my own virtue is the moment I should stop talking."

She gave a small smile.

"There's the captain I recognize."

We turned to the tactical picture. Enemy surface contacts remained uncertain to the north. The hostile air picture had not entirely cleared. The submarine threat persisted. No reason existed to believe the battle would not resume.

"What if Nygaard is hit?" Mara asked quietly.

"I'll have to live with the fact that I shared the distress position."

"He chose to go."

"I know. That won't make the consequences easier."

"Command doesn't make you responsible for every choice made by every person who hears your radio."

"Maybe not legally. But I still feel it."

She folded her arms.

"That feeling is useful until it convinces you you're responsible for the ocean."

I glanced toward the bridge video.

"It would be nice if the ocean listened."

"It doesn't even listen to the weather service."

We stood in silence.

A little later Cutlass forwarded an update about Caledonia's recovered sailors. Some were warming in makeshift treatment spaces. Others had been placed in temporary bunks. Her captain, Eileen Ward, was refusing food until the missing names had been reconciled.

I asked for an opportunity to speak to her when medical permitted.

"Captain," Idris said, "we have a high-priority surface assessment pending."

I turned away.

The wider fight had not paused for one fishing boat.

But my thoughts continued following Sigrid. I pictured her working through the waves, the deck slick with saltwater, sailors carrying rescue lines into position. I imagined a man aboard Kestrel looking through smoke at a tiny vessel that had come from the wrong side of the battle to save him.

I wondered what would happen when he saw her flag.

At 0823 Nygaard reported deteriorating conditions. He could still proceed, but the sea was making his approach more difficult. I told him no rescue was worth losing his own crew unnecessarily.

He replied with a brief acknowledgment.

There was an uncertainty in his voice that I recognized: the human struggle between a prudent calculation and the sight of somebody who might be saved.

I was fighting that struggle aboard a destroyer with nearly ten thousand tons of steel and an experienced crew. Nygaard was fighting it aboard a fishing boat.

For the first time that morning, I felt humbled in a way no military ceremony had ever managed.

The radio crackled once more.

"Northstar, Sigrid. We are approaching the reported area."

I answered.

"Copy, Captain. Be careful."

He said nothing for a moment.

Then: "Commander Vale, when you get these families to Iceland, tell them there were ordinary people out here too."

I looked down.

"I will."

We would later learn that Sigrid recovered eleven survivors before the conditions forced her to withdraw. I did not know that yet. At the time I knew only that a civilian captain was steering toward a damaged enemy ship because he believed it was the right thing to do.

The same day, I would order weapons fired against hostile vessels.

I have never found those two facts contradictory.

Perhaps I am simply a sailor. I believe a man may be dangerous while his ship is fighting and still be a human being when he is drowning.

And the sea, which so rarely grants us moral clarity, makes that truth impossible to ignore.
