**THE NAME ON THE METAL — 0845 HOURS**

There was a word painted on the floating wreckage.

At first the bridge lookout couldn't read it. The object turned end over end in the swell, vanishing behind foam before rising again like a piece of driftwood. Someone thought it said *RESCUE*. Someone else saw only a white stripe.

Through the long-range camera I made out three letters.

*...AIR.*

Perhaps it was the end of a unit marking. Perhaps something else. I found myself trying to reconstruct an entire aircraft from the last syllable of a word.

"Log the position and description," I said. "Don't attach an aircraft identity we can't support."

Mercer acknowledged.

The sea was full of stories we would never finish.

A second fragment drifted past a few minutes later, too small to justify slowing the convoy and too dangerous for a recovery in that weather. It flashed silver in the brief daylight and vanished astern.

I was still watching the empty water when Petty Officer Keene appeared at the bridge entrance. He had come up from Combat with a written note, which was unusual enough that I took notice. Most messages traveled through systems; a hand-carried report meant somebody wanted its exact wording preserved.

"Captain, the intelligence watch has collected multiple unconfirmed reports of aircraft losses in the region."

"From which side?"

"Unclear, sir. Some are ours. Some may be hostile. Some reports describe the same event twice."

I opened the note.

It contained three fragments of information: an overdue patrol aircraft, an interrupted distress transmission, and a piece of commercial radio traffic mentioning a flash over the sea. Their times were close enough to suggest a relationship and far enough apart to make that relationship uncertain.

"Have we linked any of this to the debris?"

"No, Captain."

"Good."

Keene blinked.

"I mean it," I said. "The correct conclusion may be that we don't know."

He nodded.

The note reminded me of my first years at sea, when I had imagined the Navy possessed an almost complete knowledge of the ocean. Then a senior chief handed me a list of missing merchant vessels after a winter storm, and I discovered how much could vanish despite satellites, radios, and search aircraft.

The Pacific was vast, he had said. The Atlantic was merely less polite about it.

I folded the report.

"Has any next of kin notification been made from these accounts?"

"Not that we've been told."

"Then no speculation on open circuits. Treat those reports with the care you'd want if your family were waiting at home."

"Understood, Captain."

After he left, I stayed by the bridge windows.

The last of the debris had gone. Northstar's wake carried a pale line across the water, and the merchant ships followed beyond it. The wake would vanish in minutes. The evidence floating in it might vanish sooner.

There is a particular grief in not knowing who has been lost. A sailor whose death is confirmed can be named and honored. A sailor who remains missing is held in a terrible kind of suspension. Families do not know whether to pray for rescue or prepare for burial. Friends learn to dread every incoming message.

I had never been able to decide which was worse.

When my father was young, his older brother served in the merchant marine. He had survived two Atlantic crossings in a war long before I was born. My father showed me a faded photograph of his brother standing beside a freighter's railing with a cigarette behind his ear.

"Why didn't he join the Navy?" I once asked.

My father shrugged. "He was needed where he was."

I remembered those words now, looking at Atlas Mariner. Merchant sailors had become the quiet backbone of our evacuation. Their names would seldom appear on monuments, but they were carrying the food, generators, fuel, and people without which the larger war would mean very little.

"Captain?"

Mercer had returned to the center of the bridge.

"Go ahead."

"Captain Reed on Meridian wants to know whether the debris area requires a course change."

"Tell her no immediate hazard has been confirmed, but to maintain lookout and report anything she sees. We'll send the coordinates of the debris observation through the established channel."

"Aye."

She waited.

"Sir, what do we do about the aircraft crew?"

I looked at her.

"What do you mean?"

"If we don't know who they are. Who tells their families?"

I had no good answer.

"The services will investigate the losses and make notifications through the appropriate channels. Our job is to preserve what we observed. We mustn't pretend certainty."

She lowered her eyes.

"That seems cruel."

"It is."

I glanced at the water again.

"Sometimes making up an answer is crueler."

At 0853, Brooks came over the aviation circuit after her helicopter had been secured and the flight crew had completed their immediate checks.

"Captain, I heard there was American wreckage."

"Yes. We don't know the aircraft."

"We saw a few pieces from a distance. Nothing that could safely be identified. I'm sorry."

"You brought your aircraft home. That matters."

There was a pause.

"Captain, Owen asked whether we should have searched longer."

I thought of her crew in the bad weather, their fuel reserves, the growing threat picture, and the families depending on Northstar.

"Tell him we made a decision with the information available. We should review it later, but he mustn't turn uncertainty into personal guilt."

"Yes, sir."

"How's your crew?"

"Tired. Frustrated. Nobody likes coming back with questions."

"Neither does a commanding officer."

She gave a short, humorless laugh.

"No, I suppose not."

After the call I walked to Combat. Bell was still listening to the ocean. Farouk was matching communications reports against local observations. Idris had begun building a more complicated picture of the surface force gathering beyond our horizon.

Every screen offered another version of the same problem.

There was too much to know, and we knew too little.

I handed Keene his note back.

"Make sure a copy goes into the permanent record. Include the bridge observation and the flight crew's limited report. Mark every unconfirmed element."

"Yes, Captain."

He hesitated.

"Sir, should I put the word we saw on the wreckage?"

"Exactly as seen. Not the word you think it might have been."

He nodded and went back to work.

A little later, the communications system reported a message that appeared to originate from our own operational headquarters. The formatting was convincing. Its instructions were not.

I had just watched the sea swallow the last trace of an aircraft we might never identify. I was in no mood to let another uncertain thing become a fact because it arrived wearing a familiar label.

"Authenticate it," I told Alvarez.

"Already working, Captain."

"Until then, it isn't an order."

He looked up.

"Aye, sir."

The war beyond the horizon was reaching us in fragments—painted metal, missing aircraft, contradictory tracks and messages with familiar signatures. We would have to continue without assembling those fragments into a story that merely pleased us.

And somewhere far away, a family might still be waiting for a pilot who would never come home.

I knew that because I had a family waiting too.
