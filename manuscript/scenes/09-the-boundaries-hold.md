**THE BOUNDARIES HOLD — 1111 HOURS**

The first reliable report from the damaged machinery area was that the remaining propulsion plant was stable. The second was that smoke had reached a passageway we had expected to remain clear.

Neither report described a catastrophe. Together, they warned of one.

Navarro stayed on the engineering circuit and spoke in short statements. He had assigned experienced supervisors to the affected spaces and was working through the ship's established casualty procedures.

"Port equipment isolated. Access restricted. Fire boundary teams report elevated temperatures aft of the affected compartment."

"Personnel status?" I asked.

"Still four unaccounted for."

I stared at the compartment diagram. It was neat and reassuringly two-dimensional. Real passageways were full of handrails, ladders, valves, pipes and equipment that could snag a glove or a breathing apparatus. The drawing did not show the way a ship rolled at the moment a sailor tried to climb through a hatch.

"The crew's operating inside your rescue plan?" I asked.

"Yes, Captain. I'm not sending a team blind."

"Good. Keep it that way."

The ship made a sudden, ugly vibration beneath my feet. A propulsion component shifted load; the remaining machinery responded.

"Engineering, tell me what I just felt."

"Load transient, Captain. We're stabilizing it."

"Can the ship keep maneuvering?"

"With limits. I'll tell the bridge exactly what they are."

Bishop's voice came through the bridge circuit a moment later. He acknowledged the engineering limitations and adjusted the ship's movement so the convoy remained protected without pushing the damaged plant beyond its safe envelope.

This was the part of battle the action reports rarely captured: the necessary conversations between people who understood different pieces of the same problem. Bishop knew the sea. Navarro knew the machinery. Idris knew the threats. No one knew everything. I needed them to make their expertise fit together before the next alarm arrived.

Then Cross entered Combat, carrying a list of personnel available for additional support.

"Fire party rotation is underway," he said. "We're keeping unqualified volunteers out of the hot areas."

"Anyone complaining?"

"Every sailor who wants to go in and pull somebody out."

"Tell them that's not the same as being useful."

"Already did. They don't like me."

"They never do."

He gave the smallest smile.

Outside the machinery area, sailors with firefighting gear waited for their turn. Some had served for years; others had practiced the same drills so many times that they believed they knew what a fire would look like. Now there was real smoke, and the voices on the internal circuit carried the weight of knowledge gained too quickly.

One sailor reported hearing banging through a closed access. The team checked the compartment references and determined the likely location. Another reported a heat condition that made direct entry unsafe.

There was pressure to move faster. I felt it myself. But speed without coordination could turn rescue personnel into casualties.

I had watched firefighters in training disappear into simulated smoke. They had emerged minutes later to jokes and corrective notes. No simulation could reproduce the knowledge that a friend might be dying on the other side of a bulkhead.

Navarro came back on the line.

"Captain, we have a possible route from a protected compartment. Rescue lead is assessing."

I looked at the clock.

"Understood."

One of the reports identified Price as closer to a safer exit than the others. Another suggested Ramirez had stayed with the injured Ochoa and Rios. We did not yet have communications with her.

Mara was reading the evolving casualty status.

"Any sign the fire is affecting additional mission systems?"

"Several secondary alerts. Nothing yet that changes the overall defensive capability," Idris answered.

"Keep comparing reports," she said. "I don't want a display saying one thing while smoke says another."

That became our refrain for the day: do not trust one source when lives depend upon the truth.

A sudden call arrived from engineering.

"We've established voice communication with Chief Ramirez."

The circuit was connected.

I heard breathing, a cough and a scraping sound. Then her voice.

"Captain, Ramirez."

It was the first time in twenty minutes that I felt something close to hope.

"Chief, tell me your situation."

She answered steadily, with the same practical discipline she had shown during every inspection and training drill. She gave us what she knew. She declined to guess about what she could not see. And she reminded me that Rios, barely older than a child himself, was still thinking about the ship's engines.

I almost laughed. Almost.

"Elena," I said, "you and your people are the mission right now."

A long hiss of interference came over the line.

Then, faintly, "Aye, Captain."

There were no heroics in the rescue team's subsequent movements, at least none that they would have recognized. They secured their equipment. They confirmed what they could. They worked in pairs and stayed accountable. They took an available route, rejected another, then finally reached the sailors.

Price came out first.

Then Rios, coughing hard, his hands injured from helping Ochoa.

Then Ochoa himself, carried between sailors who were stumbling from effort.

The reports reached Combat one after another. Each was a small release of pressure, followed immediately by concern for the people who remained inside.

"Ramirez?" I demanded.

No one answered at first.

Then the rescue leader reported that the chief had collapsed before reaching the safer space.

The team went back for her.

Every rule about command told me to remain where I was, preserve the bigger picture, and let trained specialists do their work. I obeyed those rules. It took almost everything I had.

At 1142, Ramirez was out.

At 1212, she was dead.

The fire boundaries held. The engines kept turning. The ship survived.

I would spend years thinking about the terrible difference between those three facts.
