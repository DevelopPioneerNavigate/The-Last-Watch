**VOICES FROM THE WEST — 1851 HOURS**

After Guardian Two-One found us, Combat filled with the sound of ordinary professional conversation.

The incoming aircraft reported the information it could verify. Our watch team gave a concise account of our damaged propulsion, reduced defensive inventory, convoy positions and medical needs. There were no dramatic requests for rescue. Just competent people building a safer picture together.

That, after the last two days, felt miraculous.

I asked Alvarez whether we could establish contact with fleet medical coordination.

"We have a circuit available, Captain."

"Send the serious casualty summary. Start with Ochoa. Tell them we need medical facilities ready at arrival."

"Aye."

I looked at the engineer's status again. The ship was making progress, but Navarro remained cautious about the damaged plant.

"How much more can we ask of her?" I said into the circuit.

"We can get you to port, Captain. I'm not promising elegance."

"Elegance was canceled two days ago."

For the first time since the fire I heard him laugh.

The convoy captains also began to sound different. Meridian's master no longer spoke as though expecting another missile with every sentence. Atlas Mariner asked about harbor arrangements. Lindholm wanted a reliable estimate of when he could tell his passengers they were close to land.

"Don't give them a clock time yet," I told him. "Tell them the corridor is being secured and additional escorts have arrived."

"Understood."

There was a murmur behind his voice, dozens of people talking in a single room.

"What's happening over there?" I asked.

"Somebody started singing."

"Really?"

"An old song. Most of the passengers know it. They're trying to get the children to join."

I closed my eyes.

A group of terrified civilians was singing because, for the first time in many hours, they believed there might be a tomorrow. I wanted to hear it. I didn't ask Lindholm to put it on the circuit. The moment belonged to them.

On our own ship, Cross began organizing what needed to happen when we reached land. Sailors who had spent two days in combat still had to tie up a destroyer, receive inspectors, transfer casualties, preserve logs, maintain security and feed the watch.

"There's no such thing as arriving," he told me. "Just another list of things you forgot to do."

"You're making port sound unpleasant."

"I'll enjoy it after the paperwork."

He paused.

"Captain, the crew is asking if you think we're safe."

I looked at the newest friendly tracks. Several hours earlier they would have been another uncertainty. Now they had identities, verified communications and a mission aligned with ours.

"I think we're safer than we were this morning. That's what I can say."

"I'll pass it along."

"Tell them the final approach still deserves full attention."

"Already planning to."

We had a brief connection with the medical officer assisting Cutlass. Captain Mallory had survived treatment and was stable enough to speak for a few moments.

He came on sounding hoarse.

"Daniel."

"Edward."

"You sound like a man who's been eating American food."

"Better than being hit by a missile."

"Debatable."

I smiled so hard my face hurt.

"How are you really?"

"Damaged. The ship's worse. My people are doing extraordinary work."

"I know."

"I heard about Ramirez."

The smile disappeared.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Thank you."

He paused, then changed the subject with the mercy of an old friend.

"When we reach port, you still owe me a tea."

"I believe you're the one who owes me."

"Perfect. We can spend the next twenty years arguing about it."

"That sounds good."

The circuit closed.

I looked around the room at the operators who had been with me since the first contact. Some were still in the same chairs, though we had rotated them whenever possible. Their faces had the drawn look of people who were awake because responsibility had not allowed them to stop.

"Listen up," I said.

Several heads turned.

"Friendly forces have established contact. The convoy remains together. We're not finished until every ship is secured safely, but you've carried these people an extraordinary distance."

I let that rest.

"Don't relax your standards. You can relax your shoulders a little."

Keene laughed. Even Idris smiled.

Then the bridge called with a routine navigation question and the spell broke. We returned to work, as sailors do.

Hours later, when the lights of Iceland finally appeared, I understood why that ordinary conversation had mattered. It had been the first time we could imagine what came after survival.
