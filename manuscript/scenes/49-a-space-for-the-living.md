**A SPACE FOR THE LIVING — 1312 HOURS**

Leah Okafor had turned a supply locker into a place where people could cry.

It wasn't intentional. She'd cleared a space for medical equipment outside the treatment area, and the little compartment had become the nearest thing to privacy on a ship that had none. One sailor had sat there after learning about Ramirez. Another had gone in to wash smoke from his face and stayed because he couldn't make himself return to his watch.

I found Okafor standing beside the open door, making a list with a short pencil.

"Captain," she said without looking up.

"Doctor's orders?"

"I'm not a doctor."

"Then whose orders?"

"Mine. They're much harder to ignore."

She closed the notebook.

I could see her fatigue in the tiny movements she made, the care required to put the pencil into her pocket instead of dropping it on the deck.

"How's Ochoa?"

"Still critical. We're supporting him and monitoring every change. We need shore care as soon as we can reach it."

"Price?"

"Stable enough to make terrible jokes. He asked whether his guitar survived."

"What did you tell him?"

"That the guitar had committed no offense serious enough to deserve him playing it."

I smiled.

"And Rios?"

"In pain. Frightened. Not in immediate danger, as far as we can determine. The burns need proper treatment when we reach port. His hands matter for the life he wants after this."

She looked at me carefully.

"The physical injuries are only part of it."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Her question landed harder than it should have.

"I'm learning."

"Good."

She opened the supply locker.

A young sailor sat on a folded blanket against the bulkhead, his knees drawn up. He had no visible injury except a small cut on his chin.

"Petty Officer Calder," she said, "the captain has come to see how people are doing."

He began to stand.

"Stay where you are," I told him.

He lowered himself again.

I recognized him from the rescue reports. He had helped bring the injured engineers through the smoke. He had also been one of the men who returned toward Ramirez when the boundaries allowed.

"I've been cleared for duty," he said.

"That's good."

"I just can't stop shaking."

I looked at his hands.

They were trembling.

"When does it happen?"

"Whenever somebody uses the word *fire*. Or I hear a tool fall. Or a compartment door closes too hard."

He swallowed.

"I know there's nothing burning. My head knows."

Okafor crouched beside him.

"Your body is still reacting to what happened. We can work with that."

Calder shook his head.

"People need me out there."

"People need you safe to work," she said. "Those aren't always the same thing."

He looked toward me for a different answer.

I could have given it. I could have told him a warship needed every qualified sailor and that he should return when ordered. All of that was true in the abstract.

But I had just lost a chief who believed the purpose of training was to protect the person beside you, not to satisfy the appearance of readiness.

"Leah is responsible for the medical assessment," I told him. "Your supervisor is responsible for assigning safe work. Nobody is asking you to prove you're brave by hiding what happened."

He looked down.

"Understood."

I stepped outside with Okafor.

"Are we able to keep handling this?"

"For now. I can manage the immediate cases. I cannot promise the crew won't need more support when we reach shore."

"Make a written list of the needs we should hand over. Not only injuries. Follow-up arrangements too."

"I'm already doing it."

Of course she was.

She unfolded her notebook. Beside the names of the injured were short notes on transfer priority, monitoring requirements, and follow-up concerns. There was also an additional page that contained no medical diagnoses.

It was titled *PEOPLE WHO SHOULD NOT BE LEFT ALONE TONIGHT.*

"Is that an official form?" I asked.

"No. It should be."

She closed it.

I stood silently for a moment.

"Leah, are you on that list?"

She stared at me.

"Captain—"

"Are you?"

"No."

"Should you be?"

She looked away.

"I had to tell a nineteen-year-old that the woman who saved him had died. I had to stop a rescue team from returning to a space that wasn't safe, even though they knew somebody was still inside. I spent twenty minutes helping a man breathe when he thought every alarm meant he was back in the fire."

Her voice remained calm until the last word.

Then it broke.

I didn't reach for her. She was holding herself together with the professional privacy she'd maintained all afternoon, and I would not take that away by making the scene about my sympathy.

"I'll arrange qualified relief for your watch," I said.

She shook her head.

"There's no relief I can simply invent."

"Then we'll work with the people we have. You can name which duties are essential and which can safely wait. But you are not exempt from needing care because everyone else needs it too."

She looked at me.

"You sound like your executive officer."

"She's been a bad influence."

Okafor gave a tired laugh.

Then she grew serious.

"I need something from you, Captain."

"Tell me."

"When you speak at the memorial, don't tell the crew they're strong enough to carry anything. Tell them they can ask for help before they reach the point of breaking."

I nodded.

"I will."

"And don't promise they'll all recover in the same way."

"No."

"Some will want to talk. Some won't. Some will have good days and then suddenly hear a sound that puts them back here. We need to make room for that."

I thought of the ship as it had been before the attack: a narrow collection of people trained to control every emergency through procedures and practice. What we hadn't practiced enough was living with the aftermath.

"We'll make room," I said.

She searched my face.

"That's a command decision too."

"I know."

An orderly appeared at the end of the passageway.

"Corpsman Okafor. Ochoa's monitor needs your attention."

She turned immediately.

The conversation ended as all shipboard conversations seemed to end that day: not with resolution, but with another need.

I followed her far enough to be useful, then stopped at the entrance to the treatment space.

Inside, people worked around Ochoa's bunk. The faces were focused. The instruments were being checked. Nobody asked the captain to do anything he wasn't trained to do.

I stayed out of their way.

At 1321, Okafor stepped back through the doorway.

"Stable for the moment," she said.

I nodded.

I didn't say *good*.

Good was a word for a ship alongside, a child asleep in a safe bed, or a friend arriving home with nothing worse than a story to tell.

For now, stable would have to do.

I went back toward Combat carrying the list of follow-up needs.

At the ladder, Calder was sitting upright in the supply locker with both feet on the deck. He had stopped shaking enough to hold a cup of water. A friend sat beside him without speaking.

They saw me pass.

Neither stood.

I considered that an improvement.

It meant they understood, for a few minutes at least, that surviving the fire did not require proving anything to me.

I climbed the ladder slowly.

The living still needed a ship.

I intended to give them one.
