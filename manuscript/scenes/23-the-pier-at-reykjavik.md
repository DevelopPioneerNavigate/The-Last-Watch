**THE PIER AT REYKJAVÍK — 1002 HOURS**

No one aboard Northstar cheered when the last mooring line was made fast.

A few sailors looked relieved. Others stared at the pier as though they no longer remembered what solid ground meant. The deck had stopped moving, but their bodies had not. A ship can carry a crew out of a battle faster than its people can stop fighting it.

I stood on the bridge and watched ambulances gather near the gangway.

The first medical team boarded with the controlled urgency of people who had received a long list of casualties and knew the list did not tell the whole story. Leah Okafor met them at the top of the ladder, handed over a written briefing, and led them below.

I watched her disappear.

She had barely slept since the fire. She had stitched, stabilized, comforted, and argued with wounded people who wanted to return to duty before they could stand. She had spent hours trying to save Elena Ramirez. I wondered when somebody would tell her she was allowed to be tired.

Mara stood beside me.

"Harbor coordination wants a formal arrival report."

"They'll have it."

"They also want a casualty update, a repair assessment, and a meeting with three different headquarters staffs."

"Tell them they'll get the essential reports as quickly as safety permits. The rest can wait until our injured are ashore."

She nodded.

"Already did."

I looked at her.

"You're getting predictable."

"Only to people who pay attention."

Below us, Rios came onto the weather deck supported by another sailor. His bandaged hands made the rail awkward to hold, and he moved with the careful concentration of a person rediscovering the basic mechanics of walking. The moment he saw land, he stopped.

I thought he might be crying.

Then he turned his face away, and I allowed him the privacy.

Two men from the Norwegian harbor service stood at the bottom of the gangway. They were waiting to receive our first medical transfer. One saluted our ensign without being asked.

That almost undid me.

For two days we had lived in a world where every unknown contact could be a threat. Now two strangers stood on a pier and raised their hands to a flag because they understood that the people beneath it had been through something.

Across the harbor, Aurora was being guided toward a passenger terminal.

Her deck windows were crowded with faces.

I lifted my binoculars.

A woman held a little boy against her shoulder. Someone farther along waved an American flag no bigger than a handkerchief. Several passengers clapped. We could not hear them over the harbor sounds, but the motion was unmistakable.

Bishop approached.

"Captain, the port authority is coordinating our berth security. We have confirmation that the surviving merchants can offload after inspection."

"Good. Any change with Meridian?"

"Fuel transfer will be prioritized once the necessary safety checks are complete."

I nodded.

The mission was finally turning into results. Medical supplies from Caledonia had been lost, but Atlas Mariner's food and shelters were still aboard. Meridian's fuel would support rescue and relief operations. Aurora's passengers would step onto land.

I wanted to feel victorious.

Instead, I felt hollow.

At 1030 a harbor officer came aboard with an interpreter and a document packet. She thanked us in careful English, then asked for an update on the evacuees. Her name was Lieutenant Commander Elín Jónsdóttir. She had spent the night coordinating the arrivals of three separate relief ships, and she looked nearly as tired as we did.

"I have hundreds of volunteers at the terminal," she said. "Doctors, social workers, interpreters, people bringing blankets and food. The city is doing everything it can."

"Thank you."

"We have heard what happened to your convoy."

I looked down.

"Not all of it, I suspect."

"No," she said. "Probably not."

Her honesty reminded me of Bell.

Jónsdóttir glanced toward Aurora.

"Some of those families will live here for months. Some for years. A few may never return to their old homes."

I remembered Liv's drawing, the square house with the green roof.

"Then I hope they feel welcome."

"We will try."

She handed me a note written in Icelandic and English.

"A child aboard Aurora asked someone to give you this. We were told the message had to reach the American captain."

I unfolded it.

The paper was wrinkled, its edges damp.

There was a simple drawing of a gray ship with a bright yellow sun above it. The ship was enormous compared with the little ferry behind it. A row of tiny stick figures stood along the deck, each wearing a blue cap.

Beneath the picture, in uneven English, were the words:

*THANK YOU FOR NOT LEAVING US.*

I stared at the paper.

"Do you know who drew it?"

"I believe her name is Liv."

The room around me seemed to recede.

I remembered the little girl in the Norwegian terminal, drawing the home that no longer existed.

I had no idea whether she had recognized me, or whether Lindholm had told her which ship we commanded. Perhaps she had seen our silhouette through the ferry windows. Perhaps the drawing was the only way she knew to say what she was feeling.

I folded it carefully and placed it inside my jacket, beside Rebecca's letter.

"Please tell her I received it."

Jónsdóttir nodded.

"Of course."

She left to continue coordinating the harbor.

Mara watched me.

"What's that?"

I showed her.

She read the words once.

Then she looked out the bridge window for a long time.

"That's why we stayed," she said.

"Yes."

We went below together to inspect the ship before the official visitors arrived.

There were scars everywhere. A metal panel had been removed from the upper deck where fragments had struck. Damaged antenna components lay secured for inspection. Near engineering, the deck had been cleaned, but a faint smell of smoke remained despite the air being circulated through the passageways.

I paused outside the compartment where Ramirez had died.

Two sailors were standing nearby, not speaking.

One of them straightened.

"Captain."

"At ease."

They looked toward the closed door.

"Anything you need?" I asked.

The older sailor shook his head.

"Just wanted to be here a moment, sir."

I nodded.

"So did I."

We stood together without words.

A ship does not have a chapel large enough to hold everything its crew carries after a battle. Sometimes a passageway becomes one. Sometimes a closed door. Sometimes a coffee mug left untouched on a desk.

The sailors eventually moved on. Mara remained beside me.

"Daniel, when the visitors come aboard, they're going to ask for a timeline of the fire."

"They'll have it."

"And there will be questions."

"There should be."

She studied me.

"Don't try to answer questions we haven't investigated yet."

I knew what she meant. Guilt was already turning every recollection into an accusation. I was tempted to claim responsibility for everything that had happened, as if doing so could give Ramirez's death a shape I understood.

"I won't," I said.

She gave a brief nod.

We returned to the bridge.

Outside, people were descending Aurora's gangway. Some carried suitcases, others carried children. A wheelchair moved down a portable ramp. An elderly man stepped onto the pier and turned back toward the ferry as though he needed to be certain it had really brought him there.

Then I saw a little girl in a blue coat holding a piece of red paper.

Liv.

I could not make out her face through the glass, but I knew the coat and the way she stood close to her mother.

She stepped onto the pier.

A volunteer offered her a blanket.

She accepted it.

Then she turned toward the harbor and raised her hand.

For a moment I thought she might be waving directly at us.

I raised mine in return.

Perhaps nobody saw.

It didn't matter.

At last, somebody from Combat called me back to a command conference. There were reports to sign, casualties to transfer, authorities to brief. The ship had survived; she still required a captain.

I put the little drawing in a dry folder, away from the official documents.

Some things belonged in the ship's historical record.

That one belonged somewhere closer to my heart.
