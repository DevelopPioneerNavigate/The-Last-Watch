**BEFORE THE SECOND SALVO — 0919 HOURS**

There was a narrow space between the morning's first missile engagement and the second one, and in that space we made dozens of decisions that would never appear in a history book.

A repair team replaced a damaged exterior component. Engineers adjusted a system that was unhappy with the cold. A cook made sandwiches and carried them to Combat. Medical checked two sailors who had been thrown against a bulkhead during maneuvering. The bridge reviewed the convoy's next course change.

None of it looked like warfare.

All of it was how a warship stayed alive.

I was standing behind the primary tactical display when Farouk brought me a communications assessment.

"Captain, we're seeing inconsistencies in the wider reporting again."

"Hostile interference?"

"Possible, but not confirmed. One source suggests the northern surface group has changed formation. Another shows little movement. The reports don't line up."

I studied both.

"Can we establish what each source actually measured?"

"That's what we're doing."

"Good. Don't combine them just to make the display neat."

Farouk nodded and returned to her operators.

The combat information center was crowded and dim. Faces appeared briefly in blue light as screens changed. On the larger displays, the ocean had become lines, boxes and estimated positions. The representation was sophisticated, but it had one inescapable flaw.

The enemy did not have to behave the way the symbols suggested.

Idris approached with a list of our remaining limitations.

"Captain, I need to discuss defensive endurance."

"Go ahead."

"If the enemy repeats the first attack with additional weapons, we'll be in a difficult position."

"How difficult?"

He looked at the operators around us, then lowered his voice.

"Not impossible. But there is no comfortable margin."

I studied him.

"That's your professional judgment?"

"Yes."

"Then don't soften it for my benefit."

"I wasn't planning to."

That was why I trusted him.

The enemy could choose the time and direction of the next attack. We had to defend the convoy, preserve the ship, and avoid exhausting capabilities we could not replace at sea. It was a problem with too many demands and not enough resources.

I could almost hear the younger officers asking whether there was a clever way out.

I had asked that question myself many times.

Usually the answer was that somebody had to accept risk.

Mara entered from a departmental status conference.

"Engineering reports steady. Aviation has the helicopter secured and the crew recovering. The British have reduced but usable communications. We have one sailor asking whether he can return to duty with a minor injury."

"Medical?"

"Not yet cleared."

"Then no."

"I told him that."

She glanced at Idris's assessment.

"Bad?"

"Serious," I said.

"That's bad."

"Yes."

I watched her read it.

Mara was one of the few people aboard whose presence could make me feel less alone without removing an ounce of responsibility. She did not offer easy reassurance. She made sure I wasn't trying to carry facts somebody else understood better.

"What are we missing?" she asked.

Idris considered the question.

"Maybe the enemy's coordination is weaker than it appears. Maybe stronger. We don't know how much of their information is current."

"And our own?"

"Not perfect."

She looked at me.

"Then we should be wary of any plan that requires everything to happen exactly as predicted."

I nodded.

"Agreed."

We asked for fresh status from the ships we were escorting.

Aurora was maintaining its assigned position. Atlas Mariner reported no immediate mechanical concerns. Meridian remained concerned about rougher weather ahead, but her master believed the tanker could continue at the revised speed. Cutlass remained damaged and occupied with repairs.

Every report had caveats.

I preferred that to false confidence.

Then Chief Bell called.

"Captain, sonar."

I moved to her position.

"What's happening?"

"Nothing I'd classify as an attack. But we're hearing more background activity than I like."

"Submarine?"

"Could be. Could be a lot of things."

She handed me the headset.

The sound was an indistinct low wash, broken by faint changes in pitch. I knew better than to make a confident statement about it.

"Tell me what matters," I said.

"We cannot promise the underwater area is clear."

I looked at her.

"Could you ever?"

"Not in weather like this."

I handed back the headset.

"Then the defensive posture stands."

Bell nodded.

As I turned away, I saw Seaman Shah staring at a screen with a frown.

"Priya?"

She looked up.

"Captain, one of the signals doesn't fit the model we've been using. I can't explain it yet."

"Have you told Chief Bell?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"She told me to keep it separate from the others until I could justify a link."

"Good advice."

Shah looked relieved.

"When you're new," I told her, "you think the senior people want you to be right immediately. What we want is for you to be honest while you're finding out."

She nodded.

I returned to the center of the room.

An operator announced a change in the northern surface picture. A track was moving, though not quickly enough to resolve the larger question of the enemy's intentions. Idris and Farouk checked the information independently.

For a few moments the tactical display appeared to settle into a coherent pattern.

I distrusted how satisfying that felt.

A pattern can be useful. It can also become a story that makes an uncertain world look easy to understand.

I remembered a seminar at the Naval War College. A retired admiral had shown us a battle map and asked which movement was the turning point. We all chose the arrow that looked decisive. Then he revealed that the commander who supposedly made the decisive maneuver had no idea the enemy fleet was in that position.

The map was correct.

The lesson we drew from it was wrong.

I looked at Idris.

"Do we know enough to say they're preparing another missile attack?"

"Not yet."

"Do we know enough to prepare for one?"

"Yes, Captain."

"Then let's remain ready and keep the assessment honest."

The room returned to its rhythm.

At 0925 the bridge reported improving visibility in a narrow sector to the west. It lasted only a few minutes. The ocean beyond remained gray and immense.

I went to the coffee station. The pot was empty.

Keene looked over.

"Sorry, sir. I took the last."

"Then you owe us a new one."

"Yes, Captain."

He stood, hesitated, and sat back down.

"Sir, do you want me to leave my console?"

I stared at him for a moment.

Then laughed.

"No, Petty Officer. Keep watching your screen. I'll make the coffee."

Several tired people smiled.

I filled the pot and waited as the machine rattled through its ordinary work. For thirty seconds I listened to water heating instead of radio calls.

I was still holding the empty cup when the tactical operator's voice sharpened.

"Captain, additional high-speed tracks developing to the north."

The coffee machine continued dripping.

Idris moved toward the display.

The room changed around him. Operators leaned forward, callouts shortened, and the sound of paper being moved across a console became audible in the small gap before the next report.

I set down the cup.

"Classify the contacts," I said.

He studied the picture, then looked up.

"Captain, possible coordinated missile launch."

Beyond the steel walls, the North Atlantic looked exactly as it had one minute earlier.

Nothing visible had changed.

Everything had changed.

The first hostile tracks appeared on the main display.

And the second attack began.
