**THE BOOK OF NAMES — TEN MONTHS AFTER ICELAND**

Rios arrived at my office carrying a blue notebook and wearing a uniform that no longer seemed too large for him.

His hands had healed, although the pale marks across his fingers remained. He had spent months recovering, then returned to a training assignment while the Navy and his doctors determined what duties were appropriate. His first request had been to work with machinery again.

My first response had been worry.

My second had been pride.

"Captain," he said.

"Come in, Jacob."

He sat opposite my desk and placed the notebook between us.

It was Chief Ramirez's qualification book.

For months I'd known the engineering department had made arrangements for its training notes to be preserved. I also knew Rios had wanted to read what she'd written about him and hadn't been ready when the book first came ashore.

"Chief Cross said you might want to see this," he told me.

"Only if you want to show me."

He opened the cover carefully.

Inside were pages of ordinary remarks. Dates. Qualifications. Names. Corrections. Small observations written in a hand that I could have recognized across a room full of documents.

Rios turned to the page with his name.

*Strong instincts. Remind him not to confuse speed with competence. Could be a very good chief someday.*

He ran his thumb along the edge of the paper.

"She thought I could become a chief."

"Yes."

"Before the fire."

"Yes."

He looked up.

"Do you think she was wrong?"

I had expected him to ask whether he had been brave. Or whether he could have saved Ramirez. Or whether I had thought about him after the battle. Instead he asked whether a person who knew him well had been mistaken about his future.

"No," I said.

He looked down.

"Sometimes I can't even walk into a training compartment without hearing the alarms. I know nobody's trapped, but I keep looking for them."

"Have you told your care team?"

"Yes. We're working on it."

"Good."

He gave a small, tired smile.

"I used to think I wanted to go back to sea so I could prove I wasn't afraid. Now I know I'm still afraid. I just don't want that to be the only thing that matters."

I thought of Ramirez telling him fear didn't disqualify him from serving.

"Jacob, being able to name your fear doesn't make you less capable. But you don't owe anyone a return to a compartment before you're ready."

He nodded.

"I know."

He touched the notebook again.

"I keep remembering something she said during a drill. That we're responsible for the person beside us, not just the equipment in front of us."

"That sounds like her."

"I was so annoyed when she made us repeat the exercise."

"Everybody was annoyed with her at least once."

He laughed.

"More than once."

A long silence followed.

Finally he said, "I thought saving Ochoa would feel different. I thought afterward I would know I had done something good. Mostly I just remember what happened to her."

I looked at the notebook.

"Good things and terrible things can happen in the same hour. One doesn't cancel the other."

He considered that.

"That's what the counselor told me."

"Then you've found somebody smarter than your captain."

He smiled a little more.

Outside my office, the station went about an ordinary afternoon. A lawnmower sounded beyond the parking lot. A car horn blared once, annoyed rather than alarmed. Somewhere nearby a sailor laughed into a telephone.

It was the first summer I had spent ashore in years, and the simplest sounds still surprised me.

Rios closed the book.

"I brought something else."

He took a folded photograph from his pocket. It showed him standing beside Ben Ochoa at a rehabilitation center. Ochoa leaned on a cane and grinned at the camera. Both men looked as though they'd spent the afternoon arguing about something ridiculous.

"He's walking better," Rios said.

"That's good."

"His twins made him a medal out of cereal-box cardboard."

I laughed.

"For what?"

"For getting up from a chair without complaining."

"Did he earn it?"

"Not even close."

We laughed together.

Then Rios grew serious.

"Captain, can I ask you about the investigation?"

"Within what I'm authorized to discuss, yes."

"They said the rescue teams followed their procedures. They also said we could improve some things."

"That's right."

"Does that mean her death was somebody's mistake?"

I let the question settle.

"The review identified areas we could improve. It did not find a simple action that would guarantee a different outcome. We need to learn from the evidence, not turn grief into blame."

He nodded.

"That's what I wanted to hear."

"Did you expect me to say something else?"

"I didn't know."

That answer felt familiar. So many of the hardest conversations since Iceland had ended at the edge of what people knew.

I stood and walked to the shelf where I kept a photograph of Northstar under sail. Rios followed my gaze.

"Do you miss her?" he asked.

"Every day."

"Even after everything?"

"Especially after everything."

He looked puzzled.

"A ship becomes the place where you learn who people really are," I said. "Not because war reveals some magical truth, but because ordinary kindness gets very important when nobody can leave."

He looked at the photograph.

"I miss the galley at breakfast."

I laughed.

"That may be the first time anybody's said that."

"Chief Mendoza made decent pancakes."

"Don't tell him I agreed."

Rios stood.

"Captain, I wanted to ask your permission for something."

"Go ahead."

"There's a new group of sailors at the training command. They keep asking me what combat was like. I don't want to tell war stories. I want to talk about the little things Ramirez taught us."

I looked at him.

"What would you say?"

"That the checklist matters because there's a person on the other end of it. That you don't get to be brave by being careless. That asking for help early is part of being good at the job."

I felt something tighten behind my eyes.

"I think she'd be proud of that."

He looked away.

"Me too."

I held out my hand.

He shook it carefully, the scars across his fingers pale against mine.

"Thank you for coming, Jacob."

"Thank you, Captain."

He took the notebook and went to the door.

Then he stopped.

"Sir, did you ever finish writing what happened?"

I thought of the document I'd started in Reykjavík, the nights I'd spent trying to turn memories into sentences without making the past neater than it had been.

"Not yet."

"Are you going to?"

"Yes."

He nodded.

"Good. I want people to know she was funny."

I smiled.

"So do I."

When he left, I sat by the window for a long time.

The book on my desk was still unfinished. The official record had described Ramirez as a casualty, a chief, a member of the damage-control team. All true. None sufficient.

Rios had reminded me that she was also the woman who believed a frightened nineteen-year-old could become a leader, who laughed at the mess-deck table, and who kept notes because she wanted her people to grow.

I opened the manuscript.

At the bottom of the latest chapter I wrote:

*When the history of the Northern War is reduced to battles and losses, someone should remember the sailors who made coffee, corrected checklists, and saw futures in young people the rest of us barely noticed.*

I read the sentence once.

Then I left it.

For the first time, I believed there might be enough words to tell the truth.
