**THE SHIPS THAT ANSWERED — 1008 HOURS**

At 1008 I received a message from Cutlass that contained almost no military information.

*THE LIEUTENANT SAYS THE KETTLE SURVIVED.*

I stared at the words.

Then I laughed.

It was not a comfortable laugh. It came out too loudly, in a room where exhausted people were expecting another warning. Two operators looked toward me with expressions that suggested the captain had finally lost the argument with his own mind.

"What is it, sir?" Idris asked.

I handed him the message.

He read it.

"The kettle?"

"Edward Mallory's tea kettle," I said. "Apparently it's being reported as an essential piece of equipment."

Idris smiled, but I saw how hard he was working to do it.

The message had been relayed through an improvised communications arrangement as Cutlass's technicians restored what they could. It carried no reassuring statement about casualties or damage. Yet it confirmed something my tactical picture could not.

Someone aboard that battered frigate still had enough spirit to make a joke.

I asked Alvarez whether he could obtain an updated personnel and medical status without overwhelming their repair teams.

"We have a limited circuit," he said. "I'll request when they have capacity."

"Tell them there is no requirement to interrupt damage control to satisfy my curiosity."

"Understood."

Then I went to the bridge for a view of the ship herself.

Through the gray weather I could just make out Cutlass at a distance. Her silhouette appeared wrong. One part of her upper structure was darker than it should have been; another seemed broken. Smoke trailed low and thin behind her as she moved through the waves.

She had not lost her way, but she had lost some of the grace of a vessel under full power. I knew her crew would be working desperately to keep her moving and safe.

Mercer joined me at the window.

"British captain still alive?"

"Yes."

"Do you know how he's doing?"

"No."

She watched the frigate.

"My grandfather served aboard a merchant ship in a war," she said. "He told me the only thing worse than seeing another ship hit was watching it move afterward and not knowing who was still aboard."

I looked toward her.

"Did he talk about it often?"

"Not until he was old. Then he couldn't stop."

There was a long silence.

"I used to think he was telling war stories because they made him interesting," she continued. "Now I think he was trying to make sure somebody remembered the people who hadn't come back."

I followed the smoke across the horizon.

"That sounds right."

A radio call came from Cutlass.

"Northstar, Cutlass. Executive officer speaking."

Her name was Commander Eleanor Shaw. I'd met her at a coalition exercise the previous spring, when she'd corrected one of my briefings on British procedures so politely that it took me an hour to realize I'd been embarrassed.

"Commander Shaw," I said. "How's your ship?"

"Not pretty, Daniel. Still a ship."

"How's Edward?"

"Conscious. Angry about being told to remain in medical."

"Then he's improving."

"That's my assessment."

I heard the exhaustion behind her dry words.

She gave me the broad status. Fires contained or being brought under control. Some communications restored. Several critical casualties receiving treatment. Damage assessment continuing. They could make safe progress at reduced capability but could not guarantee every combat system.

It was neither defeat nor recovery. It was the difficult middle between them.

"We have your last confirmed situation report," I said. "Take the time you need to stabilize. We'll cover what we can."

"We know."

She hesitated.

"We have nineteen of Caledonia's people aboard. Some are beginning to ask whether we're taking them back into the fight."

I pictured Eileen Ward trying to care for her survivors aboard a ship now wounded as well.

"What have you told them?"

"That we are keeping them as safe as circumstances allow."

"Good."

"Daniel, they think our missile damage is because of them."

I closed my eyes.

"They shouldn't."

"Try telling them that."

Shaw was quiet for a moment.

"There's a young engineer who keeps asking about his brother. Every time a sailor enters medical, he looks up because he thinks it might be the man he's waiting for."

I knew the name.

"Colin Pike."

"Yes."

"I spoke to him earlier."

"He asked whether we were taking him to a ship that could keep searching."

I leaned against the bridge console.

"Tell him Northstar has preserved the last known positions and the rescue record. No safe opportunity will be ignored, but I cannot promise another search in hostile water."

"I'll tell him."

I listened to wind hiss across the microphone.

"Eleanor, if you need assistance beyond what we can currently provide, ask."

"Believe me, we will."

She paused, then added, "And for the record, our captain wants you to know he has no intention of dying before he collects that dreadful American coffee you owe him."

I smiled.

"Tell him I intend to make it myself."

"That might finish him."

For a few seconds, we were colleagues again, not commanders looking at casualty plots.

Then Shaw disconnected to deal with another repair report.

I stood by the bridge windows until I could no longer clearly see the British frigate.

There was a tendency in war to think of another ship's courage as something displayed in its greatest moment: when it fired, when it turned into danger, when its flag survived the explosion. But courage after impact was a different thing. It was the sailor who had to reenter a compartment where a friend had been wounded. The executive officer who became responsible for a damaged warship before she knew whether the captain would live. The medical assistant who washed blood from a deck and made room for the next patient.

The battle had not ended when Cutlass was struck.

For her crew, another had begun.

When I returned to Combat, Cross was speaking quietly with one of the watchstanders.

"Everything all right?" I asked.

Cross nodded.

"He asked whether the British are going to be able to stay with us."

"What did you say?"

"That they're doing their best and we will do ours."

I thought about Cutlass limping through the waves, full of wounded people who had already survived one sinking.

"Good answer."

The watchstander looked toward me.

"Captain, are they still part of the convoy?"

"Absolutely."

"Even if they can't fight?"

I studied the question. It was not cruel. He was asking what belonging meant when a ship could no longer perform its original role.

"Especially then," I said.

He nodded.

I went to the tactical display and looked at the British symbol moving slowly beside the convoy.

There were ships we protected because they carried civilians. There were ships we protected because they carried supplies. And there were ships we protected because they had stood beside us when danger arrived.

Cutlass belonged to the last category.

The damaged frigate continued west.

For the next fifteen minutes she made no call.

I watched anyway.
