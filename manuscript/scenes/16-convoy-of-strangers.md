**THE PEOPLE WE HAD PROMISED — FIVE DAYS EARLIER**

The first time I saw Aurora, she was tied against a Norwegian pier beneath a cold gray sky, loading people who carried everything they owned in plastic shopping bags.

It was five days before the battle, although in memory it sometimes feels like five minutes. The wind blew rain under the terminal roof. Soldiers directed lines of civilians toward different gangways. At the end of the pier a yellow crane stood motionless above stacks of containers, as useless as a cathedral bell when the city around it was burning.

I had been sent ashore to confirm the convoy's readiness. The operational orders contained a cargo list, passenger totals, route restrictions, and a confident statement about available escort protection. The paperwork made the evacuation sound like a transportation problem.

The scene on the pier was a human problem that happened to involve ships.

A little boy in a blue coat sat on a suitcase while his mother argued with a port official. A man with an injured hand struggled to push a wheelchair over a steel plate bridging two surfaces of different heights. Near a warehouse door, a family stood around an elderly woman who refused to board until somebody found her husband. Every few minutes an air-raid siren sounded somewhere beyond the harbor; most people no longer looked up.

Captain Peter Lindholm met me at the ferry's gangway.

He was sixty, broad-shouldered, and wore the worn blue jacket of a man who had spent more years on ships than in offices. We shook hands while rain dripped from the peak of his cap.

"Commander Vale," he said. "They tell me you're going to take us to Iceland."

"That's the plan."

"Plans are what happens before the weather reads them."

"I've noticed."

He looked at Northstar, lying across the harbor with a tug alongside her.

"Can you protect a ship like mine?"

I followed his gaze. Aurora's high, bright windows made her conspicuous. She had the profile of a vessel intended to bring families across peaceful water. Nothing about her shape was designed to survive a missile.

"We'll do everything within our power."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only honest answer I have."

Lindholm studied me for a moment. Then he nodded and opened the gangway gate.

"Come aboard."

Inside the ferry, ordinary holiday signs remained on the walls. A poster advertised coffee and waffles. A children's playroom displayed paper stars above plastic chairs. The shops were closed, but the bright lettering over the counters still promised souvenirs and chocolate. The peaceful details looked almost indecent beside the evacuees arranging blankets on the floor.

Lindholm led me through the main lounge.

"Six hundred and twelve passengers by the latest count," he said. "Eighty-four children. The medical needs are manageable for now, but I have elderly passengers who cannot move quickly. Several families are traveling with young babies. We've turned two lounges into sleeping spaces and one into a clinic."

"Do you have enough staff?"

"Enough to sail. Not enough to make anyone comfortable."

A volunteer nurse intercepted us, her sleeves rolled up, a pen behind her ear.

"Captain, we need more drinking water on Deck Four."

"I've asked stores," Lindholm said.

"They've sent cups."

"Good. What about water?"

"That's my point."

He looked at me with weary amusement.

"Come, Commander. You see what command looks like on a ferry."

We walked to the upper deck, where a row of windows overlooked the pier. A little girl was drawing with a red crayon on the back of a safety leaflet. She had drawn a square house with a green roof, a large tree, and three people holding hands.

"Is that your house?" I asked.

She nodded without looking up.

"Where is it?"

She pointed vaguely toward the shore.

"Is it far away?"

She stopped drawing.

"It was there yesterday," she said.

Her mother, sitting beside her, squeezed her shoulder.

I had faced admirals without losing my composure. That child's sentence nearly finished me.

I stepped away before they could see.

In the crew's small meeting space, Lindholm spread his evacuation roster on the table. He spoke about food service, water, safety briefings, and how to keep people from rushing to the windows if they heard an explosion. I listened, but my mind kept returning to the little girl and her drawing.

"What is she called?" I asked.

Lindholm glanced up.

"Who?"

"The child in the lounge. Red crayon."

"Liv. Her mother told me their house was hit three days ago. They were in the cellar."

I nodded as if the answer were merely another fact.

Lindholm folded the roster.

"Commander, I have a favor to ask."

"Go ahead."

"If something happens, speak to me like another captain. Tell me what you need me to do, not what you think I want to hear."

I met his eyes.

"Agreed."

"And I'll do the same."

We shook hands a second time. It was a small agreement compared with the forces gathering in the North Atlantic. It mattered anyway.

Back on the pier I met Eileen Ward beside Caledonia. Her freighter was loading generators, refrigeration equipment, medical pallets, and boxed water purification supplies. Her crew had worked through two nights. Forklifts moved between containers, their warning alarms echoing against the warehouse wall.

Ward was in her forties, with windburned cheeks and the calm impatience of a person who hated wasted time.

"Commander Vale," she said. "Are you going to tell me I'm overloaded?"

"Are you?"

"No."

"Then I won't."

She smiled. "That's the beginning of a good professional relationship."

Her chief mate joined us with a clipboard. The ship had a revised cargo manifest: surgical supplies, antibiotics, emergency heating systems, and portable generators destined for clinics operating on temporary power.

"Why are you still loading?" I asked.

"Because a hospital inland just asked for another generator," Ward said. "They have patients in the cold."

"Every hour you remain here makes the departure more difficult."

"I know."

She stopped smiling.

"I also know those patients don't have another ship coming."

There it was again: a command decision that had already become a moral question.

We negotiated the latest acceptable loading cutoff with the port authority. Ward did not get everything she wanted. Neither did I. She accepted it without resentment and sent the last forklift back toward the warehouse.

As I left, a young engineer came down the gangway carrying two mugs of coffee.

"This is Colin," Ward said. "His brother Aaron is aboard too. The pair have been arguing about whose watch got the worse weather since we left Scotland."

Colin offered me a mug.

"Sir."

"Thank you."

"Captain Ward says the Navy's coffee is dreadful."

"Eileen has no idea how hard we've worked to keep it that way."

Ward laughed. Colin grinned, then turned to help Aaron, who was struggling with a crate at the top of the gangway.

I watched the brothers work together. One would not reach Iceland. I could not have known that then. In memory I sometimes wish I could step back onto that pier, walk over, and tell them to go home. But I did not know what the future held, and even if I had, the hospital's supplies would still have been waiting.

Farther along the pier, Meridian's captain, Sofia Reed, inspected the tanker loading operation. We exchanged reports about safe departure and the weather. She was concerned about the northern route and the limitations of a loaded tanker in high seas. I promised I would not treat her ship like a destroyer.

"Good," she said. "Because she has no intention of behaving like one."

Atlas Mariner's master sent word through his chief officer that they were nearly ready. Every ship had its own problems and its own crew. My task was to make them into a convoy without pretending they had become warships.

When I returned to Northstar, Chief Ramirez was supervising an inventory at a repair locker. She looked up from her clipboard.

"How was the pier, Captain?"

"Busy."

"That's not what I asked."

I paused.

"There are a lot of frightened people over there."

She put down the clipboard.

"We'll do our jobs, sir."

"I know."

She watched me for a moment.

"There's something you should remember. Your crew can't carry all those families' fear as well as their own. We can protect them, but don't ask our sailors to promise what no one can guarantee."

I had come aboard determined to carry every life across the ocean. Ramirez was reminding me that duty required discipline, not omnipotence.

"You're right, Chief."

"I usually am."

She returned to the inventory.

That evening I stood on Northstar's bridge wing while the last civilians boarded Aurora. Harbor lights reflected on the black water. Somewhere beyond the breakwater, war was already moving through the sea lanes.

I looked at the ferry's windows and wondered which one belonged to Liv, the little girl with the red crayon.

Then I went below to review the departure orders.

Five days later, when the first torpedo warning came, I would remember that drawing before I remembered anything printed on the convoy manifest.
