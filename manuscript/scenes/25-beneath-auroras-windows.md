**BENEATH AURORA'S WINDOWS — 0642 HOURS**

At 0642, the convoy was still intact apart from Caledonia.

I had begun to hate the word *intact*. It suggested something whole, something undamaged. Aurora could still make speed. Atlas Mariner and Meridian remained afloat. Their engines were running, their captains were answering, and their crews were doing what we had asked.

But there was nothing whole about the morning.

I asked the bridge to connect me with Captain Lindholm.

When he answered, I heard children shouting somewhere behind him. Not screaming. Shouting in the unruly way children do when they are frightened and somebody tells them to remain quiet. The sound was almost unbearable.

"Peter, do you have a moment?"

"Commander, I'll give you two. I suspect that's all either of us possesses."

"How are your passengers?"

"Alive."

The word landed between us.

"And hurt," he added. "Some fell during the turns. The doctor says the injuries are manageable. We have panic in the lower lounge. People are demanding to know whether another missile is coming."

"I wish I could answer that."

"So do I."

I heard him turn away from the radio and speak sharply to someone in Norwegian. When he returned, his voice was quieter.

"There's a woman asking if we can put the children in the vehicle deck."

"Would that be safe?"

"No. It would be colder and create other problems. People want somewhere to hide because they don't believe the decks they're standing on will protect them."

"Do you have an interior room away from the windows?"

"A conference space. Small. We already have families there."

"Keep the movement controlled. Your ship's crew knows its escape arrangements. I won't give you interior instructions from a destroyer."

"Thank you," he said.

I had learned that a captain does not become more useful by pretending expertise in another captain's ship. I knew Northstar's compartments, her systems and her limitations. Lindholm knew Aurora. His job was to keep a civilian population safe inside an unfamiliar emergency. The best help I could give was honest information and the space to command.

"Peter, tell the passengers we're still with you. Tell them we defeated the most recent threats. Don't promise there are no more."

He repeated the instruction.

Then another voice came over the circuit.

"Commander Vale?"

A woman. Steady, tired, almost formal.

I looked toward the radio operator.

"Go ahead."

"My name is Ingrid Voss. I'm the volunteer teacher aboard Aurora. Captain Lindholm says you can hear me."

"Yes, Ingrid."

"I don't need you to solve anything. I just want to tell you what the children are doing."

I sat down.

"Please."

"We took the paper from the closed travel office. Ticket brochures. Old safety leaflets. The children are folding boats from them."

I thought of Liv, the little girl with the red crayon.

"How many boats?"

"Dozens. They keep sinking in the washbasin."

I smiled despite the ache in my face.

"We know something about that."

"They're naming them," she said. "Some after your ship. Some after the British one. A little boy made a ship with no name because he said the crew could choose it themselves."

I closed my eyes.

"Tell him I think that's a good idea."

She hesitated.

"Commander, there's another thing."

"Go ahead."

"When the missiles came, we turned the children away from the windows. Some thought it was thunder. Others had heard the alarms and knew better. One little girl asked whether the American sailors had mothers."

My throat tightened.

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her everybody came from somewhere."

I could hear children in the background singing a tune I did not know.

"Then she asked whether the sailors wanted to go home."

I stared at the deck.

"What did you say?"

"I said yes. I said that was why they were working so hard."

I wanted to say something intelligent. Something worthy of the strange little bond now reaching across miles of freezing sea.

Instead I answered, "Thank you."

"No, Commander. Thank you."

The circuit fell quiet for a moment.

Then I said, "Ingrid, please tell them that we have a ship full of people who would very much like to see what those paper boats look like when everybody reaches Iceland."

She laughed.

"I will."

Lindholm took back the radio.

"Daniel?"

"Yes?"

"I know you have threats to manage. But thank you for listening."

I looked at the tactical display, at the places where hostile weapons had disappeared and where the next ones might appear.

"I needed to hear them, Peter."

"So did I."

We ended the call.

Idris stood nearby, reviewing the ammunition inventory.

"You all right, Captain?"

"No."

He waited.

"There's a teacher aboard Aurora making paper boats with the children."

His expression changed.

"That's good."

"One asked whether our sailors had mothers."

He looked toward the deck.

"Mine's going to be furious with me if I get home and tell her what happened."

I managed a short laugh.

"Mine too."

Idris did not smile for long.

"Captain, we've used a great deal of ammunition."

"I know."

"And we cannot assume the attack is complete."

"I know that too."

He pointed to the available defensive assessment. I could see the numbers and the uncertainty ranges. They were the facts we had to live with.

"There's a temptation," he said, "to think we can prevent every loss if we choose the perfect engagement sequence."

"Can we?"

"No. We can defend with what we have. We can't promise perfection."

I looked at him.

"Thank you for saying it."

He seemed surprised.

"Aye, Captain."

I stood and approached the main display.

The picture showed Aurora slightly ahead of Atlas Mariner, with Meridian farther astern and Cutlass still occupied with the aftermath of the sinking. We had a damaged formation and a dangerous ocean. The next threat might arrive before any of us had recovered enough to think clearly.

I asked Idris for a concise status summary. No speculation. No assurances based on hope. Just what we could defend.

He gave it.

I then called the wardroom mess attendant and asked whether any food could be taken to the crews still working extended watches. She told me Chief Mendoza was already arranging it.

Of course he was.

There was a strange strength in realizing how many people were caring for one another without waiting for the captain's permission.

A messenger arrived with an updated weather report. The pressure was falling. The northern horizon remained empty. The bridge expected poor visibility to continue.

I walked toward the ship's internal camera feed and found the forward view.

Nothing but steel bow and violent gray water.

Somewhere beyond that image, in a bright passenger lounge with windows covered against the sight of war, eighty-four children were folding paper boats.

I thought about our vertical launch cells and our radar and our years of training. Every system aboard Northstar had a purpose, but none of them was an end in itself.

The paper boats were the purpose.

Those children had lost enough already. They should have been arguing over toys, not hiding from weapons in the middle of an ocean.

I picked up the command circuit and checked that the bridge, engineering and Combat had what they needed for the next hours.

Then I returned to my chair.

The ship rolled to starboard.

A sailor tightened the strap on his headset.

Nobody spoke for a few seconds.

I watched the convoy symbols move west and silently counted them again.

Three civilian ships.

Hundreds of lives.

One promise we had not yet broken.
