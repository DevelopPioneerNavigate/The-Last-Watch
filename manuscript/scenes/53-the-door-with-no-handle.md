**THE DOOR WITH NO HANDLE — 1108 HOURS**

The first report said there was fire in the machinery spaces.

The second said there was a sailor unaccounted for.

Neither report told me what I needed most: whether the two facts had anything to do with one another.

I stood behind the central display while Navarro's voice came over an internal circuit. He was speaking from a damage-control station, close enough to the smoke that every sentence seemed to have weight.

"Captain, we've isolated the affected equipment. Boundaries are being established. Access is restricted until we have a reliable picture of heat and atmosphere."

"Personnel accounting?"

"Working. Several names have been confirmed at alternate stations. One team hasn't reported through its expected point."

"Ramirez?"

"Her team is in the affected section. I haven't reached her directly."

I looked at Mara.

She was already speaking into another telephone, gathering information without competing with Navarro's authority over the engineering response.

"Don't ask them to move simply to satisfy our need for an answer," I told him. "Keep their safety checks in place."

"Aye, Captain."

The circuit closed.

I had seen fires during exercises. Training smoke smells wrong. It tells you that a lesson is underway. The odor coming through Northstar's ventilation now was hot insulation and something bitter that made the back of my throat tighten.

A sailor near the hatch to Combat tried to conceal a cough.

"Get that reported to damage control," I said. "If smoke is moving where we didn't expect it, we need to know."

He acknowledged and left.

Idris watched the tactical plot.

"Possible undersea threat still unresolved, sir."

"I know."

I had a damaged ship, civilians to defend and an enemy we could not see. Everything in me wanted to go below, through the passageways toward the noise, and stand beside the people fighting to keep Northstar alive.

But if I did, the ship would lose the one person who had to keep all three problems in view.

Mara returned to my side.

"Navarro's casualty organization is functioning. Cross is confirming personnel at the repair stations. The reports are late, not necessarily bad."

"There's a difference."

"Exactly."

For a moment I believed her tone meant the situation was improving.

Then someone in engineering reported a door whose manual handle had become too hot to touch.

The compartment number meant nothing to me until Bishop marked its location on a ship diagram. It was adjacent to the affected route. A sailor had reached it while checking a boundary and found the metal radiating heat.

"Any indication it's been opened?" Mara asked.

"Negative."

"Good. Keep it closed until the damage-control lead authorizes action."

I stared at the diagram. A rectangle labeled with a space name, a line showing an access route, a small symbol denoting an alarm. On paper the door was merely an object.

On the other side of it could be somebody I knew.

We had not established that. I refused to turn the possibility into a report.

The bridge called with an update from Aurora. Lindholm had seen our smoke and wanted to know whether Northstar could remain with the convoy. He had also asked whether his ferry should prepare to receive injured sailors.

I took the circuit.

"Peter, we're dealing with an internal fire. The ship remains under command. We are not requesting a transfer. Prepare your crew for any coordinated instruction, but don't attempt an approach."

"Can we help?"

"By keeping Aurora safe and moving."

"I don't like watching your ship burn."

"Neither do I."

There was a quiet moment.

Then Lindholm said, "Understood. We're still with you."

I put down the handset.

I wanted to believe those words meant more than they did. The ferry could not stop the flames or replace our damaged machinery. But knowing its captain would not turn confusion into a reckless maneuver was real help.

At 1111, a messenger came from one of the repair stations. His face was streaked with sweat and gray dust.

"Captain, Chief Ramirez's team was seen moving toward an alternate access point before communications became intermittent."

"Who saw them?"

"Petty Officer Calder."

"When?"

He checked the written note.

The time was imperfect. The watch clocks on different circuits had not all been synchronized after the casualty.

"Record the discrepancy," Mara said. "We'll reconstruct it later."

The messenger nodded.

I looked at him.

"Is Calder safe?"

"Yes, sir. At the station."

"Good."

The young sailor seemed surprised that I had asked.

"Anything else?"

He shook his head and hurried out.

The next minutes were made of fragments. One alarm was acknowledged. A repair team withdrew from a hot boundary. Navarro reported that a temporary electrical route was holding. The bridge gave me its latest safe-speed assessment.

At 1114 Cross arrived with an accounting board. There were names in pencil and others underlined in red.

"Captain, we're still checking the team in the affected section."

"Does the list show anyone confirmed missing?"

"Not yet. It shows people we haven't spoken to. Those are different things."

He held my eyes.

I knew he was repeating the lesson we'd tried to teach the crew all week: absence of information does not authorize a terrible conclusion.

"Keep checking," I said.

At 1116 the radio carried a voice I had been waiting to hear.

"Captain, Ramirez."

The sound was broken by static.

I closed my eyes for half a second.

"Chief. Tell me your situation."

And from the other side of a door whose handle had become too hot to touch, Elena Ramirez began telling us what her people needed.

I listened.

Every word mattered.

There would be time later to reconstruct the exact sequence, to compare clocks, to decide whether any report could have arrived faster.

For now, there was a living voice on the circuit and a ship full of people depending on us to answer it correctly.

I placed my hand flat on the console.

"Go ahead, Chief," I said.

"We're here."
