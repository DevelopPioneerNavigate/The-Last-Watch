**THE WATCHKEEPERS' BOOK — 1448 HOURS**

The notebook was red, no larger than a man's hand, and nobody could explain why it had survived the fire.

Petty Officer Calder brought it to Cross, who brought it to me. Its cover was stained, and the lower corner had been softened by water. Inside, written on lined pages, were the names of engineering watchstanders, remarks about equipment, reminders for people taking over, and a few jokes of such astonishing poor quality that I knew a sailor had written them.

Across the first page, in block letters, were the words:

*DON'T MAKE THE NEXT WATCH GUESS.*

I recognized Ramirez's handwriting.

"Where was this?" I asked.

"In a protected station," Cross answered. "A sailor found it when they were consolidating the department's papers. He thought you should see it."

I turned the pages.

One entry noted that Rios had remembered to report an intermittent indication without being prompted. Another reminded the next watch to check the same fitting twice in cold weather. On a third, Ramirez had written: *If Ochoa says the coffee is good, do not believe him.*

I smiled.

Then I came to the page dated the day before the attack.

*Practice again with the new team. Nobody gets left behind because somebody was embarrassed to ask for help.*

I looked at the words until they blurred.

Cross stood beside my desk.

"Are you all right, Captain?"

"No."

He nodded as if that were the expected answer.

"What do you want done with it?"

"Preserve it with her effects. Take photographs of the pages needed for our records. Don't let somebody turn it into a souvenir."

"Already told the department."

I closed the notebook.

"Good."

Cross didn't leave.

"There's something else."

I waited.

"The watch has started adding names to another book."

"What kind of names?"

"People they've checked on. Sailors who need rest. People who've had a hard time since the fire. They're not doing it for an official report."

"Who's managing it?"

"The chiefs, with medical helping when needed."

I considered that.

"Make sure private information stays where it belongs. We don't want a well-intended list becoming something the entire crew can read."

"Agreed."

He folded his arms.

"Ramirez used to keep track of the people others overlooked. They want to continue."

I looked at the notebook again.

"Then make sure they do it properly."

He smiled a little.

"She'd have said the same thing."

After Cross left, I tried to return to the combat reports. The tactical situation was still uncertain. Intelligence had asked for clarification of the earlier surface engagement. Engineering had sent an updated operating limitation. Aurora was reporting passenger fatigue. Cutlass was continuing its slow, difficult work of repairing herself while underway.

Everything demanded attention.

And still my mind kept returning to the little red book.

I had spent years learning to read an operational log as a source of evidence. Time, event, action, result. The entries were disciplined and impersonal for good reason. A ship needs a reliable record that can survive changing watches and tired memories.

Ramirez's book was different. It had almost none of the language a staff officer would expect. Yet it recorded the things that made our formal system work: the junior sailor whose confidence had improved, the machinery problem that somebody needed to remember, the friend who would notice if another friend skipped a meal.

I realized it might be the truest history of Northstar I'd ever seen.

At 1503 I asked the ship's administrative officer to come to my cabin.

Lieutenant Hannah Pierce arrived holding a folder and looking as though she expected a reprimand.

"Captain?"

"I need your advice."

She blinked.

"Sir?"

"Ramirez kept personal training notes. Some may be relevant to the formal casualty review. Some belong to her family. I want them handled carefully, without copying her private words into every document we send ashore."

Pierce relaxed into a problem she knew how to solve.

"We can establish which pages are necessary for a specific official purpose, record who has access, and preserve the original separately. We shouldn't assume every note belongs in the investigative file."

"Exactly."

"I'll coordinate with the proper offices and the family liaison."

"Thank you."

She made a note.

"One other issue, Captain. The crew keeps offering to sign letters. Some are addressed to the family, some to the Navy, some just to Chief Ramirez."

I looked at her.

"What are they asking us to do with the last kind?"

"Nobody knows. They seem to want somewhere to put the words."

I thought for a moment.

"Provide a secure place for voluntary notes. Explain who might eventually receive them, and don't promise privacy we can't maintain. Anything for the family should go through the liaison arrangements when appropriate."

Pierce nodded.

"We can do that."

After she left, I spent twenty minutes reading the accounts of the damage-control teams. Calder had remembered one sequence of events, another sailor remembered it slightly differently. The official clocks were not perfectly aligned. I felt the familiar temptation to make a single clean line out of all the reports.

Then I remembered Ramirez's first page.

*Don't make the next watch guess.*

The lesson wasn't to erase the uncertainty. It was to label it so the next officer would know where the real work began.

I made a note that the accounts required careful reconciliation.

Then I asked for another status of the surviving machinery.

Navarro answered from engineering.

"Within limits, Captain. We're still working."

"Thank you."

I hesitated.

"Luis, did you know about Ramirez's little red notebook?"

A pause.

"Of course."

"Why didn't anybody tell me?"

"Captain, every good chief keeps one. Hers was better than most."

He sounded tired.

"What was so different?"

"She wrote down what people needed to learn, not merely what they'd done wrong."

I thought about Rios, struggling with the feeling that surviving was some kind of failure.

"That sounds right."

Navarro cleared his throat.

"Captain, do you mind if I ask something?"

"Go ahead."

"At the memorial, can you talk about her being a teacher?"

"I intend to."

"Good."

He was silent.

"She trained half my people," he added. "Some of them don't even know how much they learned from her."

I stared at the notebook.

"Then we'll tell them."

That evening Cross returned. He'd spoken with the chiefs and the medical team. The rest arrangements were in motion. A letter collection had been prepared. The original notebook would be preserved.

"Anything else?" he asked.

I held up the little red book.

"One thing. I want every department head to make sure the next watch has a useful handover. Not a flood of paperwork. A real account of what has changed and what still needs attention."

Cross looked at me.

"Because of the chief?"

"Because she was right."

He nodded.

"That'll make the next watch happy."

"Probably not."

He smiled.

"Good. Then it's in her spirit."

I placed the notebook back inside its protective folder.

Outside, the ship rolled through the long, black water. Somewhere forward, people were checking equipment they had already checked and helping shipmates who did not want to admit they needed help.

The ocean would remain indifferent to every one of them.

The next watch did not have to be.
