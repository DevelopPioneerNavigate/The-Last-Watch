**WHEN HAWTHORNE ANSWERED — 20 NOVEMBER, 1221 HOURS**

The news arrived twenty minutes after Eileen Ward left my cabin.

I was trying to persuade myself to eat a bowl of soup when Alvarez appeared at the door. He carried a printed message and an expression so carefully neutral that I knew the contents mattered.

"Captain, an update from fleet operations."

"Hawthorne?"

He nodded.

I stood too quickly. The chair struck the desk.

"Tell me."

"Her captain is alive."

I stared at him.

"Confirmed?"

"Yes, sir. Verified medical and personnel report."

I took the paper.

Captain Matthew Hayes: wounded, stable, receiving treatment. Several members of Hawthorne's crew confirmed dead. Others under care. The ship had survived its damage, though repairs would be extensive.

I read the message again.

The words about Matthew remained in the same place.

*Alive.*

For two days I'd been holding the possibility of his death in a corner of my mind, afraid to look directly at it. Now the corner opened, and I found something almost more difficult than fear: relief so strong that it felt disloyal to the families whose messages would not carry that word.

"Thank you, Chief," I said.

Alvarez nodded.

"There's more. We have authorization to pass a short personal message through the established welfare channel. Nothing operational."

I looked up.

"Can we speak?"

"Not yet. Communications are limited, and medical doesn't want him handling unnecessary calls."

"Good. I don't want a conversation at the cost of his rest."

I sat down to write.

I had imagined the first words a hundred times while we were still at sea. *You scared me, you idiot.* *I told you I'd see you on the other side.* *You owe me dinner.*

Instead I wrote:

*Matthew. I am grateful you are alive. We reached Iceland. Take care of your people and yourself. Rebecca and Ethan send their love when I can tell them. Your friend, Daniel.*

I stared at the message.

Then I added:

*Your cheeseburger story is still terrible.*

I handed it to Alvarez.

He looked at the last line.

"Sir?"

"It's an old joke."

"Should I remove it?"

"Absolutely not."

He smiled and left.

I went onto the weather deck.

The harbor was busy. Volunteers carried boxes toward temporary warehouses. Meridian was waiting for the next stage of fuel-transfer coordination. Atlas Mariner's crew was making preparations to discharge shelter materials. Aurora's passengers were moving through a receiving system that seemed determined to account for every name without making the families feel like cargo.

The sight should have made me happy.

It did.

But beneath it was the image of Hawthorne, somewhere far away, damaged and surrounded by sailors doing their best to repair what war had broken.

Mara found me at the rail.

"You heard."

"Yes."

"Good news."

"Yes."

She stood beside me.

"You don't look convinced."

"I am. I just keep thinking about the ones who aren't coming home."

She followed my gaze to the harbor.

"Relief doesn't cancel grief."

"I know."

"Then you're allowed to feel both."

I looked at her.

"Every time I think I understand command, you say something that makes it complicated again."

"That's what you pay me for."

I smiled.

For a while we watched a group of children being guided from Aurora toward a waiting bus. One boy stopped to look back at Northstar. A volunteer crouched beside him, said something reassuring, and waited until he was ready to walk.

I wondered whether Matthew's daughters had heard the first accurate news yet.

"Can we verify that his family has been notified properly?" I asked.

"Through the authorized office, yes. I'll ask."

"Thank you."

She touched my arm briefly.

"Don't contact them separately until that's confirmed."

"I won't."

The warning was unnecessary, but I was grateful for it. A captain who survives does not give another captain permission to forget how fragile the notification process can be.

I returned to my cabin and opened a photograph from an earlier deployment. Matthew and I stood beside a barbecue grill, both wearing ridiculous aprons. Ethan was younger then, barely tall enough to hold the plate. Rebecca had taken the picture just before rain forced us indoors.

I remembered Matthew insisting that the burgers were supposed to be black on the outside.

"That's called flavor," he'd said.

Rebecca had called the fire department.

Not really. But she told the story that way whenever Matthew visited.

The memory made me laugh out loud.

At 1258 Cross came by with a personnel note.

"Captain, the crew's heard Hawthorne's skipper made it."

"Yes."

"They're happy."

"Good."

"Some of them were friends with the sailors who didn't."

My smile faded.

"I know."

He folded the note.

"What should I tell them?"

"Tell them the verified news. Don't promise every family has heard yet. And make sure the casualty support arrangements are carried out properly."

He nodded.

Then he looked at me.

"Are you going to visit him?"

"When medical says he's ready."

"Good."

Cross started to leave.

"Master Chief?"

He turned.

"Thank you for asking about everybody, not just the captain."

He shrugged.

"That's my job."

I thought of Ramirez's little red book.

Yes, it was.

That afternoon the reply arrived from Matthew.

It was very short, probably dictated because his hands were being used for something medical.

*Northstar made it. Knew you would. They took away my coffee. Send reinforcements. And tell Ethan I still make the better burgers.*

I laughed so hard I had to sit down.

Then I read the casualty summary again.

I would not turn Hawthorne's survival into a clean victory. There were names still being reconciled, men and women whose families had a lifetime of mourning ahead, and a wounded ship waiting for repair.

But Matthew had answered.

For a moment, that was enough.

I folded his reply and placed it beside Rebecca's first letter from the crossing.

Two small pieces of paper.

One from home.

One from a friend.

Both reminders that every name on a situation board belonged to a life much larger than the symbol beside it.

I closed the folder and returned to the reports.

The next convoy would sail.

Hawthorne would be repaired, if her engineers and the Navy could make her safe.

Northstar would need work of her own.

And when Matthew was well enough, I intended to serve him the worst hamburger he'd ever eaten.

It would be the most welcome argument of my life.
