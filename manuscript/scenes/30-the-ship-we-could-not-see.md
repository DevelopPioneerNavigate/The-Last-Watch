**THE SHIP WE COULD NOT SEE — 1036 HOURS**

After we fired, I expected the picture to become simpler.

For a few minutes, it did.

The enemy group was no longer moving in the same coordinated fashion. One hostile contact's emissions disappeared. Another began to maneuver away from its previous course. The reports were consistent with disruption, which had been our immediate purpose.

Then a new symbol appeared near the northern edge of the plot.

It was slow.

Too slow to fit the picture we had built around the hostile combatants. It did not appear on the last report. It carried no reliable identification.

I stood beside Idris.

"What is that?"

"Unknown surface contact. We may have had it inside the weather clutter before. Or it may have just entered the area."

"Hostile?"

"Not classified."

"Any association with the group we engaged?"

"Not enough to say."

I studied the display. The contact was no more than a small mark. Its size invited certainty. The ocean behind it did not.

"Get me what you can. Passive and independent sources first."

"Aye."

A communications operator raised a hand.

"Captain, an intermittent civilian transmission has been heard on a maritime frequency. The sender claims to be a coastal cargo vessel with damaged equipment."

"Position?"

"Uncertain. Could be related to the new contact. We cannot establish that yet."

I felt a cold sensation behind my ribs.

A merchant ship might be moving near the hostile group.

Or a hostile vessel might be using a civilian radio.

Or the two reports might describe entirely different things.

The information did not support choosing the explanation that happened to be easiest to fight.

Idris watched me.

"We can continue evaluation, Captain. No further engagement recommended against that contact."

"Agreed."

He passed the decision to the watch team.

Mara joined us.

"What's the concern?"

"New unidentified surface vessel. Possible merchant communications in the region. We haven't established any relationship."

"Could be deception."

"Could be."

"Could be innocent."

"Yes."

She looked at the display and said, "Then we must act on what we know."

That sounded painfully simple. Perhaps everything worth remembering eventually did.

A few minutes later the contact turned and became less distinct. A second transmission from the apparent merchant station began, then broke off. The words that reached us were ordinary enough to be unsettling: a request for medical assistance and a problem with a generator.

I remembered Caledonia's lost cargo, and the hospitals still waiting for electricity on shore.

What if the ship out there was simply another freighter, trying to cross an ocean that had become a battlefield without asking permission?

I thought of Ward and her crew.

I thought of Colin Pike.

I thought of the brother whose pancakes would never improve.

"Maintain the observation," I said. "Record what we can. Do not identify it by imagination."

Idris nodded.

There was a time early in my career when I believed confidence in command meant presenting a decision without hesitation. A captain should look at an uncertain situation and know.

I had learned otherwise.

Confidence is not the absence of uncertainty. It is knowing which uncertainties can be tolerated, which require action, and which must never be concealed.

The air-defense team continued reviewing our remaining capabilities. The bridge maintained a safe position relative to the convoy. Engineering reported stable propulsion at that moment. Cutlass remained damaged but afloat.

The war had not become kind.

It had simply offered us a few minutes in which no weapon was heading toward the ferry.

Mara lowered her voice.

"Daniel, can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"When you authorized the strike, did you imagine the other captain?"

I stared at the display.

"Yes."

"What did you imagine?"

I took my time answering.

"That he was probably tired. That he had an executive officer telling him things he didn't want to hear. That he knew the names of his sailors."

She nodded.

"I imagined a young ensign on the bridge," I continued. "Somebody trying to prove he could stand a watch."

Mara looked down.

"That's who I always picture when we do this."

We stood together in the blue light of the consoles.

There are things two officers can say to one another that sound disloyal when repeated outside a ship. In truth, they are part of remaining decent while performing indecently difficult duties. We had authorized an attack for a legitimate defensive purpose. That did not require us to celebrate the possibility that another crew was dying.

"Do you regret the decision?" she asked.

"I don't know yet. I believe it was necessary. That's not the same as knowing every consequence."

She looked at me.

"That's an important difference."

I rubbed my forehead.

"At the Academy, we studied commanders who made terrible decisions because they couldn't admit a mistaken assumption."

"Yes."

"I used to think those men were fools."

"And now?"

"Now I wonder how many of them were just exhausted and frightened and surrounded by people who wanted certainty."

Mara did not answer immediately.

"That's why we have to challenge one another," she said.

"I know."

"Even when you're tired of me?"

"Especially then."

She smiled slightly.

Farouk reported that the uncertain merchant transmission remained unverified. We preserved it. A later investigation might explain it. Or it might become another fragment of the war never fitted to a complete picture.

I resisted the urge to ask for a definitive answer again.

Then Alvarez passed me an operational report from elsewhere in the theater. A separate Allied group had experienced heavy interference and was trying to establish the condition of several vessels. It offered no clean numbers, only the suggestion that the fighting was far broader than our little patch of sea.

I read it slowly.

Somewhere beyond our horizon, captains I had never met were making choices like mine. They were guarding ships, losing sailors, struggling with bad information, and reporting what they could through disrupted channels.

The Northern War did not consist of one great battle.

It consisted of hundreds of rooms like ours.

Hundreds of voices speaking through static.

Hundreds of commanders deciding what they could justify and what they could not.

I handed the report back to Alvarez.

"Preserve it with the wider situation log. Make sure the next watch knows which details are unconfirmed."

"Yes, Captain."

At 1044, the new surface contact faded.

I did not mark it destroyed.

I did not mark it hostile.

I did not remove it from the record.

I marked it unknown.

That was the truth.

Four minutes later, sonar reported a fast underwater contact from the south.

The next emergency began before I had finished thinking about the last.
