**LETTERS NEVER SENT — 1335 HOURS**

The ceremony for Ramirez began long before anyone assembled.

It began with people trying to decide what could be said.

I sat at a table in the wardroom with a sheet of paper in front of me and a pen that had stopped writing twice. On the page were words arranged into sentences that seemed appropriate for the death of a sailor: service, courage, sacrifice, dedication.

They were true words.

They were not enough.

Across the room, Mara was reviewing the next set of casualty notifications and family liaison arrangements. Neither of us wanted the job, and neither of us would have handed it to someone else.

"Have we confirmed the family's official contact arrangements?" I asked.

"Yes. The proper notification chain is moving. We shouldn't contact them outside that process until it's confirmed."

"Good."

She looked at the page in front of me.

"Memorial remarks?"

"Attempts."

"You've written a lot of these."

"Too many."

She pulled up a chair.

"Tell them about the chief instead of the citation."

I stared at the pen.

"Which part?"

"The part that makes her Elena."

I thought of the galley table, the photograph of Sofia and her purple bicycle, the way Ramirez had laughed at Ochoa. I thought of her irritating habit of correcting my damage-control terminology when she knew perfectly well what I meant. I thought of the afternoon she had spent three hours helping a junior sailor prepare for a qualification he was convinced he would fail.

"She never pretended somebody understood something when they didn't," I said.

Mara smiled.

"That's the chief."

"She could be terrifying."

"Only if you were careless."

I looked down.

"She was kind, Mara. The real kind. The kind that asks more of you because it believes you can give more."

Mara said nothing.

I wrote that sentence on the paper.

A knock came at the door.

Cross entered carrying a small envelope.

"The engineering department asked me to bring this, Captain."

Inside was a card signed by sailors who had served with Ramirez. The signatures ran onto the back because there had not been enough room. Beneath them somebody had drawn a purple bicycle.

I had to set it down.

"Who drew this?"

"Rios, sir. Had to hold the pen between his bandaged fingers."

I swallowed.

"Make sure the family gets it."

"They will."

Cross remained standing.

"One more thing, Captain. The crew wants to help with the service. Not just attend it."

"What do they have in mind?"

"One of the musicians wants to play. Her division wants to speak. The chaplain has the formal parts. They'd like the family to know what kind of chief she was."

"Let them."

He nodded.

"Captain, there's no perfect way to do this."

"I know."

"There isn't supposed to be."

After he left, Mara looked at me.

"You need something to eat."

"Everyone seems to have formed a committee about my lunch."

"We did. Passed unanimously."

I laughed, but it hurt.

Later I went to the hangar, where several sailors from the damage-control parties were quietly arranging chairs. One of them was still wearing the same boots as during the fire, the leather stained in places that no amount of cleaning would remove.

I asked whether the boots had been inspected for safety.

"Yes, sir."

"Then get a fresh pair from supply if you can."

The sailor glanced down.

"These were the ones I wore when we got Rios out."

"I know."

He looked up. "I don't want to throw them away."

"You don't have to. But you don't have to wear that night forever either."

He nodded slowly.

It occurred to me that I was telling him something I had not yet learned to do.

We walked past the aircraft bay where Raven One sat secured. Brooks stood beside the helicopter, reading a maintenance report. She looked up.

"Captain, the crew wants to fly again as soon as possible."

"Not until the aircraft and people are ready."

"Understood."

She hesitated.

"Could I ask something?"

"Of course."

"At the memorial, could you mention that Ramirez helped our detachment too? Not during the fire. Before. She was the first chief who came down when we couldn't get a repair locker to understand what we needed."

"I didn't know that."

"She never told you?"

"No."

"That sounds like her."

I added another line to my notes.

By the time the service began, my paper contained fragments: the bicycle, the qualification, the help nobody knew about, the smoke, the name of a child who would not see her mother come home.

Then I folded it and put it away.

The truth was that I did not need a perfect speech.

I needed to stand in front of the people who loved Ramirez and refuse to let the military vocabulary hide the human being we had lost.
