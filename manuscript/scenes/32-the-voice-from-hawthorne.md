**THE VOICE FROM HAWTHORNE — 0756 HOURS**

The news about Hawthorne arrived in the form of twelve words.

*Severe damage reported. Personnel casualties. Ship status presently unconfirmed.*

The next line named her commanding officer.

Captain Matthew Hayes.

I had known Matthew since we were twenty. At the Academy he had been the person who could make a disciplinary inspection feel like an absurd theatrical production. He once appeared at a formal dinner with his uniform pressed so sharply it looked dangerous, then sat down and whispered that he had hidden a cheeseburger in his pocket because the food smelled suspicious.

He was a good sailor. I had trusted him with my life during exercises, with my wife and son's hospitality during shore assignments, and with things I would never have said to another officer. He had been the best man at my wedding.

Now somewhere in the North Atlantic his ship was damaged and people were dead.

I read the message again.

"Who verified this?" I asked Alvarez.

"Fleet operations is still reconciling separate reports, Captain. The damage indication is credible. Numbers aren't confirmed."

"Any direct circuit?"

"Not at present."

"Can we request one?"

"We already have."

I looked at him.

"Thank you."

My first impulse was to order Northstar toward Hawthorne. We were both American warships, operating in the same conflict. Matthew's crew needed help. Mine knew the crew. Some had served together only last year.

Then I looked at Aurora.

The ferry moved steadily west, its windows pale against the morning gloom. Behind it came Atlas Mariner and Meridian. The escorts' mission had not changed because I recognized a name in an emergency report.

That was one of the cruelties of command. Private knowledge could make a decision hurt more, but it could not be allowed to make innocent people worth less.

Mara joined me without being asked.

"Matthew?"

I nodded.

"Nothing confirmed about him."

She stood beside me.

She had met his family at our house the previous summer. His youngest daughter had insisted on putting a plastic crown on Mara's head. Mara had worn it through dessert and later demanded that everyone address her as Admiral Princess.

"Have you requested information?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then let's keep going."

I turned to her.

"You make it sound easy."

"It isn't."

"How can you stand there and say it like that?"

She met my eyes.

"Because if I say it the way I feel it, we won't get through the next hour."

I looked away.

I had not realized how much she was carrying too.

We returned to Combat. Idris was reviewing the latest threat estimates, careful to separate older intelligence from the reports still arriving. The picture suggested our own fight was part of a larger pressure campaign against shipping and naval forces across the northern approaches. But beyond a scattering of confirmed engagements, the extent remained unknown.

"Captain," he said, "if the enemy is pushing other escorts at the same time, relief may be harder to obtain than headquarters expects."

"Understood."

"That has implications for our ammunition and damaged-ship plans."

"Present the options. We will not assume support can arrive simply because the network says someone intends to send it."

Idris nodded.

On a wall screen a chart showed the Norwegian Sea, Iceland, Greenland, and broad areas of the Atlantic. Each symbol stood for something enormous: a naval unit, a merchant group, a search area, a threat estimate. The distances were so great that our convoy looked insignificant.

Yet a symbol near the southwest edge represented Hawthorne.

I knew that behind it were officers who complained about the same coffee we did, engineers who kept imperfect machines running, and a captain who could turn anything into a joke.

I thought of his daughters.

What would their mother tell them if he did not come home?

When I was younger, I believed the hardest part of war would be watching enemies attack. I had not understood what it would mean to fight while unable to help friends who were suffering elsewhere.

A junior operations specialist interrupted.

"Captain, an additional incomplete report. One escort group requests medical evacuation capability. They do not identify their ship."

"Do not connect that to Hawthorne without evidence."

"Aye."

I admired the fact that he had asked. It meant our habit of treating uncertainty honestly was still working.

At 0805, Cross came to me with a small, folded sheet of paper.

"Sir, the crew has heard about Hawthorne."

"How?"

He lifted one eyebrow.

"It's a ship."

"Of course."

"Several sailors want to know whether we should start a collection for the families. They know people aboard her."

I looked at the paper. It contained the names of a dozen crew members willing to contribute money, letters, or whatever the eventual survivors might need.

"They're ahead of us."

"They usually are."

"Tell them we'll organize support when we have verified information. The Navy's official family assistance procedures come first. I don't want rumors reaching anyone's home."

"Agreed."

He stood for a moment.

"Captain, is Matthew alive?"

I had never heard Cross use a fellow captain's first name so naturally.

"I don't know."

Cross nodded.

"Then I'll tell them we don't know."

He left.

I stared at the chart again.

A few minutes later, we received a brief operational note from an Allied command. Several warships across the region had been engaged, and an effort was underway to reorganize the defensive coverage of maritime routes. It described no decisive victory. It offered no assurance that the next convoy would have enough escorts.

I read it and thought about what we had sailed into.

The Northern War was not a clean line of opposing fleets, one moving east and one west. It was hundreds of ships and aircraft and people scattered over distances larger than any commanding officer could grasp. Every small piece of knowledge came late. Every loss became part of a report that reached someone else too late to change it.

I understood, in a way I hadn't the day before, that I could do my job well and still know almost nothing about the battle around me.

At 0818, Alvarez informed me that headquarters had received our request for Hawthorne's status. No reply had been provided.

"Keep the request active," I said.

"Aye, Captain."

I looked toward the bridge feed.

The sea had no sympathy. Our convoy continued through the waves with the dull persistence of machines carrying people who hoped to live.

I took the photograph Matthew had sent before deployment from the inside pocket of my notebook. He stood with his wife and daughters in front of a flower garden. He was smiling broadly, one daughter leaning against each leg.

Underneath, he had written, *See you on the other side, brother.*

I studied the words.

I had assumed he meant the end of deployment.

Now I wasn't sure what they meant.

I slipped the picture back into the notebook and returned to the tactical display.

"Idris, update me on our own situation."

He began speaking.

I listened closely.

Somewhere else in the Atlantic, a captain I loved was fighting to get his crew home.

The only help I could offer him was not to fail mine.
