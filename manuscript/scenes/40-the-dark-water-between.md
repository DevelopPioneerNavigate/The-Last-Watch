**THE DARK WATER BETWEEN — 0321 HOURS**

There are moments at sea when the distance between two ships can seem less substantial than the darkness separating them.

At 0321, Caledonia lay on our port quarter, her lights moving gently against the black water. I could see her only when the bridge camera caught the slope of a swell or when a burst of white foam outlined her bow. The freighter was moving steadily, doing everything we'd asked.

From Combat, Chief Bell reported that the weak acoustic contact had reappeared.

"Same bearing?" I asked.

"Similar. Not identical."

"Confidence?"

"Enough to continue tracking. Not enough to connect it to the previous event."

Bell's voice had the calmness of a woman who refused to make the ocean more convenient than it was.

"Can we improve it?"

"We're looking. Priya thinks she may be hearing a second component, but we're checking whether it's the same source."

I walked to the sonar station.

Seaman Shah had one hand against her headset, the other moving over a collection of notes. She was young enough that I could still see the effort of making her voice sound professional when a captain appeared behind her.

"What have you got, Priya?"

She looked at Bell first.

"Tell him," Bell said.

"There are two patterns that don't quite agree, sir. One is moving the way I'd expect from the track we've been working. The other could be a separate source. Or it could be propagation."

"Meaning?"

"The sound may be reaching us by different paths through the water. If I call it two submarines and it's only one, we're wrong. If I call it one and there are two, we're wrong in a different way."

I pulled up a chair.

"What makes you think it might be separate?"

She showed me the time sequence, explaining where a repeated tone appeared and why it did not fit neatly with the movement we'd estimated. Her explanation was careful. She admitted two observations were weak. She declined to draw a line through the gaps.

"Is that enough to report?"

"Yes, sir. As uncertain."

"Then report it."

She looked surprised.

"I don't want to give you a false picture."

"You haven't. You told me what you know, what you suspect, and what you can't establish. That's the picture I need."

Bell nodded almost imperceptibly.

For a few minutes we watched the weak information move across screens without settling into a single answer. The sea had layers. Sound bent through them, arrived late, vanished and returned. Every ship in the convoy added its own machinery noise, and every wave seemed determined to bury something important.

A report arrived from the bridge.

"Captain, Aurora requests a minor speed adjustment. They're having difficulty maintaining their position in the swell."

"How much?"

Mercer gave me the requested change.

"Coordinate with navigation and the other merchants. We cannot let one ship solve her problem by creating a collision risk for another."

"Aye."

I turned back to Bell.

"Could an altered convoy speed help you distinguish the sounds?"

"It might change the background enough to give us a different picture. It might also make matters worse."

"Then we won't maneuver the civilians to satisfy an uncertain acoustic theory."

Bell nodded.

That was the sort of decision that never appears dramatic in the final histories. No missile launched. No alarm sounded. A captain simply refused to make one danger worse in pursuit of information about another.

Farouk arrived with a message suggesting hostile submarine activity somewhere east of our projected track. She was already checking its origin.

"How old is the observation?" I asked.

"Possibly several hours."

"Does it come from an independent source?"

"That's not established yet."

"Then don't add it as confirmation of Bell's contact."

"Understood."

The crew moved around us with deliberate economy. One operator reviewed an alternate communications channel. Another checked the status of the merchant vessels. Idris stood near the weapons console without turning uncertainty into an unauthorized engagement.

I thought of Caledonia, carrying generators and medicine, and of her captain, Eileen Ward, who had refused to leave the pier before the last supplies were aboard.

Her ship could not hear what Bell was listening for.

Even if Ward had the same information, she could not turn her freighter like Northstar. She had a deep-loaded hull, a crew that knew cargo better than weapons, and a schedule that mattered to hospitals waiting ashore.

I asked Alvarez for a private circuit to Caledonia.

Ward answered after several seconds.

"Northstar, Caledonia. What can I do for you?"

"Eileen, I wanted your latest steering and machinery status."

"Steady enough. Engineering is watching a vibration. Nothing we can't manage."

"Any problems with visibility from your bridge?"

"Rain and black water. Same as you, I expect."

"Exactly."

She paused.

"You're worried about something."

"Sonar is examining an uncertain contact. No instruction to alter course yet. I need you ready to respond to a coordinated maneuver, but I don't want your crew guessing at what we're seeing."

"I appreciate that."

She lowered her voice.

"One of my deckhands says he heard a strange noise through the hull. I told him a freighter makes all sorts of strange noises and sent the chief engineer to inspect."

"Good decision. If anything changes, call immediately."

"Daniel?"

"Yes?"

"Do you think we're going to get there?"

I looked at the audio status on Bell's screen.

"I think we're still capable of getting there."

"That's a captain's answer."

"It's all I've got."

"Then I'll keep mine steady."

We ended the call.

I looked toward the bridge display where Caledonia's position was marked. The civilian ships formed an imperfect procession through open water. There was enough space between them to maneuver safely, and enough ocean surrounding them to conceal almost anything.

"Captain," Bell said.

Her voice had changed, not louder but narrower.

"Go ahead."

"We're getting a stronger tone from the southeastern bearing. Separate from the background component Priya flagged."

"Can you classify it?"

"Possible submarine machinery. Not yet enough for certainty."

I studied Idris.

"Keep the watch prepared."

He nodded.

I knew what readiness meant. It meant the operator could carry out a justified decision without losing precious seconds. It did not mean the threat had become certain merely because we were prepared for it.

At 0336 the contact weakened again.

Shah looked frustrated.

"Lost it," she said.

Bell placed a hand briefly on her shoulder.

"No. You lost the signal. That's different from knowing the contact is gone."

I watched Shah absorb the distinction.

"I understand, Chief."

"Then keep listening."

At 0342, Mercer called from the bridge.

"Captain, we're approaching the next scheduled course-control review."

"Keep the convoy on the coordinated plan. Confirm every merchant has the same instructions."

"Aye."

I remained at the sonar station.

I could not shake the idea that two things were moving beneath the sea. It might have been a mistaken reading of one source. It might have been the ocean playing its usual tricks. Or it might have meant someone had divided our attention before we'd even understood the game.

Bell refused to tell me which.

I was grateful.

At 0350, the aviation circuit came alive. Raven One was preparing to report from its assigned search area.

I looked at the helicopter symbol, far from the ships we were escorting, and then back at Caledonia.

In peacetime, a navigator draws a line between two points and calls it a route.

In war, a route is the space through which everyone you have promised to protect must pass, whether you know what's waiting there or not.

I watched the freighter's lights rise and fall in the darkness.

"We'll keep listening," Bell said.

I nodded.

The gap between what we heard and what we knew remained large enough to hide a submarine.

Perhaps two.

And there was not a thing I could do to make the water tell the truth before it was ready.
