**THE DISTANCE BETWEEN SHIPS — 0436 HOURS**

When a ship is sinking close enough to see but too far away to reach, distance becomes a physical pain.

Cutlass was working near Caledonia's wrecked stern. The British crews were hauling soaked people from the water into rescue boats that seemed too small for the ocean around them. Northstar held her protective position near the remaining merchants, and I had never hated a correct decision so much.

I called the ferry.

"Peter, I need to explain the situation."

"I saw the explosion," Lindholm answered.

"One of our cargo ships has been hit. Cutlass is conducting rescue. We'll remain with you."

"Should I tell the passengers?"

"Tell them there has been an incident and that we're responding. Don't speculate about the cause or casualties."

He sighed. "There is no way to keep this quiet. People were looking out the windows."

"Then let them know what we're doing and ask them to help one another stay calm."

There was a voice behind him, a woman speaking Norwegian quickly.

Lindholm returned. "A nurse aboard the ferry is asking if there may be survivors transferred to us."

"Possibly, but that will be coordinated if needed. Keep your medical space ready."

"Understood."

The human consequences of Caledonia's attack spilled across the radio. A sailor rescued by Cutlass kept calling for his brother, who had been on a different watch. Another survivor knew he had seen somebody jump into the sea but could not remember from which side of the ship. Ward, still aboard, reported that water had reached a compartment containing emergency stores.

Mallory transmitted a concise rescue assessment.

"We've got survivors coming in, but we're fighting the weather. I can attempt a further search near the stern before she goes under."

"Your boats' safety?"

"A concern. My boat officer knows when to break off."

"Edward, don't let one rescue become six deaths."

"I won't. But we're not leaving while people are visibly alive."

He had said what I would have said in his place. The important difference was that he was the captain who could act.

I kept glancing toward the sonar display. A submarine might still be inside the sea noise. Another torpedo could arrive while our attention was fixed on the rescue. That possibility made every voice from Caledonia harder to hear.

Alvarez patched a garbled call from Ward into the command circuit. She was trying to confirm the condition of her engine-room team. The names came through poorly; the ship's electrical damage was compromising her internal communications.

"Daniel, I've got men who went back for a shutoff," she said. "The access is flooded. I can't get an answer."

"What are your surviving watertight boundaries doing?"

"Some are holding. Some aren't."

"Can they be reached safely?"

"Not from the route we used."

I wanted to offer a clever solution. A route nobody had thought of. A device our destroyer could send. A practical miracle.

But I did not know her ship's internal condition, and pretending otherwise would be worse than silence.

"Eileen, don't send more people into a route you know is lost."

She drew a shaky breath.

"I know."

The sound of a ship sinking is not always the sound of an explosion. Often it is the absence of ordinary reports. The engine stops. Pumps lose power. A radio goes silent. Someone who should answer does not. An enormous steel vessel becomes less and less able to speak for itself.

From our bridge camera, Caledonia looked wounded rather than dying. There was a deceptive orderliness to the few lights still burning. I wondered whether it was better or worse for the crew to see their ship from the water.

Cross joined me at the rear of CIC.

"The men are asking whether we're going to help with the recovery," he said.

"Cutlass is doing it."

"Yes, sir. They know that. They want to know why we can't."

"Because Aurora and the others need protection."

"I'll tell them."

"Tell them it hurts me too."

He nodded.

A few minutes later, I received an internal message from the junior officers' mess. Several sailors had volunteered to stand additional watches so rescue-qualified crew could remain available if called upon. They wanted to do something. Anything. I approved the sensible adjustments and declined the ones that would have increased fatigue without improving a rescue.

The hardest part of command is not refusing to help. It is having to refuse a form of help that feels noble but would make everything worse.

After Caledonia vanished, Cutlass continued searching the wreck area as conditions permitted. A life raft emerged from the foam, empty. A floating toolbox bumped against a rescue boat. A torn orange suit drifted past a searchlight and made everyone believe for a moment that they had found a person.

Then a survivor appeared, barely responsive. The boat crew pulled him aboard. He was hypothermic and had stopped asking questions. He kept trying to open his eyes.

The radio report was almost unbearably simple: "One more alive."

I repeated it to the bridge.

"One more alive," Mercer acknowledged.

I looked toward Aurora's lights.

One more alive was not enough. It was also everything.

When the final confirmed count arrived, I asked the communications chief to preserve the distress traffic. The future investigation would need it. Families would need it. Historians might one day ask whether the escorts had done enough. I wanted the record to show what people had actually tried.

Later, when Caledonia became a dot on a casualty summary, I would remember that morning's individual voices. Ward asking about her engine-room watch. Mallory refusing to turn away while anyone was alive. The rescued sailor calling for his brother. Cross asking what to tell the crew.

The ocean did not turn them into statistics. We did, because we needed to continue operating.

I promised myself I would turn them back into names when there was time.
