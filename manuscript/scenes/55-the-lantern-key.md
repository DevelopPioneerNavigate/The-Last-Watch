**THE LANTERN KEY — 19 NOVEMBER, 1226 HOURS**

The merchant captain who called us did not give a name.

"Northstar, commercial vessel requesting route assistance. We have people aboard and our navigation picture is incomplete."

Alvarez held a hand over his headset.

"Captain, the transmission is on a maritime channel. We haven't established the sender's identity."

I looked at the chart.

The voice appeared to come from well outside our immediate formation. We had no confirmed ship at the reported position, though that meant little in such weather.

"Do not pass our location," I said. "Ask for identifying details and an independent means of verification."

Alvarez nodded and returned to the circuit.

The reply came in fragments. A coastal vessel operating near a northern transit corridor. Mechanical trouble. Civilians aboard. A request for an escort route that would take it west.

The words were plausible.

They were also exactly the kind of story that might persuade a tired watch team to reveal information it should have protected.

Mara stood beside me.

"Could it be genuine?"

"Yes."

"Could it be a lure?"

"Yes."

"Then we're going to have a long conversation."

We were.

I asked Farouk to evaluate the call while Bishop checked the navigation implications. The voice had provided a partial description and a destination, but the reported equipment failure made some of its position information unreliable. No trusted source yet confirmed a ship there.

"Captain," Bishop said, "if we gave them a route from the position they claim, we could be sending an innocent vessel toward hazards we haven't checked."

"Then don't give a route based on an unverified origin."

Farouk moved closer.

"There's another problem. They're asking for a current passage instruction. If we send them our own planned course, they could learn more about LANTERN than the information is worth."

"Agreed."

Idris looked over the tactical display.

"We have possible hostile activity in the broader area. I recommend we keep the convoy on its assigned course and use the approved maritime rescue coordination system for the caller."

"Do that."

It was a small decision. None of the missiles on our displays moved because of it. No weapon was fired. Yet my heart was beating harder than during some of the actual engagements.

I remembered Caledonia sinking. I remembered the uncertainty in the first distress transmissions. It was possible that a genuine crew was calling for help and we were the nearest people capable of hearing them.

But I also knew that giving a stranger an unverified route could endanger them and every ship behind us.

I took the radio.

"Unidentified vessel, Northstar. We have your report. We cannot verify your position or provide a safe route from the information supplied. We are passing your request through the appropriate rescue channels. Please continue transmitting your identity, position information and any immediate danger to life. Do not assume your present position is confirmed."

There was a pause.

Then a man's voice answered.

"Northstar, do you believe us?"

I looked at the handset.

"That isn't the question. The question is how to help you safely."

He said nothing for several seconds.

Then: "There are children aboard."

I thought of Liv.

"I'm listening," I said.

The voice came back.

"We have twelve passengers. Two are sick. We cannot keep the radar operating. We need a place to go."

I asked whether they were in immediate danger of sinking or required urgent medical evacuation.

The response was broken. Something about an engine running hot. A sailor who had been injured in bad weather.

I relayed the information to the coordination watch and asked for an appropriate medical consultation through available channels.

No one aboard Northstar was going to diagnose a child on the basis of a frightened stranger's radio report. No one was going to ignore the call either.

Farouk's team found a possible match in delayed civilian traffic: a small registered supply vessel that had departed before the latest regional shipping restrictions. The name and description resembled the caller's account, but the transmission itself remained unverified.

"This may be our vessel," she said.

"May?"

"May. I don't have independent confirmation."

"Keep working."

Mara watched the weather display.

"Even if it is genuine, can we reach them?"

"Not without making a major change to the mission and risking the merchants."

"We should be clear about that."

"I know."

At 1244, a friendly maritime coordination authority acknowledged receiving the distress traffic and began trying to establish direct contact. It had access to information we did not. The caller was advised to remain on the established emergency channel and report changes in the vessel's condition.

We did not become the rescue commander simply because we had heard the first transmission.

The distinction mattered.

At 1253 the caller returned.

"Northstar, we have heard another station. They say they will help us determine a safe harbor."

"Good," I answered.

A moment passed.

"Are you still there?"

"Yes."

"Will you stay on the channel?"

I looked around Combat. The convoy continued west. A repair report needed attention. Cutlass was limping through the swell. The threat picture remained uncertain.

"I cannot promise continuous communication," I said. "But your call has been received and transferred to people who can coordinate the assistance you need."

The man was quiet.

Then: "Thank you for not ignoring us."

I closed my eyes.

"You're welcome."

We handed off the information, recorded the uncertainty, and returned our attention to LANTERN. A later verified update indicated that the small vessel had established contact with a coastal service and was proceeding under its guidance. We did not receive enough detail to claim a rescue or an arrival.

I marked the matter unresolved in Northstar's log.

Mara read the entry.

"Does that bother you?"

"Yes."

"Because you don't know the ending?"

"Yes."

She nodded.

I thought of the many people in war who never receive a final report. Families who wait months for confirmation. Crews whose distress calls are passed from one office to another while the weather changes. Captains forced to choose between competing obligations.

The ocean had never promised neat endings.

But we could promise not to make them up.

At 1308 Alvarez delivered an updated list of communications channels expected to remain reliable through the afternoon. He had marked which ones were degraded, which had been verified recently, and which could not be trusted without another check.

"Good work," I told him.

He looked at the small note next to the unidentified caller's entry.

"Captain, we still don't know whether they reached port."

"No."

"Should we keep asking?"

"Through the appropriate office, when it doesn't distract from an immediate need. And make sure the next watch knows they might receive another call."

He nodded.

I looked at the title on the convoy folder.

*LANTERN.*

We had called the mission that because it was supposed to help people find their way through darkness.

A lantern does not turn night into day. It gives enough light for the next safe step.

On that afternoon, it meant listening to a stranger without surrendering the safety of the people already in our care.

I hoped it would be enough.
