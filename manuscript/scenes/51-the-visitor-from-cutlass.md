**THE VISITOR FROM CUTLASS — SEVEN MONTHS AFTER ICELAND**

Edward Mallory arrived in Norfolk carrying a walking stick and a box of tea.

Neither suited him.

The walking stick was dark wood with a silver handle. The tea came in a cardboard box printed with the sort of dignified lettering that suggested the contents had survived at least three empires.

He stood outside my office, one eyebrow raised, waiting for me to comment.

"You're late," I said.

"Your country is too large. I've spent an unreasonable amount of time moving between rooms."

I looked at the stick.

"How are you?"

"Well enough to be irritated by your question."

"Then you must be recovering."

We shook hands.

He gripped mine a second longer than either of us intended.

It had been seven months since I'd last heard his voice through a damaged circuit during the battle. I'd seen photographs of Cutlass in repair, read the medical updates he was willing to share, and spoken to him twice by video. None of it had prepared me for the actual sight of him walking into the room.

He looked older.

There was a scar near his temple that hadn't been there in Scotland. His hair had gone almost entirely silver. The pain in his leg was obvious even when he tried to conceal it.

He watched me studying him.

"You're allowed to ask about the leg."

"Will it heal?"

"The doctors say improvement is possible. They've given me exercises that appear designed by a particularly resentful enemy."

I laughed.

He placed the tea on my desk.

"For your education."

"Is this an apology for your crew's radio manners?"

"No. This is a humanitarian intervention. Your coffee is still an offense against civilization."

We sat.

For a while we spoke about things that had nothing to do with the war: the weather, the absurd cost of travel, the British officer who had tried to explain American football to an entire wardroom and nearly caused a diplomatic incident.

Then Edward looked at the photograph of Northstar on the wall.

"How is she?"

"Repair work continues. Engineering has made good progress. There are still inspections to finish."

"She's a stubborn old girl."

"She kept us afloat."

"So did mine."

He looked at his hands.

The conversation changed without either of us intending it.

"How many did you lose?" I asked.

He told me the number.

I did not write it down. He had come to visit as my friend, not to provide another report.

He named several sailors. One had been a carpenter's son from Wales. Another had played the violin badly during quiet evenings at sea. A third was so good at making tea that the galley staff had accused him of cheating by boiling water twice.

Edward told their stories without looking up.

I let him.

When he finished, he sat back.

"Eleanor kept the ship together."

"Shaw?"

"Yes. She took command while I was busy making the medical staff furious."

"She gave us clear reports."

"She gave me clear reports too, when I finally stopped insisting I could get up."

He smiled faintly.

"Armitage did well on the bridge. He told me later that you asked him whether he was telling the truth."

"I did."

"He thinks you saved his career."

"I doubt that."

"Well, don't tell him. He needs a little confidence."

I thought of the young lieutenant's voice shaking through the static.

"He earned it."

Edward nodded.

We fell quiet again.

I had been dreading this visit in a way I hadn't admitted to Rebecca. Not because I feared Edward would blame me for the missile that struck Cutlass, but because I knew I was still looking for somebody to tell me the decisions I'd made had been right.

I could hear Mara telling me not to demand certainty from people who hadn't possessed it.

Nevertheless I asked.

"Edward, do you ever think we should have done something different?"

He looked toward the window.

"Every day."

"About the second attack?"

"About all of it."

I waited.

He adjusted the walking stick against his chair.

"The thing that keeps returning isn't the missile. It's one of Caledonia's survivors. He asked whether we could go back for his brother."

I knew the name.

"Colin Pike."

"Yes."

Edward's face tightened.

"I had a ship on fire. I had injured men. I had a damaged communications system and a crew trying to make sense of the impact. And Colin looked at me as though I still had the power to make an ocean give back somebody it had taken."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I was sorry."

"That's all?"

"That's all I had."

He looked at me.

"I've spent years telling young officers that leadership is about decisions. But sometimes leadership is about standing in front of somebody and letting them hate the answer."

I nodded.

That was something no exercise had ever made clear to me.

Edward reached into his jacket and produced a folded photograph.

It showed the crew of Cutlass standing on the quarterdeck during repairs. Some wore bandages. Others leaned on railings or chairs. At the center, Commander Eleanor Shaw held a small mug above her head as though presenting a trophy.

"What is that?" I asked.

"The kettle."

I stared at him.

"The kettle survived?"

"Against all military expectations."

I laughed.

He turned the photograph over.

On the back, in a dozen different hands, was written:

*TO NORTHSTAR — THE WATCH CONTINUES.*

Beneath it, Armitage had added:

*REPORT VERIFIED.*

I was laughing when the tears came.

Edward pretended not to notice.

For several minutes we sat like that: two middle-aged captains who had spent much of their lives practicing for a war they would rather not have fought, finding ourselves defeated by a ridiculous little joke about tea.

Eventually he said, "Daniel, I've been asked to speak at a professional exchange next month."

"On the convoy?"

"Yes. They're calling it lessons in coalition resilience."

"That sounds dreadful."

"It does."

"What will you tell them?"

He looked at the photograph.

"That the Americans were late, the weather was appalling, and the enemy had the poor manners to interrupt our schedule."

"Edward."

He sighed.

"I'll tell them we were fortunate to have each other. I'll tell them neither ship was sufficient by itself, and that the merchants deserved as much respect as the warships. I'll tell them Eleanor took command when the proper structure failed and that nobody should ever be surprised when a well-trained officer does her job."

He looked directly at me.

"And I'll tell them I was afraid."

I thought of Ethan asking the same question after Iceland.

"That might be the most useful thing you say."

He nodded.

I walked him to the door.

As he stood, his leg stiffened and he leaned on the stick. I offered my arm. He accepted it without embarrassment.

Outside the building, the American summer was warm enough to make the sea feel like a dream. Cars moved slowly through the parking lot. Two young sailors hurried past us with a stack of folders, arguing about lunch.

Edward watched them.

"Look at those two," he said.

"What about them?"

"They have absolutely no idea how lucky they are to be arguing over sandwiches."

I smiled.

"Neither did we."

He turned toward me.

"Come to Portsmouth when I'm able. I'll show you Cutlass. She'll be a different ship by then."

"I'd like that."

He paused.

"And bring decent coffee."

"That might be difficult."

"Then bring none."

We embraced awkwardly, two men who had been trained to shake hands and move on.

When he left, I stood outside until his car disappeared.

Back upstairs, I placed the photograph beside the drawing Liv had sent from Aurora. One showed a ship's crew battered but alive. The other showed a child who had survived because people she did not know had stood between her and danger.

For years I'd kept photographs of ceremonies and inspections in my office.

Those two belonged there more than any medal.

I sat down and returned to the manuscript.

There was another chapter to write about the British ship, but I couldn't begin it with the missile strike. I knew now the story of Cutlass did not begin when she was hit or end when the fires were out.

It began long before, with sailors learning one another's names, and continued long afterward, with a wounded captain who crossed an ocean carrying a box of tea.

I opened a new page.

I wrote Edward's name.

And then I wrote the word that had mattered most on the worst day of our lives.

*Friend.*
