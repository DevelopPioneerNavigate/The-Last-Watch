**THE DISTANCE HOME — 20 NOVEMBER, 1828 HOURS**

The call connected on the third attempt.

I'd been looking at the same blank video window for nearly ten minutes, listening to a series of soft clicks that could mean almost anything. Then the screen brightened, the image froze, and I saw Rebecca's face.

She looked tired. Her hair was pulled back, and she'd put a cup of coffee next to the computer as though we were about to have an ordinary conversation.

For a moment neither of us spoke.

Then she said, "Daniel."

I closed my eyes.

"Hi."

She looked at me and began crying.

I had imagined this moment a hundred times during the crossing. In every version I had something sensible to say: the ship was safe, the mission was over, I loved her. The words had sounded complete in my head.

Now all I could manage was "I'm here."

She covered her mouth.

"I know."

The picture stuttered.

I thought the connection would fail.

Then she leaned forward again.

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Are you sure?"

"A few bruises. I'm tired."

"That's not what I asked."

I looked at her.

"Physically, I'm all right."

She nodded, but I could see her working through the distinction.

"Is Ethan there?" I asked.

"He's downstairs. He wanted to be on the call, but he said he'd give us five minutes first."

I smiled.

"He's growing up."

"He thinks so."

We were quiet for a while.

Behind Rebecca I could see part of our kitchen, the shelf where we kept the cookbooks, and the small dent in the wall Ethan had made years earlier trying to carry a chair through a doorway by himself. I'd forgotten the dent was there.

The sight hurt worse than the battle had.

"I heard about the ship," Rebecca said.

"Some of it."

"People were calling. Friends. The spouses' network. Someone said Northstar had been hit. Someone said it wasn't coming back. Then we heard you were in Iceland."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry for the people who were worried. Just tell me what happened."

I looked down at my hands.

"There was a freighter. Caledonia. We lost her. Cutlass took damage. Northstar had a fire in engineering."

She nodded slowly.

"Elena?"

The name stopped me.

Rebecca had met Ramirez at a command picnic. They'd spent most of an afternoon discussing children, school schedules, and the strange ways military families learn to plan without knowing where anyone will be next month.

"Elena died," I said.

Rebecca put a hand against the screen.

"Oh, Daniel."

"She went back for three sailors. They survived."

"And she didn't."

"No."

Rebecca looked away for a long time.

"Her little girl."

"Sofia."

"I remember."

I wanted to explain how brave Elena had been. How well she'd trained her people. How the crew would remember her. But Rebecca knew all of those things were true and none of them would help a nine-year-old at bedtime.

"What does the family know?" she asked.

"Official notifications are being handled. I haven't spoken with them personally yet."

"Don't call them before the proper people do."

"I won't."

"And don't decide you're responsible for every terrible thing they feel."

I looked at her.

"That's not fair."

"No," she said. "It isn't. But I know you."

The line went silent for an instant, then returned.

I could hear our refrigerator humming through the speakers. Such a stupidly ordinary sound. I'd been surrounded by machinery for days, and this was the only machine I wanted to hear.

Rebecca picked up her cup.

"Do you remember the garden fence?"

"Of course."

"You promised you'd fix it in the spring."

"Did it fall over?"

"Not yet."

"That's good."

"Your son says we should leave it until you come home because he wants to watch you swear at the posts."

I laughed.

It came out like a cough.

She smiled through tears.

"There you are."

I shook my head.

"Where?"

"The man who can't build a straight fence."

"I command a destroyer."

"Exactly. You should know your limits."

I laughed again, and this time the sound felt real.

Then she became serious.

"Are you coming home?"

"I don't know when. The ship needs repairs, and there will be reports. People are injured. There are still things to do."

"That's not a date."

"I know."

She looked directly at the camera.

"I don't need a date you can't give. I need to know you're going to let somebody else carry part of this."

I stared at her.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean you can be a captain without believing you're the only one who can grieve for your crew. Your executive officer is there. The chiefs are there. Your sailors have people who care about them. You have us."

I looked away.

"I don't know how to stop thinking about it."

"Then don't stop. Just come back to the room when you can."

I had no answer.

A sound came from downstairs.

Rebecca glanced toward the doorway.

"Ethan wants to know if his five minutes are up."

"Tell him to come."

There was a shuffle, a thump, and an exasperated "Dad?" from somewhere beyond the camera.

Then my son appeared.

He was sixteen and somehow taller than I remembered. His hair was too long. He wore a T-shirt from a band I'd never heard of and had the uncomfortable expression of a boy trying to look unimpressed by the fact that his father was alive.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey, buddy."

"Mom says you got there."

"I did."

"Was it bad?"

I could see Rebecca watching both of us.

"Yes," I said.

He nodded.

He looked down at the table.

"Are you going back out?"

"Not for a while. The ship's damaged."

He considered that.

"Can you just tell them not to?"

Rebecca started to speak, but I raised a hand.

"I can tell them what my ship is capable of and what my crew needs. I can speak up when something isn't safe. I can't make every decision for the Navy."

"That's a stupid system."

"Sometimes it feels that way."

He smiled a little.

"Did you see any other ships?"

"Lots."

"Did you fight?"

"Yes."

He went quiet.

I'd expected a teenager's curiosity about missiles, explosions, and what it felt like to face an enemy. Instead he asked, "Were you scared?"

I looked at him.

"Very."

His face changed.

"Really?"

"Yes."

"I thought captains didn't get scared."

"Then somebody gave you bad information."

He nodded, considering that.

"Okay."

I waited.

"Are you still?"

I was surprised by how much I wanted to lie.

"Sometimes," I said.

He looked toward his mother.

Then back at the camera.

"Well, when you get home, we can fix the fence."

I smiled.

"That sounds good."

"And I'll let you do the measuring."

"That's generous."

"Mom says you need practice."

Rebecca laughed.

I watched them both, standing in the kitchen I had been trying not to imagine for two days. The camera image was grainy. The sound lagged. I knew the connection could fail at any moment.

Nothing about it was perfect.

It was enough.

The call ended after the connection began to break down. We said goodbye three times, each one more reluctant than the last.

When the screen went black, the cabin felt smaller.

I sat for a long time looking at my reflection.

I had brought the convoy to Iceland. I had lost a friend aboard my own ship. I had answered questions from admirals and medical officers and grieving captains. But the hardest task waiting for me might be learning how to return to the people who had been living without me.

A knock came at the door.

Mara leaned in.

"Everything all right?"

I nodded.

"Spoke to Rebecca and Ethan."

"Good?"

"Hard."

She understood.

"Want some company?"

I looked at the empty chair.

"Yes."

She came in and sat down.

Neither of us said much.

For the first time since Norway, I allowed silence to be something other than the space before the next alarm.
