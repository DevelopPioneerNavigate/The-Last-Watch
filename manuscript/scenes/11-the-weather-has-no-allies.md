**THE WEATHER HAS NO ALLIES — 1558 HOURS**

A winter storm in the Norwegian Sea is not scenery. It is another problem the commanding officer must solve, and it has no commander who can be negotiated with.

The bow rose high enough that I felt the ship hesitate. Then she struck the descending slope of the swell and water swept across the forward deck. Spray burst around the superstructure in gray sheets. The lookouts remained in protected positions, their reports a mixture of poor visibility and disciplined patience.

Navigation had established a safer northern track that avoided the forecast ice while reducing our predictability. The route was not ideal. It was simply the least bad option the charts and current conditions allowed.

Bishop showed me the margins.

"We have a corridor, Captain. It's narrower than I like."

"How much confidence in the ice information?"

"Enough to navigate with caution, not enough to relax. We'll use every independent observation we can."

"And the merchants?"

"Meridian is requesting a speed reduction. Her master says the roll is affecting cargo stability and crew safety."

"Let him slow. We adjust as a convoy."

Idris joined us from Combat.

"That gives the pursuing surface group another chance to close."

"I understand."

"Recommend we consider a higher transit speed when the weather permits."

"Agreed when it permits. Not before."

I studied the struggling tanker through the spray. A merchant captain knows the shape of his vessel and the limits of her cargo better than a destroyer commander does. An escort that ignores those limits can wreck the convoy without enemy assistance.

I asked for a circuit to Captain Sofia Reed of Meridian.

"How's your ship, Sofia?"

"Unhappy, Daniel."

"Mine too."

"I can maintain the revised speed. I can't promise she'll enjoy it."

"Nobody's having fun today."

She laughed once, then lowered her voice.

"Captain, I know you've got bigger things to think about. But I want to tell you something."

"Go ahead."

"My crew can see your destroyer from the bridge. Every time we make the turn into a swell, they look for you. They want to know you're still there."

I stared at our own bow, rising and falling.

"We're still here."

"That's all I needed."

The radio closed.

I thought of the tanker crew, thirty people who had loaded fuel before this voyage without knowing they would soon be traveling through missiles and torpedoes. To them, Northstar was not merely an escort. We were visible proof that somebody had not forgotten them.

The ship rolled deeply to port. An unsecured chart roller clattered across the bridge, earning a sharp look from Bishop. A sailor secured it before I could say anything.

"Captain," Bishop said, "we're approaching the next validated leg."

"Proceed."

He repeated the order and checked the positions of the merchants. There was competence in the way he worked, quiet and almost stubborn. I wanted to thank him, but I knew praise in the middle of a difficult maneuver could distract as easily as help.

Instead I kept watching.

Far ahead the sky brightened faintly where a gap in the clouds allowed a few minutes of pale daylight. It exposed a strip of ocean the color of iron filings. There was no land. No other ship in sight. Only water and weather.

I imagined what it would be like if our remaining propulsion failed here. Three civilian ships, one badly damaged British frigate, and a storm that could keep rescuers away. We had been saved by our engineering teams once. I did not intend to demand a miracle from them twice.

"Engineering, Captain."

"Navarro, sir."

"How stable is the starboard plant?"

"As stable as I can make it. We're operating within the revised limits. Monitoring the damaged equipment continuously."

"Any signs the remaining machinery is under unusual stress?"

"Nothing beyond what this sea is doing to the whole ship."

I heard the fatigue in his voice.

"Luis, I need you to put somebody else on the circuit for a while and get ten minutes of food."

"Captain—"

"Ten minutes. Nobody gets wiser through starvation."

He exhaled.

"Yes, sir."

In the next hour we revised the formation several times. Each change was checked against the actual sea conditions and the ability of the damaged ships. The convoy did not move as neatly as a set of icons on a plot. One freighter lagged on a swell. The tanker yawed. Cutlass had to compensate for her reduced maneuvering margin.

It was exhausting work, but it was work we understood.

A threat appeared briefly on an electronic display and vanished before it could be classified. We did not chase it. We did not pretend to know what it was. We recorded it and kept moving.

When I finally looked toward Mara, she was studying me instead of the chart.

"What's wrong?" I asked.

"Nothing new," she said. "And that is precisely why I'm going to insist you sleep."

I wanted to object. But the storm had made one thing clear: the weather could take our judgment long before it took the ship.

A tired commander who insists on remaining indispensable eventually makes himself dangerous.

I knew that lesson in theory.

Mara was about to make me practice it.
