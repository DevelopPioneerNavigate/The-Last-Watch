**THE INVISIBLE FRONT — 0858 HOURS**

Electronic interference does not smell like gunpowder. It does not shake a ship or throw sailors against bulkheads. It announces itself as a wrong number, a missing message, a picture that contradicts the one beside it.

That made it especially dangerous.

We were trained to be skeptical of systems, but modern naval operations depended on networks so extensively that their failure changed the tempo of every decision. A ship could still navigate, fight, and communicate without every digital convenience. Yet the transition from plentiful information to incomplete information could be harder than the loss of information itself.

Chief Alvarez led me through the communications failures.

"This circuit works until it doesn't. This one is receiving delayed traffic. This purported headquarters message failed authentication. The network is producing more data than confidence."

"Can we separate weather from hostile interference?"

"Not cleanly, sir."

"Then don't try to make every failure part of a master plan."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

I looked at the time stamps from three reports describing the same movement by a friendly aircraft. Each used a different moment as its reference. Together, they made the aircraft appear to occupy three places at once.

"That'll get somebody killed if we don't notice."

"Yes, Captain."

A junior information warfare officer, Lieutenant Nadia Farouk, joined us. She had been working without a break to compare independent sources and establish what was still trustworthy.

"We've put the unverifiable traffic into quarantine for command decisions," she said. "The teams are now labeling uncertainty directly, rather than passing apparently precise tracks along."

"Any evidence of intrusion into ship control systems?"

"Nothing confirmed. We're treating the communications anomaly separately from equipment faults unless evidence links them."

"Good. I want discipline, not conspiracy."

Farouk looked relieved.

I had known officers who became so convinced of enemy deception that they started distrusting accurate information simply because it was inconvenient. The opposite failure was equally dangerous: assuming every display must be true because it looked authoritative.

"What's reliable?" I asked.

She listed it: local instruments, independent observations, appropriately verified messages, and the professional judgment of watch teams who understood the limits of their sensors.

It was less glamorous than a network diagram, and infinitely more useful.

On the bridge, Bishop and Mercer were cross-checking the navigation picture against established sources. A paper chart lay open beneath weighted edges. The sight was almost comforting.

"We've checked the route against the best available ice warning," Bishop said. "There's room to maneuver, but not room to become careless."

"Mark the uncertainties. I want every officer of the deck to know where the confidence drops."

"Aye, Captain."

I returned to Combat, where Idris was discussing one of the doubtful aircraft reports with the intelligence specialist. Neither man raised his voice. They challenged each other's assumptions, returned to the evidence, and reached a narrower conclusion than either had hoped.

That was how a ship defended itself against bad information. Not by pretending its people were infallible, but by making disagreement an ordinary part of doing the job.

Then the false message arrived ordering us to abandon the convoy.

I read it and felt a brief, poisonous temptation.

It would be easy to obey an order that promised escape from responsibility.

If headquarters had truly directed us to turn southwest, the burden would belong to someone else. I could say that I had followed orders. I could leave the ferry and hope a different force would find her. I could sleep.

That was what made the message so dangerous even before we identified it as false. It offered a tired captain exactly the permission he most wanted.

Alvarez reported the authentication failure. We rejected the order and sought confirmation.

Afterward, Mara remained beside me.

"You looked at that one for a long time," she said.

"I did."

"Wanted it to be real?"

For a second I considered lying.

"Part of me did."

She nodded.

"Part of me too."

I looked at her, surprised.

"Why?"

"Because if someone told us our responsibility had ended, we could stop carrying it."

We let the words sit between us.

Then she said, "But it hasn't."

"No."

We returned to our stations.

Farouk issued a short watch advisory. Nothing dramatic: state the source, state confidence, avoid presenting incomplete data as fact, and escalate inconsistent information. I approved it.

Modern war contains moments of terrifying spectacle. Missiles rising through cloud. Ships burning. Helicopters hovering above black waves.

It also contains a junior officer correcting a timestamp before somebody acts on it.

The second may save as many lives as the first, and nobody will ever film it.
