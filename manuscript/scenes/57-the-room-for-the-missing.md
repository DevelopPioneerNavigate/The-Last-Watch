**THE ROOM FOR THE MISSING — 20 NOVEMBER, 1525 HOURS**

The names were written on a whiteboard in an empty classroom.

The room belonged to a community building near the harbor, temporarily converted into a family reception center. Its walls were decorated with children's paintings that had been left there before the war. A bright orange sun hung above a blue mountain. Another picture showed three people beside a house with smoke coming from its chimney.

On one wall was a list of evacuees who had arrived from Aurora. On another were names of relatives being sought by people who had stepped ashore.

The lists were not complete.

I had been invited by Lieutenant Commander Jónsdóttir, the Icelandic liaison officer who had helped coordinate our arrival. She wanted me to meet the people managing the records, not to make a speech.

"We have families separated by evacuations from different ports," she told me. "Some names are spelled differently on separate manifests. Some people have no documents. We're trying to keep the process accurate and humane."

I looked at the board.

"Are these names for public display?"

"No. This room is restricted to the reception team and the people who need to use it. The records are handled carefully. Families come here with an interpreter when appropriate."

A woman at the nearest desk was speaking softly with an elderly man. He held a photograph between shaking hands. The interpreter asked him to repeat the name of a town. He did, then corrected the pronunciation twice.

Nobody rushed him.

I watched from the doorway.

This was another kind of search operation, one I had never trained to command. Instead of sonar tracks there were names and photographs. Instead of a hostile ocean there were broken journeys, missed buses, missing documents, and the terrible uncertainty of not knowing whether a relative had survived.

Jónsdóttir introduced me to the reception coordinator, Margrét. She had worked for the city before the crisis, organizing ordinary administrative services. Now she was coordinating volunteers, translators, and information exchanges with hospitals and shelters.

"Commander," she said, "I'm told you have the original passenger records from Aurora."

"We have some voyage records and the confirmed arrival information. Captain Lindholm's people will be the most reliable source for what happened aboard the ferry."

"Could we arrange a verified handover?"

"Yes. With the appropriate permissions."

She looked relieved.

"We've had three people come here claiming they can produce lists of missing passengers. Two were well-intentioned but wrong. One wanted money."

I stared at her.

"Money?"

She nodded.

"People are frightened. Somebody always decides that creates an opportunity."

I felt anger rise so quickly I had to look away.

"Who is handling it?"

"The police and the reception authorities. We don't need a warship captain to solve it."

She said it kindly.

I smiled without amusement.

"Fair enough."

We sat at a table and reviewed the basic process. Verified names, corrected spellings, protected details, a method for relatives to receive updates without exposing everyone's private information. The questions were not unlike those we'd faced during Caledonia's rescue: which names were confirmed, which were missing, and which had been entered twice because nobody could reconcile an uncertain report.

But here the corrections might bring a father into the room with his daughter.

That possibility made the work feel unbearably important.

At 1602 a volunteer came through the door carrying a child-sized mitten and a slip of paper.

"Margrét, we've found the family that asked about this."

Margrét looked at the note.

"Confirmed?"

"Yes. The mother is at the northern shelter. The child is here with an aunt. The liaison staff have made contact with both."

The volunteer's face brightened.

I looked down at the mitten.

It was gray, with a faded yellow stripe across the cuff.

Such a small object to hold so much relief.

A short time later a family was reunited in the corridor. I did not watch closely. Their privacy belonged to them. But I heard the sound when the mother recognized her child, a word spoken in a language I did not know and repeated until it stopped sounding like a word at all.

I stood quietly beside Jónsdóttir.

"That happens every day now," she said.

"Good."

"Not always. Sometimes we have no answer."

"I know."

She looked at me.

"Do you?"

I thought of Hawthorne, of Caledonia's missing sailors, of Matthew's family waiting for a message.

"Yes."

She nodded.

Back in the classroom, Margrét had covered the board with a clean sheet while the records were being reconciled. Several names had already been resolved. Others remained.

I asked what the reception teams needed most.

"Reliable information," she said. "And patience."

It was the same answer I'd been hearing from sailors for days.

"Anything Northstar can do to help with the first?"

"Make sure the crew provides what they actually know. Not what they suppose. And when someone has no answer, don't give them a reassuring invention."

"I can do that."

I left the building as evening rain began to fall.

Outside, people were carrying blankets from a van to a shelter entrance. A child stood under an umbrella, trying to catch raindrops in her mouth. A volunteer laughed and told her to stop.

I wondered how many of the names inside would be resolved before morning.

I hoped all of them.

I knew better than to promise it.

On the way back to Northstar, I stopped beside the harbor wall. The sea was dark, but the lights behind me made little patterns on its surface.

For days I'd been trying to describe the convoy in terms of ships saved and ships lost.

Here was another measure.

A child found her mother.

An elderly man was given time to pronounce a name correctly.

A stranger did not have to purchase the truth from somebody pretending to own it.

Those were results too.

I returned to the ship and asked Alvarez to coordinate the authorized information handover with Lindholm and the reception team.

Then I opened my notebook.

Under the heading *People*, I wrote:

*The mission was not complete when the ships reached land. It was complete, in small pieces, whenever someone found the person they were looking for.*

I left room beneath the sentence.

There were more names to learn.
