**THE CAPTAINS WITHOUT SHIPS — 1138 HOURS**

Eileen Ward arrived aboard Northstar carrying a borrowed coat and no luggage.

I was in my cabin reviewing the first post-arrival reports when Cross called.

"Captain, the master of Caledonia is at the quarterdeck. She's asking to see you."

I set the reports aside.

"Bring her up."

He hesitated.

"She doesn't look well, sir."

"Medical?"

"Cutlass cleared her for a brief visit and arranged the transfer. She's refusing anything more than that."

"All right. Have Leah available if she needs assistance."

A few minutes later Eileen stood in my doorway.

She had a bruise along her cheekbone and a bandage on her wrist. Someone had given her a dark wool coat that was too large in the shoulders. Her hair, which I remembered neatly pinned beneath a captain's cap in Norway, had been cut unevenly at the ends where it had been scorched or tangled.

She looked older than she had on the pier five days earlier.

I stood.

"Eileen."

"Daniel."

We shook hands.

For a moment, the gesture was absurdly formal. We had both spent the last two days wondering whether we would live, and now we were behaving as though we'd met at a maritime conference.

"Sit down," I said.

She looked at the chair.

"Is this your captain's chair?"

"Yes."

"Then I would rather stand."

I understood. There was dignity in refusing to occupy a place that did not belong to her, even though she no longer had a bridge or a command.

I sat on the edge of the bunk instead.

"How are your people?"

"Alive ones are receiving treatment. Some will need months. Colin Pike keeps asking about his brother."

"I know."

She looked down at her borrowed coat.

"I have twenty-six names on my crew list, Daniel. I know where nineteen of them are. Three are confirmed dead. Four are still missing."

She held up a folded sheet of paper.

"I made the list again this morning. Wrote it all by hand. I thought perhaps the figures would become different if I stopped using the official report."

I said nothing.

She unfolded the paper and read the names quietly, one after another.

They were not ranks or positions. They were names spoken by a captain who had once heard those people complaining about the weather and laughing in the galley.

When she finished, she folded it again.

"I want you to tell me something."

"Anything I can."

"Was there more you could have done?"

I felt the question strike like a hand against my chest.

I had been asking it of myself since Caledonia went down.

"We kept Northstar with the remaining convoy because the threat was still active. Cutlass moved to assist you. We could not safely abandon the other ships."

"I know what your decision was."

"Yes."

"I'm asking whether it was right."

I looked at her.

"I believe it was the best decision I could make with what we knew. I also know that doesn't bring your sailors back."

She watched me.

"Do you ever want to give me a more comforting answer?"

"Every time I think about them."

A long silence followed.

Then she sat in the chair after all.

"Good," she said.

I frowned.

"Good?"

"If you had told me you did everything perfectly, I would have walked out."

I gave a tired smile.

"So would I."

She stared at the wall, where a photograph of Ethan stood beside one of Rebecca.

"Your family?"

"Yes."

"How old is your boy?"

"Sixteen."

She nodded.

"My youngest niece is about that age. She wants to study medicine."

She looked toward the small windowless bulkhead.

"Caledonia was carrying supplies for a clinic where she'd volunteered last summer. I kept thinking about that when we were loading. That was why I agreed to take the extra generator."

"I remember."

"Now the cargo's gone."

"Yes."

"And I keep wondering if the people waiting for it know what happened."

"They've been notified. The relief coordination teams are arranging replacement shipments where possible."

She closed her eyes.

"Good."

For a while we sat without speaking.

I understood something I had missed when Ward first appeared in my doorway. She had not come to ask me to justify my decisions. She had come because she was a captain without a ship and needed someone who knew what that meant.

A commanding officer's entire life becomes entangled with a vessel. You learn her systems, her temperament, her peculiar sounds. You know the names of people who repair her and feed her. You absorb the worries of others until a hundred ordinary details feel like parts of your own body.

Then, if the sea takes the ship, it takes a version of you with it.

"You going back to sea?" I asked.

She looked at me as though I had asked a difficult mathematical problem.

"I don't know."

"That's allowed."

"I used to think I couldn't imagine a life ashore. Now I can't imagine walking onto another bridge."

I nodded.

"Both can be true for a while."

She looked at my photographs again.

"Did you tell your wife what happened?"

"Not all of it."

"Why?"

"Because I don't know how."

Eileen laughed softly.

"There's an honest answer."

A knock came at the door.

Cross looked in.

"Captain, Commander Shaw from Cutlass is requesting a short conference when convenient."

"Tell her I'll call shortly."

"Yes, sir."

He started to leave, then stopped.

"Captain Ward, there's something your people asked us to give you."

He held out a small cloth pouch.

Eileen took it.

Inside was a metal key ring with a brass plate engraved with the name CALedONIA. It was blackened on one side.

"One of your sailors recovered it after the sinking," Cross said. "He wanted you to have it."

She ran a finger across the letters.

"That was from the wheelhouse," she whispered.

Cross nodded.

"I'll leave you both."

The door closed.

Eileen held the key ring for a long time.

"She was a good ship," she said at last.

"I know."

"People like to say that as though it's a technical judgment. Good engine. Good hull. Good handling."

"Yes."

"But that's not what I mean."

I looked at her.

"I know what you mean."

She put the key ring carefully into her pocket.

"Thank you for bringing the others home, Daniel."

I shook my head.

"Cutlass did the rescue. Your people fought for one another."

"And you stayed with the convoy."

I could not find a reply.

She rose from the chair.

"We both did what we thought was right. I suppose that's all a captain can do."

I walked her to the passageway.

Before she left, she turned.

"One more thing."

"Yes?"

"When you write your report, don't let Caledonia become just a lost hull and a cargo figure."

"I won't."

"Write about the engineers who kept the lights on. Write about the cook who gave away his own meal. Write about Aaron Pike, who worked an extra watch because his brother was tired."

I nodded.

"Every name, Eileen. I promise."

She held out her hand.

This time our handshake felt less formal.

Then she went ashore, carrying the only piece of her ship that had been returned to her.

I stood in the passageway after she'd gone.

Through the deck beneath me came the steady hum of Northstar's remaining machinery. The sound was familiar, reassuring, almost unbearably precious.

I placed a hand on the bulkhead.

For the first time since the fire, I thanked the ship out loud.

Not because steel deserves gratitude.

Because three hundred living people still called her home.

And Eileen Ward had just reminded me how quickly that can be taken away.
