**THE CONVOY AFTER OURS — 20 NOVEMBER, 1655 HOURS**

I had not expected to be asked about another convoy before the first one was fully unloaded.

A naval officer found me on Northstar's quarterdeck while I was speaking with the duty watch. She wore the insignia of a captain, a wet coat, and the expression of somebody who had received too much news in too little time.

"Commander Vale? Captain Ingrid Halvorsen. Maritime operations liaison."

I shook her hand.

"Welcome aboard."

"Thank you. I'm sorry to arrive unannounced. I've reviewed your preliminary messages. We have another civilian movement under consideration, and the planning team wants to understand what happened to LANTERN."

"Then we should sit down."

She looked toward the harbor.

"Not yet, if you don't mind. I'd like to see the ships."

I led her to a place on deck where the surviving merchants were visible beyond our mooring lines.

Aurora lay alongside a passenger terminal. People were still moving down the gangway, escorted by volunteers and medical workers. Atlas Mariner was preparing to discharge relief shelters. Meridian remained at its berth under a careful transfer plan.

"Three ships," Halvorsen said.

"Four when we departed."

She nodded.

"I know."

Her voice was quiet.

The wind moved across the harbor, cold enough to cut through my uniform.

"What's the next convoy?" I asked.

"Relief movement from a different northern port. Civilians and equipment. Details are still being evaluated."

"How many escorts?"

"Not finalized."

"Then my first recommendation is that the shipping schedule not be treated as a substitute for escort availability."

She looked at me.

"That's what I expected you to say."

"Good."

"But it doesn't solve the problem. People need to leave. Supplies need to move. And every available escort is already carrying commitments."

I thought of the hospitals waiting for Caledonia's generators.

"I understand that."

"I know you do."

She turned toward Cutlass.

"How much confidence can we place in the broader naval picture?"

I studied the scarred British frigate.

"Enough to use it. Not enough to stop checking it."

Halvorsen took out a small notebook.

"Can you be more specific?"

"Some information reached us late. Some reports appeared independent but traced back to the same uncertain observation. A message claiming to be a headquarters order failed authentication. We rejected it, but it arrived at a moment when following it would have pulled us away from the civilian ships."

"Did you establish its origin?"

"No."

"Did it resemble a genuine instruction?"

"Yes."

She stopped writing.

"That's the part I'm concerned about."

We went into a small briefing room below deck. Mara joined us with Farouk and Alvarez. None of us had slept enough, but it was clear the questions mattered for people still at sea.

Halvorsen spread a copy of our operational chronology across the table.

"I want to understand where command control helped you and where it nearly failed you."

I looked at Mara.

She answered first.

"It helped whenever the intent was clear and our teams knew what they were authorized to do. It nearly failed us when the reporting system created a false sense of certainty."

Farouk nodded.

"Several times, data appeared more current than it was. We had to separate what was directly observed from what somebody inferred. That distinction is easy to lose when everyone is staring at the same display."

Alvarez added, "And formatting is not proof of authenticity. We used the approved alternate channels before changing the mission."

Halvorsen listened without interrupting.

Then she asked a question I had not expected.

"Commander Vale, what did your civilian captains know?"

I took a moment.

"Less than we did, naturally. But they knew their ships. We kept them informed about actions that affected them. When weather and maneuvering became difficult, we used their limitations in our decisions."

"Did you always consult them?"

"No. In emergencies there wasn't time. But when we had time, yes."

"Did they understand the threat?"

"They understood enough to be afraid. And enough to cooperate."

Halvorsen wrote something down.

"Some of the proposed procedures for the next movement give the merchants only their assigned course and speed."

"That's insufficient for the voyage we made," I said. "You don't have to reveal protected information to tell a civilian master why an instruction matters or what conditions require an immediate report."

She looked at me.

"Would you put that in writing?"

"Gladly."

Mara produced the merchant communications log. It showed time after time when Ward, Lindholm, Reed and Solberg had supplied observations our combat systems could not have generated: mechanical limitations, passenger injuries, cargo shifts, the need to keep people away from exposed decks.

"These weren't complications to the mission," she said. "They were facts the mission depended on."

Halvorsen studied the log for several minutes.

Then she looked toward Farouk.

"And what about the false order?"

"We don't have proof it was tailored specifically to us," Farouk answered. "We do know that it exploited a dangerous possibility: a crew under pressure might welcome a simple instruction because it relieves uncertainty."

"How do we protect against that?"

"Better authentication and disciplined challenge procedures. But also training people to say 'I cannot verify this' without fear of being treated as obstructive."

Halvorsen closed her notebook.

"That may be the most important sentence I've heard today."

I glanced toward Alvarez.

He looked embarrassed.

"Captain, Chief Alvarez is the one who refused to accept the message. He deserves the credit."

Alvarez shook his head.

"I just did my job, sir."

"Exactly," Halvorsen said. "That's what I want the next ship to do."

The discussion continued for nearly an hour. Nobody asked us for a miracle. We described the things that had been difficult, the decisions that hadn't worked as well as we'd hoped, and the limits of what we could know even after the fact.

When the meeting ended, Halvorsen folded the papers into a narrow file.

"Commander, I won't tell you that this guarantees the next convoy will be safe."

"Please don't."

"I will tell you we've identified changes worth making before it sails."

I nodded.

"Then this was useful."

She hesitated.

"There's something else. The planning board asked whether Northstar might return to sea after temporary repairs."

I looked at her.

"Not until my engineers and the proper authorities say she's safe."

"I thought so."

"And not with sailors who haven't had the medical and rest they need."

"Understood."

She held out her hand.

"I'll make sure that position is conveyed accurately."

We shook.

After she'd gone, I returned to the quarterdeck.

The harbor was growing dark. Temporary lights had been erected near the warehouses, and the first evening buses were carrying evacuees toward their shelters. Across the water, Aurora's upper decks were almost empty.

Cross joined me.

"Captain, they say another convoy is forming."

"Yes."

"Going to have a hard time finding escorts."

"I know."

He watched the harbor for a moment.

"Think they'll get through?"

I looked toward the ships.

"I don't know."

He nodded.

"At least the people planning it now know what happened to us."

"Some of it."

"Enough to change something?"

"I hope so."

Cross studied me.

"That's a big difference from yesterday."

"What is?"

"Yesterday all we could do was survive our own voyage. Today we might be able to keep somebody else's from becoming as bad."

I thought of the men Caledonia had lost, the damage to Cutlass, and Ramirez's daughter waiting to be told her mother wasn't coming home.

"Yes," I said. "That matters."

There was no announcement that the next convoy would depart. No triumphant broadcast from headquarters. Just a planning team revising procedures, sailors sharing their hard-earned observations, and civilians who still needed a way out.

I had spent two days believing the war was the narrow strip of water around Northstar.

Now I understood that our watch had not ended when the mooring lines were secured.

It had become an obligation to tell the next captain the truth.

The harbor lights came on, one by one.

And somewhere in the north, other ships were preparing to sail.
