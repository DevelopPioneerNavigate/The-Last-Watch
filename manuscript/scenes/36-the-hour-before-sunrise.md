**THE HOUR BEFORE SUNRISE — 0556 HOURS**

The young sailor holding the clipboard had not yet learned how to make his hand stop shaking.

His name was Seaman Apprentice Noah Ellis, and I'd seen him on the bridge earlier that morning. He was the boy from Idaho who had joined the Navy to see something larger than home. He stood just outside Combat with a casualty-message routing sheet, looking as though the paper weighed more than he did.

"Captain, I was told to bring this directly."

"All right, Ellis."

He held it out with both hands.

It was a preliminary record of the Caledonia rescue traffic, names of those recovered, names not yet accounted for, and a final position marked with the careful precision of men who knew a ship had gone down there. Somebody had written *subject to correction* across the top.

I took the paper.

"Who asked you to carry it?"

"Chief Alvarez, sir. The internal network had a delay. He didn't want the list waiting."

"Good judgment."

"Yes, sir."

He remained in the doorway.

"Something else?"

He swallowed.

"Captain, am I allowed to ask a question?"

"You are."

"Why aren't we going back?"

I looked at the paper again. He had heard about the survivors. He had heard the rescue calls. He had probably watched the sinking on a camera feed, along with half the ship. The question was not indiscipline. It was a young man's first encounter with the fact that the right decision can look cruel from where you stand.

"Come inside for a minute."

I led him toward a quiet part of the room, away from the operators. We stopped beside a chart showing the remaining merchant formation.

"What do you see?"

"Three ships, sir."

"And how many were there?"

"Four."

"Yes."

He looked at me, frightened by the simplicity of the question.

"Cutlass went to help Caledonia because she was positioned to do so. We remained with Aurora, Atlas Mariner, and Meridian because they were still vulnerable. We also had evidence that a submarine threat remained. If we all turned toward the sinking, those ships would have been left exposed."

He nodded, but his face did not clear.

"That doesn't mean everybody on Caledonia could be saved," I added. "It means we have to protect people who are still in danger."

"Yes, Captain."

"Does that answer your question?"

He looked toward the map.

"It explains it."

"That's different from liking it."

"Yes, sir."

"Nobody aboard has to like it."

He looked relieved to hear that.

For a moment I thought he would leave. Instead he said, "My dad drives a truck. He says sometimes you do everything you're supposed to and somebody still gets hurt. I used to think he said that to make me feel better about getting in trouble."

"Do you still think that?"

"No, sir."

I could have offered some line about maturity or duty. I didn't. The boy had learned enough for one night.

"Go find Chief Alvarez. Tell him the list arrived. Then get back to your watch."

"Aye, Captain."

He left with his shoulders a little straighter.

I looked down at the names.

Each was someone a family might be waiting for. One was a man whose brother had already asked me to keep searching. Another was an engineer who had worked through the night to get medical supplies aboard. A third had served as an ordinary deckhand and had no remarkable detail listed beside his name.

I thought that detail was the most important one.

The sea had taken men who never expected to be remarkable.

I stepped back toward the central plot.

"Combat, Captain. What's the next known threat window?"

Idris looked up.

"We cannot establish a specific window with confidence."

"Good. Don't invent one. What changes our defense if the enemy returns?"

He gave me a concise assessment: uncertain aircraft activity to the north, the possibility of further underwater attack, and the hard limit of what the escort ships could defend at one time.

I listened and asked him what evidence would undermine his current assessment.

He didn't hesitate.

That mattered more to me than a comforting answer.

At 0559 a fresh weather report reached the bridge. The cloud cover had thickened and the sea state was worsening. Bishop asked whether we intended to maintain the planned western turn at the next waypoint.

"Within the agreed merchant limits," I said. "Coordinate it. Don't surprise the tanker."

He acknowledged.

The watch changed over in the sonar compartment. I heard Chief Bell reminding the incoming team what had been detected and, more importantly, what had not been established. Her voice was steady despite the strain.

I wondered whether the young sonar operators had ever imagined a real ship sinking while they listened.

Most of my crew had practiced battle. Some had watched missile footage and rehearsed attacks against moving symbols. There is no simulator for understanding that a voice you heard over the radio an hour ago may now belong only to the past.

Cross approached carrying a cup of coffee and a wrapped sandwich.

"Your executive officer says you haven't eaten."

"That's not true."

"She said you'd say that."

"I had soup."

"Yesterday?"

I considered lying.

Cross handed me the sandwich.

"Here. It comes with no lecture."

"That's suspicious."

"The lecture's from me."

I took a bite.

The coffee was hot enough to burn my tongue. The bread was soggy. It tasted better than anything I'd eaten in two days.

"How are the sailors?" I asked.

"Trying to do their jobs. Asking questions they don't want the answers to."

"Ellis asked why we didn't go back."

"Good question."

"I gave him an explanation."

"That won't make him stop seeing the ship sink."

"No."

Cross watched me.

"Captain, you can't be the only person aboard allowed to feel responsible."

"That's not what I'm doing."

"Isn't it?"

I looked at him.

He didn't retreat.

"You have chiefs. You have officers. You have sailors who know what their jobs are. We share the responsibility for keeping this ship alive. Don't make it something only you can carry."

I thought of Ramirez supervising training, of Bell correcting a junior operator without embarrassment, of Mara insisting I sleep.

"You're right."

"I know," Cross said.

For a moment I was grateful for his complete lack of humility.

Then the first alert reached us from the air-defense watch.

A possible hostile aircraft group had been detected at long range. Not yet a confirmed launch. Not yet a weapon in flight. A possibility that was becoming more difficult to dismiss.

Idris stood beside the plot.

"Captain, track confidence is improving. Multiple contacts appear to be moving in a coordinated pattern."

I put the sandwich down.

The bridge called to confirm the convoy's relative positions.

I answered. Asked for the latest weather. Acknowledged the limitations. Heard all the familiar words I had been trained to say.

Inside, I thought of Ellis and the question he had asked.

Why aren't we going back?

Because there were still people ahead of us who needed to get home.

At 0602 the operators began to classify new high-speed indications. I could hear the entire room narrow its attention to the threat picture. The next moments would demand every ounce of training our people possessed.

I looked once at the casualty list on the console.

*Subject to correction.*

Then I turned it facedown.

"All stations, Captain. Remain ready."

At 0602 the indications sharpened, and I turned toward Idris as the air-defense team prepared to name what was coming. The terrible silence before the first attack had almost ended.
