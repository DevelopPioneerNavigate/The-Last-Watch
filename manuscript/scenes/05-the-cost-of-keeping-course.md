**THE COST OF KEEPING COURSE — 0728 HOURS**

The distress message from Kestrel remained in my mind after we gave Nygaard the last known position.

I asked Alvarez whether the enemy frigate was still transmitting.

"Intermittently, sir. Very weak."

"Any further personnel information?"

"Not reliable."

Mara glanced at me. "You did what you could."

I kept watching the blank area where Kestrel ought to have been.

"Did I?"

"What would you have us do? Take Aurora into an area where the enemy has already sunk one merchant?"

"No."

"Then don't punish yourself for not being in two places at once."

I nodded, but the feeling did not change.

Every profession develops its own moral compromises. Doctors have to choose patients. Firefighters decide which entrance is safe enough to attempt. At sea, I sometimes had to decide which ship could receive help and which one had to wait.

The uniform made the decision mine. It did not make it painless.

Cutlass finally sent an updated casualty report. Her medical spaces were crowded with Caledonia survivors, many with cold-water injuries. A few had severe burns or fractures from the attack. The British were treating them alongside their own crew without hesitation.

One survivor, a young engineer named Colin Pike, asked to speak with the American commander.

"Captain Vale?" he said when the circuit was patched through.

"Yes, Colin."

"Did you see the ship go down?"

"I did."

He was silent long enough that I thought the radio had failed.

"My brother's name is Aaron."

I looked at the current survivor list. I knew the names; I had read them three times already.

"Aaron Pike?"

"Yes, sir."

I searched the confirmed recovered list anyway, absurdly hoping I had missed him.

"I don't have any confirmation on Aaron."

"He was in the aft section. We worked different watches. He was supposed to come to breakfast when I finished."

There was a noise in the background—someone calling for a stretcher.

"He told me he was going to make pancakes when we got home," Colin said. "Our mother says he burns them, but he still makes them every Sunday."

I stared at the radio handset.

"I am sorry, Colin."

"Could you make sure they keep looking?"

"We will search wherever it is safe and possible. I won't tell you we've stopped caring."

"Thank you, sir."

The circuit ended.

Mara looked toward me from the far side of the space. I must have looked different because she said nothing.

A minute later an orderly came in with a small paper cup of coffee. He had been sent by the mess chief, who knew the captain had not eaten. I thanked him. I did not drink it.

I thought about the hundreds of people aboard Aurora. Six hundred and twelve lives could remain a number if I allowed it. I asked Lindholm to have his staff send us a broad accounting of medical needs, emergency preparations, and any urgent passenger welfare concerns. Not names—he had enough to manage. I wanted the ship's people made real in our planning.

The response came in fragments. A pregnant woman needed a quiet place to rest. Several elderly passengers had difficulty moving across the decks. A boy had asthma and his mother was worried about the cold. Families were sharing blankets. A volunteer schoolteacher had organized games for children in a lounge where the windows were covered to keep them from watching the sea.

I asked whether the schoolteacher could put together a list of reassuring activities that worked during alarms. Lindholm sounded surprised.

"You want advice from a teacher?"

"I want everyone who knows how to keep children calm to help."

A short time later, a message arrived through the convoy circuit. The children were making paper ships from spare pamphlets. They had begun naming the ships after the escorts.

A seven-year-old had made one with an enormous American flag taped to the mast.

"He says it's the destroyer," Lindholm told me.

"Does it float?"

"Not particularly well."

"Neither do we in this weather."

He laughed. It was the first relaxed laugh I had heard from him since the attack.

In the middle of an ocean war, a paper boat could be a piece of hope.

That was also why Kestrel troubled me.

If we only saw the human beings behind our own convoy, the war would eventually take the rest of our humanity. The enemy remained dangerous. I had to be prepared to fight those ships. But the existence of a threat did not erase the existence of people.

By the time the next fleet message arrived, I had added a note to the operational log about the rescue coordination. I had no illusions it would alter the course of the war. It might, however, help a future officer understand that a commanding officer can defend his own people and still refuse to cheer another crew's drowning.

That distinction mattered to me more than any medal I would ever wear.
