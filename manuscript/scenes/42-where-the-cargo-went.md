**WHERE THE CARGO WENT — 20 NOVEMBER, 1430 HOURS**

The first person to ask me about the missing generators was not an admiral.

She was a hospital administrator in an orange raincoat, standing on the pier beside a forklift that had stopped working.

Her name was Dr. Freyja Einarsson. She had been directing volunteers through the arrival of the surviving convoy and looked at my uniform as though it might contain a practical answer.

"Commander Vale?"

"Yes."

"I'm told you escorted Caledonia."

I nodded.

"She didn't make it."

"I know. I'm sorry for her people."

She seemed to be deciding how much she could safely say to me.

"I need to know about her cargo."

I looked toward the warehouses.

"The revised manifest has been sent to relief coordination."

"I have the manifest. I need to know whether any of it came off the ship before the attack."

"No. Caledonia was struck at sea."

Her expression did not change, but something in her posture surrendered.

"We had hoped one of the transfers had been made before she sailed."

"I wish I could tell you that."

She glanced at the broken forklift.

"Three clinics are operating on limited electricity. We can move some patients, but moving them carries its own danger. The generators weren't simply equipment we ordered. They were hours of heat, light and medical care."

I knew that already.

Hearing her say it on land made the knowledge heavier.

"Is there another shipment?" I asked.

"Possibly. We've been asking everyone. The equipment is distributed across ports, contractors and warehouses, and half the people we call are dealing with emergencies of their own."

I looked out across the harbor. Atlas Mariner was unloading shelter material. Meridian's fuel had been placed under careful transfer controls. The surviving ships had brought aid, but not the aid she needed most.

"Can I speak to the relief coordinator?" I asked.

"You can speak to me," she said.

I smiled despite myself.

"Then let's see what we can verify."

We found a table in a temporary operations center set up inside a warehouse. Several maps covered one wall. A collection of radios sat beside a battered laptop. Volunteers moved between pallets with lists taped to clipboards. Outside, heavy rain struck the roof hard enough to make conversation difficult.

Freyja introduced me to a Norwegian logistics officer and an Icelandic dock supervisor. They had lists of expected cargo, actual arrivals, urgent requests and ships that could not yet be reached.

The lists contradicted one another.

One indicated a backup generator had reached a southern terminal. Another showed it still aboard a ship whose arrival had been delayed. A third referred to a unit that had already been allocated to a different clinic.

It reminded me unpleasantly of our tactical picture.

"Do we know which units physically exist and where?" I asked.

The dock supervisor looked at me.

"Some."

"Then start with those. Everything else is a request or a rumor until somebody confirms it."

He raised an eyebrow.

"You sound like our warehouse manager."

"I take that as a compliment."

For half an hour, the room worked through what was actually available. Nobody fired a weapon. Nobody maneuvered a destroyer. Yet the same discipline we'd used during the war was suddenly useful ashore: distinguish observation from inference; identify the person responsible for a decision; never allow a confident-looking display to replace an actual verification.

The Norwegian officer produced a report about portable units stored in the north of the island.

"Those are owned by a contractor," she said. "They may not be configured for the clinics."

"Can the contractor confirm their condition?"

"Not yet."

"Then don't count them."

Freyja was watching me.

"You're very careful."

"I spent two days wishing people had been more careful with the information they sent us."

She looked down.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I am trying to be useful. There's a difference."

The door opened and Eileen Ward entered, escorted by a relief worker.

I had not expected to see her again so soon after our conversation aboard Northstar. She was still wearing the borrowed coat. Her wrist remained bandaged, and her face was pale.

"Daniel," she said.

"Eileen."

"Dr. Einarsson asked whether I could help with the cargo records."

Freyja moved toward her.

"Captain Ward, you shouldn't be on your feet."

"I don't intend to unload anything."

She placed a small stack of notes on the table.

"My chief mate kept a separate record of every late addition in Norway. The electronic manifest was updated, but the paper trail is clearer about which crates were aboard."

I looked at her.

"You kept a copy?"

"I keep copies of everything. My crew complained about it for fifteen years. They're welcome to complain again when I see them."

Her voice caught on the last words.

Nobody spoke.

Then she opened the notes.

For two hours she helped the logistics team distinguish which medical supplies had been lost, which had been carried on other ships, and which might be sourced from warehouses without waiting for another Atlantic crossing.

I stayed because I could carry a message between the naval authorities and the people handling the supplies. I made no promises about equipment I hadn't seen. Where someone needed an authorization, I helped find the person who could legitimately give it.

At one point the dock supervisor became frustrated.

"Commander, are you saying you cannot simply order the navy to deliver a generator?"

"That's exactly what I'm saying."

"You're a captain."

"Of a damaged destroyer. I can't make a machine appear in a warehouse because I wear a uniform."

He gave a short, embarrassed laugh.

"I suppose not."

"Let's call the people who actually have it."

Eventually we received confirmation that two portable units were available at a support facility, both inspected, neither yet allocated. They would not replace everything Caledonia carried. They could support a temporary clinic if the receiving technical team approved their use.

Freyja read the report carefully.

"These might help."

"Might," I repeated.

She smiled.

"You're teaching me to be precise."

"You're teaching me what our cargo was supposed to do."

By late afternoon the transfer arrangements had been placed in the hands of the appropriate authorities. I made no claim that Northstar had saved another hospital. What I knew was that a real generator was now more likely to reach a real medical team because several exhausted people had spent the afternoon sorting facts from wishes.

Eileen leaned back in her chair.

"I want to see the patients these supplies help," she said.

Freyja looked at her.

"When you're well enough, I can arrange a visit if the patients and hospital agree."

Eileen nodded.

"Thank you."

I thought of the extra generator she'd waited for in Norway, and the ship that had carried it into the ocean.

"Do you still wish you'd left earlier?" I asked quietly.

She looked at me for a long moment.

"No."

"Even now?"

"That cargo mattered, Daniel. The fact that we lost it doesn't mean we were wrong to try."

I did not answer.

She reached across the table and touched the folded notes.

"The mistake would be letting the next ship carry the same information problems we did. If you want to honor my crew, make the next convoy better."

Those words stayed with me.

The war would continue. More convoys would sail. Some would be protected by ships newer and better equipped than Northstar. Others might have to cross with less. None deserved to learn the same lessons only after somebody died.

When the meeting ended, Freyja offered to walk me back to the pier.

Outside, the rain had thinned.

Aurora's passengers were being transferred into buses. A child in a blue coat stood beside a volunteer, holding a paper boat in both hands. I could not tell whether it was Liv. I didn't need to.

The buses were heading toward shelters with hot food and beds.

That was where the cargo went, in the end. Not into the neat boxes on a manifest, but into things a person could feel: light on a hospital bed, heat in a room, a roof over a frightened family.

Some of the aid had arrived. Some was still lost at sea. Some might reach the people who needed it only because a captain whose ship had sunk was unwilling to let the paperwork die with her.

I stopped at Northstar's gangway.

Freyja shook my hand.

"Thank you for coming."

"Thank you for the work you do."

She looked at the ship's scarred side.

"Will you sail again?"

"Not soon. She needs repairs."

"And after that?"

I thought of the sailors still aboard, the ones in hospital, the families waiting in America, and a war that did not care whether we were tired.

"I don't know."

It was the truth.

For once, the answer didn't feel like a failure.
