**THE CORRIDOR OF LIGHTS — 1941 HOURS**

The first friendly escort looked almost exactly like an enemy ship.

A narrow gray shape emerged through the bad weather beyond our forward quarter. She had the familiar proportions of a warship: a long, low hull and a superstructure that seemed to rise from the sea without ever becoming part of it. Through the spray, there were no visible flags. No faces. Only steel moving toward us.

The tactical identification was confirmed before I allowed myself to believe what I was seeing.

USS Jefferson.

I had met her captain, Commander Rachel Ward—not related to Eileen Ward—during a training visit the previous year. She was quick with figures and slow with jokes, the sort of officer who made you check every assumption before opening your mouth.

Now she came over the radio sounding tired but unmistakably alive.

"Northstar, Jefferson Actual. We have your formation in sight."

"Good to see you, Rachel."

"Not the first time I've been told that, Daniel. Usually it doesn't sound so desperate."

"Usually you arrive with better weather."

She laughed once.

Then her tone changed.

"We've reviewed your latest reports. We'll assume assigned escort duties once coordination is complete. I understand you've got casualties."

"Yes. One dead aboard, several injured. Medical support will be needed. Cutlass is damaged and treating survivors from the freighter we lost."

There was a pause.

"I'm sorry," she said.

I looked at the British frigate on the edge of our display.

"Thank you."

"Have you had a chance to sleep?"

"Apparently I'm required to answer that question on every circuit today."

"That bad?"

"Worse."

"Then we'll get the defensive responsibilities settled and give you room to breathe."

I wanted to accept her offer as a promise.

Instead I said, "We'll do it safely."

"Of course."

Jefferson moved into position under established coalition procedures. I watched the coordination unfold without trying to micromanage it. Idris discussed the confirmed threat picture and known limitations with his counterpart. Navigation worked with the other ship's bridge team. Communications agreed on verified channels and procedures that would not depend on unreliable wider networks.

There were no grand speeches.

The relief consisted of people carefully making the same facts mean the same thing to two different ships.

At 1958, a Norwegian officer came over the coalition circuit. His frigate was approaching from another sector and had received word that one escort had suffered a significant propulsion casualty.

"Northstar, this is Fenrir. Do you require immediate assistance with machinery?"

I looked toward Navarro's latest report.

"Negative immediate assistance, Fenrir. We have reduced capability, but the surviving propulsion plant is stable within limits."

"Understood. Our technical team can prepare support for arrival."

"Appreciated."

He paused.

"Your convoy has caused much discussion ashore, Commander."

"Hopefully about the supplies."

"About the children."

I stared at the radio.

"Children?"

"Word has reached the receiving authorities that the ferry carries families evacuated from several damaged towns. There are volunteers assembling at the harbor. Many have children of their own."

For a moment I thought of Liv again, holding a red crayon above a ruined paper house.

"I'm glad they're ready."

"They are. And they asked us to tell the escorts that the harbor lights will be on."

I couldn't answer immediately.

"Thank you," I said at last.

The Norwegian officer acknowledged and returned to his ship.

I looked at Mara.

"Did he really say the harbor lights will be on?"

"Yes."

She smiled, and there was something exhausted and tender in it.

"Sounds like a good reason to keep moving."

I nodded.

It had been almost two days since I'd seen anything that made me think of safety. A harbor was not safety by itself; war could reach ports as easily as ships. But a harbor with people prepared to help was different from open water. It was a place where somebody else could carry part of the burden.

For the first time I allowed myself to imagine Aurora tied alongside. Ambulances waiting on the pier. Volunteers with blankets. Children eating something warm without having to listen for alarms. Caledonia's survivors moving into real hospital rooms instead of cramped compartments aboard a damaged frigate.

The thought almost hurt.

Idris interrupted.

"Captain, we're reviewing how to transfer escort responsibility without gaps in coverage. One of our systems is still intermittent."

"Take the time you need."

"Yes, sir."

I returned to Combat.

The room was quieter than it had been during the missile engagements, but no one was relaxed. Every operator understood that a mistake during relief could be just as consequential as a mistake during an attack. We had more friendly ships on the picture now, which meant more coordination and a greater need to separate trustworthy reports from assumptions.

Farouk stood beside a secondary display.

"Captain, the theater intelligence picture is improving, but there are gaps in the reports from other convoys."

"What are we hearing?"

"Some have reached safe waters. At least one suffered losses. Several reports remain unverified."

I looked at her.

"Any news of Hawthorne?"

"Not yet in a form I would present as confirmed."

"Thank you for not dressing that up."

She gave a slight nod.

"You're welcome, sir."

I noticed an extra coffee cup by her chair. It was cold.

"Have you eaten?"

"Not recently."

"Then get somebody to relieve you."

"Captain, we're in the middle of—"

"I know exactly what we're in the middle of. That is why I need you capable of thinking."

She sighed.

"Aye, sir."

As she left, she muttered something about every senior officer turning into a mother during deployments. I chose not to hear it.

A few minutes later, a junior officer from Jefferson sent a short message over the established link. It was directed personally to me.

*Commander Ward says the coffee is better on her ship, but she has prepared extra just in case.*

I laughed.

Cross, passing behind me, looked up.

"Good news?"

"Apparently the Navy's biggest strategic asset has finally arrived."

"Coffee?"

"Coffee."

"Then we may yet win this war."

The humor lasted only a moment, but it gave the watchstanders a chance to smile without feeling disloyal to the people we'd lost.

At 2030, Jefferson confirmed the planned handover arrangements. Fenrir acknowledged its responsibilities. Northstar remained part of the formation, but we were no longer carrying the largest share of the defensive burden.

I felt the weight shift—not disappear, merely become bearable.

Then Jefferson's captain called privately.

"Daniel, there's something you should know."

I waited.

"We've been told your crew is being discussed in the regional command briefings. The convoy's survival may change how the next evacuation is organized."

"That's good, if it helps."

"It will. But I want you to be prepared. People are going to ask you to explain what happened."

I looked at the exhausted faces around me.

"Most of them won't like the answer."

"Then give them the real one."

I thought of Caledonia's sinking position, Ramirez's empty place at the table, and the uncertain enemy contact we had refused to label destroyed.

"I intend to."

There was a pause.

"Daniel?"

"Yes?"

"You got them this far."

I looked toward Aurora.

"Not yet."

She understood.

"Then let's finish it."

At 2112 the Norwegian frigate appeared on the bridge camera, a second friendly shape moving through the rain. For a moment the three warships formed a rough triangle around the surviving merchants.

I watched them through salt-streaked glass.

In every direction beyond the convoy lay ocean. I knew hostile ships and submarines might still be out there. I knew the war would continue after our passengers stepped ashore.

But for one narrow stretch of water, the people carrying out the same duty had finally found one another.

I did not call it victory.

I called it company.

And after two days in the dark, company was enough.
