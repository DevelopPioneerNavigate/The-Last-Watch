**THE PICTURE IS NOT THE SEA — 0604 HOURS**

A missile on a display has no sound. It has a designation, a position, and a collection of numbers that update faster than human thought. There may be warning colors. There may be projected paths. None of it explains what it means for a person standing outside under that same patch of sky.

Our combat system fused information from several sensors, each with different strengths and limitations. It was easy to admire the result. It was also easy to forget that the result was an interpretation, not the ocean itself.

"Track correlation changing," the air-defense officer said.

"What changed?"

"Possible duplication resolved. One track now assessed as two distinct threats."

We had gained a missile without anyone firing one.

"Adjust the count. Notify Cutlass."

"Aye."

The young sailor operating the weapons coordination display was Petty Officer Third Class Daniel Keene. He had a narrow face and a habit of biting the inside of his cheek when concentrating. I knew his name because he had once asked me, during a crew forum, why every senior officer described the combat system as a tool and then blamed the operators when the tool was wrong.

"Because senior officers need somebody to blame," I had told him.

He had laughed.

Then I added, "Which is why you tell your supervisors when the picture doesn't match what you're seeing. No one aboard gets punished for reporting uncertainty honestly."

Now Keene was watching a track that could threaten Aurora, and I wondered whether my answer had been good enough.

He called a discrepancy. A secondary indication did not agree with the primary source.

Idris considered it, then asked for a fresh correlation.

For several seconds the result remained uncertain.

Those seconds mattered. A false duplicate could waste an irreplaceable defensive weapon. An actual second missile dismissed as a duplicate could kill hundreds.

We had rehearsed those choices in simulation. In simulations, when an exercise ended, the dead came back the next morning and the missiles reappeared in their cells. At sea, ammunition did not replenish because the instructor reset the problem.

The weapons teams were working inside a framework of authorized defenses, threat assessments, safety checks, and human judgment. It was not a simple case of pressing a button at every red symbol. There were layers of decisions, one built on the last, and every layer had to keep pace with weapons that did not care whether we understood them.

I moved behind Keene's station.

"How are you holding up?"

"Working, Captain."

"That's not quite what I asked."

He looked at me for half a second. "Scared, sir."

"Good answer. Keep reporting what you see."

A faint alarm sounded from another display. The ship was maneuvering. A series of small advisories appeared as equipment adjusted to the new motion. My mind filed each one away until there was no room left.

Across the communications circuit, Cutlass acknowledged its assigned area. Mallory sounded infuriatingly relaxed.

"Your lads have the eastern threats nicely in hand," he said. "We'll keep an eye on the rest."

"Don't admire the display too long, Edward."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

Then we lost the first hostile track and picked up confirmation that it had been defeated.

A moment later a second disappeared. A third persisted.

I watched a sailor close his eyes for a fraction of a second before opening them again to call the next engagement. It was not a failure of discipline. It was a human body trying to make sense of something the body had not evolved to endure.

"Captain," Idris said, "we're approaching the limits of reliable long-range correlation in this weather."

"What are you confident about?"

"That there are multiple hostile weapons inbound. Their exact number and geometry may change."

"Then keep refining it. Don't build the next decision on a count you no longer believe."

A violent roll made somebody catch a coffee cup before it slid from a console. I could hear the ship working around us, the groan of steel and the faint vibration of machinery. Somewhere above, lookout teams strained their eyes into the freezing spray. They would probably never see a missile until it was close enough to endanger them, and they kept watching anyway.

That is one of the oldest duties in the Navy: stand where you are told and look for danger even when technology seems to have taken the responsibility away from you.

Then the defensive layer engaged again. Through the bridge camera, a white flash briefly appeared above the horizon.

I thought of the children on Aurora. I thought of the difference between a track labeled *defeated* and a ship filled with living people.

On the display, another hostile symbol vanished.

Keene called it calmly.

"Threat no longer tracked."

"Is that confirmation of destruction?" I asked.

"Not by itself, Captain. We're checking."

"Good."

The person who knows what he does not know is more valuable in combat than the person who never admits doubt.

When the last incoming weapon had been accounted for, Keene leaned back just enough to take a breath. I could see the bite mark inside his cheek when he spoke.

"Captain, all assigned threats cleared."

"Well done."

He started to smile and then seemed to think better of it.

I knew why.

Somewhere behind us, Caledonia was gone. Our defense of the living did nothing to bring back the dead. The crew understood that without being told.

In the minutes after the attack I called every department for status. Not just weapons. Not just sensors. The deck teams, cooks, engineers, medical personnel, and repair lockers all had to continue their jobs while the rest of the ship celebrated a narrow survival.

There was a faint ringing in my ears that stayed with me until evening.

I never found out whether it came from the launches, exhaustion, or fear.
