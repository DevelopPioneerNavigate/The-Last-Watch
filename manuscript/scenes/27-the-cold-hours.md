**THE COLD HOURS — 1908 HOURS**

By 1908 the storm had stripped the ocean of any useful color.

On the bridge camera, the world existed in three shades: black sea, gray spray, and the occasional white breaking wave. Every time Northstar met a swell, the bow vanished for a moment behind water rising higher than the deck. Then the ship lifted, engines steady, and reappeared.

I had been awake again for more than two hours, long enough to regret the short sleep and not long enough to feel much better for having taken it.

Mara was reviewing a report when I entered Combat.

"How's the weather?" she asked.

"Intemperate."

"Very precise."

"The technical term is worse than unpleasant."

She smiled and handed me the latest convoy status.

Meridian had reduced speed slightly. Atlas Mariner was holding course. Aurora was struggling with passengers injured by the ship's motion. None of the reports amounted to a fresh crisis, but the combined picture demanded attention.

I called Lindholm.

"Peter, how are you managing?"

His reply was strained.

"We've moved some passengers away from the outer lounges. The doctor has seen more injuries from falls. One child is having difficulty breathing. We have a volunteer nurse with him."

"Can your medical team manage?"

"We think so. But we're asking whether there is a better time to request specialist advice."

I looked toward Okafor, who had been called into Combat for a medical coordination briefing.

"Leah, can you assist without leaving your patients?"

"I can talk to the ferry's doctor," she said. "I can't diagnose anyone over a radio, but we can discuss what resources they've got and whether a transfer is actually necessary."

"Do it."

She took the medical circuit.

For several minutes, I listened to people doing something that made me proud of our ship in an entirely different way from firing weapons.

There was no dramatic rescue maneuver. No helicopter launch into impossible weather. Okafor and the ferry's doctor compared available information, identified practical limitations, and arranged to keep monitoring the child while the escorts worked toward safer waters. They agreed a transfer at sea would be far more dangerous than remaining aboard unless the situation changed considerably.

When the conversation ended, Okafor looked at me.

"They have a plan. They also have a capable nurse and a captain who is taking the passengers seriously."

"Good."

She hesitated.

"Daniel—Captain."

I raised an eyebrow.

"I need to tell you something that isn't a medical report."

"Go ahead."

"The crew keeps asking how long the danger will last. Some are having trouble sleeping even when they're exhausted. Others are staying busy because if they stop moving, they start remembering."

I looked away.

"That sounds familiar."

"It should."

She folded her arms.

"I've heard people saying they ought to be grateful to be alive and therefore shouldn't be upset about anything else."

"That's not how grief works."

"No. It isn't."

"What can we do now?"

"Keep the watch rotations sensible. Let people talk to one another. Don't mistake emotional shutdown for readiness. And when we reach port, make sure nobody slips through the cracks just because they haven't been wounded visibly."

I nodded.

"And you?"

She seemed surprised.

"Me?"

"You've been taking care of everybody else."

She looked down at her hands.

"I haven't really thought about myself."

"Then start."

A small, tired smile.

"That's what I keep telling other people."

"Good. Try your own advice."

I asked Mara to ensure the medical team had proper rest opportunities as soon as the immediate demands allowed. She agreed without argument, which told me she had already been thinking the same thing.

A report arrived from the bridge: heavy rolling on the next leg, and a concern from Atlas Mariner about securing equipment on a weather deck. The master had already directed his crew to avoid unnecessary exposure.

I acknowledged the report.

Then I stood for a minute listening to the sea against the hull.

There is a quality to storms that feels almost intentional. They arrive while you are weak. They find every loose fitting and every tired muscle. They make small mistakes expensive.

But the weather was not cruel. Cruelty requires intention. The sea simply existed, indifferent to what human beings wanted.

I found that thought less comforting than people expected.

At 1936, Cross called from the mess decks.

"Captain, are you free for a minute?"

"Depends."

"Then it'll be two."

He had gathered a handful of sailors in a small space away from the traffic of the watch stations. They were not conducting a formal meeting. They were simply talking. Some had taken part in the engineering fire. Others had watched the missile engagements from their duty positions. One sailor had lost a friend aboard a ship elsewhere in the North Atlantic and learned about it through a delayed message.

I stood at the doorway, uncertain whether my presence would help.

Cross noticed me.

"Captain, come in if you want. Nobody needs to stand at attention."

I entered.

The young signalman whose daughter had learned to walk was there. He was holding a folded letter between his hands.

"We were talking about what we want to do when we get home," he said.

"Good subject."

"What about you, Captain?"

The question surprised me.

I thought of Rebecca and Ethan. Of our porch. Of the sound of grass being cut in the neighborhood. Of a cup of coffee I could finish without somebody reporting another threat.

"I want to sit outside and listen to absolutely nothing important," I said.

They laughed.

"What about you?" I asked him.

"I want to see my daughter walk."

Another sailor said he wanted to order a pizza and eat it without anybody calling general quarters. One wanted to sleep for two days. A third admitted he wanted to tell his father he was afraid and had been afraid for longer than he could remember.

The room went quiet.

Cross leaned forward.

"You think your father won't understand?"

"I don't know."

"Then give him a chance."

The sailor nodded.

I stayed for fifteen minutes. I listened more than I spoke. Nobody asked for a speech. Nobody wanted to be told they were heroes. They wanted to know that being afraid did not disqualify them from being sailors.

When I left, a faint melody was coming from somebody's phone. It was a recording sent by a family member before the war, a child singing badly and enthusiastically. The others listened without embarrassment.

I returned to Combat and found Bell still at her station.

"Anything?"

"Nothing new, Captain."

That was a remarkable phrase. I had almost forgotten how to accept it.

Mara looked up.

"You seem better."

"I spoke to the crew."

"Good."

"They want pizza and sleep."

"Reasonable strategic objectives."

"And one wants to see his daughter walk."

Mara's smile faded.

"We should get them home."

I looked toward the tactical plot. Our three civilians were moving west. The hostile contacts had become uncertain in the weather. Friendly support was still distant but no longer entirely imaginary.

"Yes," I said.

"We should."

At 2050, I ordered another status review. The watch teams reported their findings. No one claimed the storm had become safe. No one pretended the submarine could not return. The ship was operating within her remaining limits.

Outside, the water continued beating against the hull.

Inside, sailors made coffee, checked equipment, tended wounded shipmates, and counted the hours until land.

That was our victory for the moment.

Not a ship sunk.

Not an enemy driven away.

Just another hour with everyone still moving toward home.
