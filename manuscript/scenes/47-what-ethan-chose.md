**WHAT ETHAN CHOSE — TWO YEARS AFTER THE CROSSING**

My son told me he wasn't joining the Navy over a plate of burnt toast.

We were sitting in our kitchen on a Sunday morning. The yard was wet from overnight rain, and the repaired fence beyond the window leaned just enough to remind us who had built it.

Ethan was eighteen, broad-shouldered, and trying to make a decision about the rest of his life as though the rest of his life had agreed to cooperate.

He had spent the past six months talking about the Naval Academy. Not because I had asked him to. He'd looked at the requirements, visited a campus, and spoken to people who knew what service demanded. He had even begun preparing the paperwork.

Now he was turning a piece of toast over with his fork.

"Dad, I think I'm going to apply for engineering school."

I looked up.

"All right."

He waited.

I took a sip of coffee.

"What?"

"That's it?"

"What's the alternative? Arrest you?"

He gave a nervous laugh.

"I thought you'd be disappointed."

I set down the cup.

"Why?"

"Because you served. Granddad served. You keep talking about people who did the right thing when it mattered."

I looked through the window at the crooked fence.

"Do you think doing the right thing requires a uniform?"

He hesitated.

"No."

"Then why would I be disappointed?"

He pushed the toast aside.

"Because I don't want to be you."

The words were out before either of us could prepare for them.

I looked at him.

He went red.

"I didn't mean it like that."

"Tell me how you meant it."

He looked toward the doorway, perhaps hoping Rebecca would come in and rescue the conversation. She had gone to the grocery store and was unlikely to return soon.

"I mean I don't want to spend my life away from people. I don't want to wait for calls that might not come. I don't want to make decisions where somebody dies no matter what I choose."

I could hear the careful control in his voice. He wasn't rejecting me. He was telling me what the years of deployment had cost him.

I looked down at the table.

"That's reasonable."

"Really?"

"Yes."

He frowned.

"You always talk about the Navy like it made you who you are."

"It did."

"Then how can you say it's reasonable for me not to go?"

I thought for a moment.

"Because I also know what it took from us."

He looked up.

For years I'd been the father on a screen, the voice delayed by connections, the photograph standing beside a bed. My son's memories included birthdays I had missed, dinners where Rebecca had left a place for me out of habit, and a night when he thought my ship had been sunk.

I had never asked him what that night felt like.

"Can I ask you something?" I said.

"Sure."

"When we were out there, during the convoy, what did you hear?"

He stared at the toast.

"That there'd been attacks. Mom didn't want to tell me more until she knew."

"And what did you think?"

He was quiet.

"I thought you were dead."

I felt the words as a physical blow.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. That's the problem. Nobody told me anything."

He rubbed his thumb against the edge of the plate.

"Mom kept saying we had to wait for confirmation. I knew she was trying to be calm. But the longer we waited, the more I thought she was protecting me from what she already knew."

I had commanded a ship through missiles and fire and a sea violent enough to pull men off their feet. I could not remember feeling as helpless as I did listening to my own son describe our kitchen.

"I'm sorry," I said.

He nodded.

"I know."

"I should have talked to you about it sooner."

"Probably."

The answer was honest enough to sting.

We sat in silence.

Then Ethan said, "I do want to build things."

"What kind of things?"

"Systems that actually help people. Power systems. Emergency equipment. Stuff that works when everything else goes wrong."

I smiled faintly.

"That sounds familiar."

He looked at me.

"Not weapons. Not ships. I mean things for hospitals and shelters. I saw those reports about the supplies your convoy was carrying. There were people waiting for generators and equipment because they'd lost power."

"Caledonia."

"Yeah."

I looked at him more closely.

"You've been reading about it?"

"Some of what was released."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because every time somebody mentions that ship, you get this look."

I lowered my eyes.

"Fair enough."

He took a breath.

"I was thinking maybe I could work on emergency power systems. Make sure places don't depend on just one truck or one ship getting through."

For a moment I could barely speak.

The world had spent years telling me that my work mattered because it happened aboard a warship. My son was sitting across from me describing a different kind of service, one that might save people without ever crossing an ocean under fire.

"That's important work," I said.

"Do you mean that, or are you being nice?"

"I mean it."

He nodded slowly.

"Okay."

I watched him pick up the toast again.

"Do you still want to visit the Academy?" I asked.

"Maybe. Just to see it."

"Then go. Looking at a place doesn't mean you owe it your life."

He smiled.

"Mom said you'd say something like that."

"Did she?"

"She said you were going to be impossible about the engineering program, though."

"How?"

"She thinks you'll start making spreadsheets."

I considered it.

"That's a fair concern."

He laughed.

We had another cup of coffee. Then he showed me the engineering programs he was considering, along with a sketch he'd made of a modular emergency generator system that could be moved into position when a hospital's primary power failed.

It was crude, missing half the difficult parts, but there was real thought behind it.

I asked what he'd do if a component failed.

He had answers for some things. Not for others.

I resisted the temptation to turn breakfast into a board examination.

"That's a good start," I said.

He looked relieved.

For the next hour we talked about schools, costs, coursework, and how little either of us knew about the kind of career he might eventually build. I found I enjoyed not knowing. It meant the future still had room for him to invent something of his own.

After he went upstairs, I stood at the kitchen sink looking out toward the yard.

The fence I'd promised to repair was still slightly crooked. Ethan had helped me build it after I came home. We'd argued about measurements, cut a post too short, and laughed so hard Rebecca had taken a photograph.

It was one of my favorite pictures.

I used to think my son would remember the destroyer, the medals, and the years his father spent commanding sailors.

Now I hoped he'd remember the fence.

I went into my study and opened the unfinished manuscript of The Last Watch.

For months I'd been writing about duty, courage, and the choices made by captains. I had written about Ramirez, Ward, Mallory, and the men and women who carried the convoy through darkness.

I had not written much about the people who stayed home.

I added a page about Rebecca listening to rumors without knowing whether I was alive. I wrote about Ethan standing in the kitchen, waiting for a call. I wrote that service can be honorable and costly at the same time, and that children have the right to choose a different path from the one their parents walked.

Then I added a line I hoped Ethan would someday read.

*The sea taught me to protect lives I could not see. My son taught me that some of those lives had been waiting for me at home.*

I saved the page.

From upstairs came the sound of music, too loud for my taste and badly played on the guitar Ethan had bought secondhand.

I almost shouted for him to turn it down.

Instead I went to the foot of the stairs.

"Ethan?"

The music stopped.

"Yeah?"

"Want to help me check the fence later?"

A pause.

"Again?"

"It's leaning."

"That's because you measured it."

I smiled.

"Then perhaps I need an engineer."

He laughed from upstairs.

And for once, the sound in my house was not a radio warning or an alarm or a call coming too late.

It was simply my son.

Home.

Alive.

Choosing his own future.
