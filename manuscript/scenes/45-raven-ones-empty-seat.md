**RAVEN ONE'S EMPTY SEAT — 19 NOVEMBER, 1658 HOURS**

I found Samira Brooks sitting in the darkened hangar, staring at a helmet that did not belong to her.

It lay on a maintenance bench, its visor raised and a strip of white tape fixed along the side. Somebody had written a surname in black marker. I recognized it only after she'd turned the helmet toward me.

WELLS.

"Is Owen all right?" I asked.

Brooks glanced up.

"Sleeping, Captain."

"Then why do you look like you're waiting for somebody to tell you he isn't?"

She did not answer immediately.

A helicopter hangar has a different silence from a bridge. The bridge is full of motion and anticipation. A hangar holds machinery that seems to be waiting for a person to make it alive. Tools rest in their trays. Tie-downs stretch across the floor. The helicopter itself appears almost embarrassed by how motionless it has become.

Raven One's rotor blades were secured. Her skin showed the dirt and salt of hard flying. A maintainer worked near an access panel, turning a flashlight as though the compartment contained a secret.

Brooks folded her arms.

"Owen was supposed to call his wife before the sortie. He put it off because we were behind schedule. Then we spent hours in the air wondering whether we'd be shot down."

"Has he called her now?"

"Not yet. Communications won't promise delivery."

I looked at the helmet.

"That happens."

"I know."

She exhaled.

"I don't know why it's bothering me so much."

I sat on an equipment case.

"Maybe because you've had time to think about it."

"Maybe."

She turned the helmet again.

"Before we launched, he made a joke about a meal he'd promised to cook when he got home. Some terrible pasta thing. I told him nobody would want to eat it. He said his wife would because she loves him."

She gave a quiet laugh.

"Then we were airborne. We lost the visible horizon. The weather was getting worse. Every contact was uncertain and every correction felt important. Somewhere below us Caledonia went down."

Her voice changed at the ship's name.

"We heard fragments over the radio. Not everything. Enough."

I waited.

"I kept thinking there would be some way we could help," she said. "And there wasn't. Not safely, not with the equipment and position we had. By the time we got back, people were already dead."

"Samira—"

"I know the answer. We were assigned a search. We provided the information we could. We brought the aircraft back. I know."

She looked at me sharply.

"But I also know that when you're flying, the world gets very small. Your crew. Your fuel. Your instruments. The weather. There can be a ship full of people dying somewhere beyond that little world, and you keep doing your checks because that's the only way anybody goes home."

I thought of the twelve tracks in Combat, of my own fixation on the display.

"That's true on a destroyer too."

"I suppose it is."

The maintainer closed the access panel with a soft metallic click. He glanced toward us, decided not to intrude, and returned to his work.

Brooks set the helmet on the bench.

"Captain, do you think we'll fly again before Iceland?"

"That depends on the aircraft, the weather, and the mission. Navarro's team is working with reduced capability. We're not taking risks to make ourselves feel useful."

She nodded.

"Good."

"Would you tell me if you disagreed?"

"Yes."

I smiled.

"That's why I asked."

She looked toward Raven One.

"The younger crew keep asking whether they're allowed to be afraid after they've already survived."

"What do you tell them?"

"That fear isn't a switch you turn off when the danger moves away."

"Sounds right."

She sat beside me on the case.

"I didn't always know that."

"Neither did I."

For a while we listened to the hangar ventilation.

Then she asked, "What happens when we get home?"

"To the aircraft?"

"To us."

I considered it.

"Some will need medical treatment. Some will need rest. We'll make reports and answer questions. Maybe we'll be sent out again after the ship's ready. Maybe we'll have more time than we expect."

She looked unconvinced.

"I don't mean the Navy's answer."

I took a breath.

"I hope you get to sit at a kitchen table and hear about the things your family did while you were away. I hope they get annoyed with you for putting a cup in the wrong place. I hope there are days when nobody needs you to be brave."

She stared at her hands.

"That sounds nice."

"It does."

A radio squawked from the maintenance station.

One of the sailors answered, listened, and looked toward us.

"Captain. Aviation relay. Friendly reconnaissance traffic requesting our latest safe contact status."

Brooks rose immediately.

"That's mine."

"Take it."

She went to the communications terminal, checked the origin of the message with the sailor, and began exchanging a concise account of what Raven One had observed and what remained uncertain. She separated facts from recollections; I could hear Bell's influence in her words.

When she'd finished, she turned toward me.

"There's an Allied aircraft farther west working toward the corridor. They're collecting verified observations to help the relief units find us."

"Good news."

"Maybe the first good news in a while."

I followed her to the hangar opening, where the sea was visible through a narrow safe viewing area. The water was slate-colored and broken into irregular white lines. The wind had not eased.

"We didn't get all the answers during the sortie," she said.

"No."

"But somebody else might get the next piece."

"That's how the job works."

She looked toward the horizon.

"Captain, if the aircraft reaches us, I want to make sure the information we send doesn't tell them we've cleared threats we never confirmed."

"Agreed."

"I want them to know Cutlass is damaged. And that Caledonia's survivors are aboard. And that the merchants can't maneuver like us."

"Make sure all of it gets to the right people."

She nodded.

Then she stopped.

"One more thing."

"Go ahead."

"Somebody has been asking if Raven One's crew can help carry a memorial flag when we reach port."

"For Ramirez?"

"Yes."

I thought of Elena on the deck before the war, correcting a sailor's emergency equipment check, her expression fierce and amused.

"That's something the family and the command team should arrange together," I said. "But I can't imagine she'd object to having the aviators there."

Brooks smiled faintly.

"She always complained we took too much maintenance space."

"She complained about everybody."

"Yes."

Then the smile vanished.

"Captain, I never thanked her for helping our detachment after the last inspection."

"She knew you valued her work."

"I hope so."

I looked at the helicopter.

"Tell the people she taught. That's how you thank her now."

Brooks nodded.

We walked back toward the hangar bench. She picked up Wells's helmet and carried it to the correct locker, setting it carefully beside her own.

"Samira," I said.

She turned.

"When Owen wakes up, tell him to call his wife."

She gave me a look of exhausted disbelief.

"He'll say communications aren't working."

"Then he can record the message. It will be there when the connection returns."

"I'll tell him."

"And ask what kind of pasta he was planning."

"Why?"

"If we all get home, I want to know whether it was really that bad."

For the first time that afternoon she laughed without having to force it.

Then the aviation relay called again. More verified positions. A better route picture. A friendly aircraft moving east toward the convoy.

I left her working with her team, the hangar no longer quite so silent.

Raven One might not leave the deck again before Iceland.

But her crew was still flying a watch of its own—helping another aircraft reach us, making sure somebody else's arrival did not depend on the kind of guess that had cost so many lives.

There are many ways to bring a ship home.

Some begin in the air.

Others begin when a tired pilot stops staring at an empty seat and remembers that she still has people to lead.
