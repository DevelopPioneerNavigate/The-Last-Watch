# THE LAST WATCH
## Extended Manuscript — Part II

# CHAPTER EIGHT — THE ORDER TO FIRE

**1023 hours**

A captain is taught that the use of force must be lawful, necessary, and grounded in reliable information. Those words sound orderly in a classroom. They are harder to hold onto after your ship has absorbed a missile attack and the civilians behind you have spent hours wondering which explosion will be the last thing they hear.

We had a developing picture of the enemy surface group. Our combat system showed intermittent contacts and electronic emissions, but bad weather and hostile interference made the information uneven. More than one hostile ship appeared to have contributed to the strikes against our convoy.

I was angry. Angry at the lost Caledonia sailors. Angry at the damage to Cutlass. Angry that people who had already fled burning cities were being pursued across an ocean.

Anger was not a justification to fire.

{{scene:08-the-space-before-fire.md}}

I had Idris lead the evaluation. He reviewed identification evidence, the hostile conduct already observed, and the requirements of our standing orders. A second officer independently challenged the assessment. We checked the picture for neutral shipping, friendly aircraft, and other possible mistakes.

When Idris finally looked at me, he was tired enough that the words sounded old.

"Captain, the engagement criteria have been met. We recommend an authorized strike against the identified hostile combatant."

"Any unresolved identification concerns?"

"None that alter the recommendation."

"Any known protected or neutral vessels in the engagement area?"

"None identified."

I turned to Mara.

She did not nod. She did not encourage me. She simply met my eyes, reminding me that even a justified decision belonged to the person who made it.

"Execute the engagement," I said.

Northstar fired offensive missiles.

The launches were fierce enough to rattle coffee cups in the wardroom. Then our weapons vanished over the horizon and there was nothing to do but wait for information that might remain incomplete forever.

I imagined a second combat information center aboard the enemy warship. Perhaps its captain was the same age as me. Perhaps a young sailor there had written home that morning. Perhaps someone had put a photograph of a daughter in the corner of a console.

I did not want them dead.

I wanted them to stop attacking the convoy.

Several minutes later, the enemy surface picture changed. One identified hostile vessel ceased transmitting its previously detected emissions. A second turned away from its prior course. There were indicators of an effective strike, but not enough to make a confident damage claim.

"Hostile group coordination appears disrupted," Idris reported.

"Do not count the ship sunk," I said. "Keep tracking."

Mara joined me.

"Whatever happened over there, their next attack may be delayed."

"That's what matters."

"You all right?"

I looked at her. "Ask me after the war."

She rested a hand briefly on my shoulder.

"Fair enough."

As we assessed the aftermath of the strike, we kept reorganizing the convoy, reviewing the ice-avoidance route, and preparing for deteriorating weather. Cutlass remained afloat, her crew fighting fires and treating casualties. We had neither the space nor time to celebrate a tactical success.

{{scene:30-the-ship-we-could-not-see.md}}

At 1048, Chief Bell made another report.

"High-speed underwater contact, bearing south. Possible torpedo."

The enemy had found a way to remind us that every dimension of the ocean remained dangerous.

Northstar maneuvered. Defensive procedures were executed. The contact changed relative bearing, then a dull concussion traveled through the hull. We heard it as a heavy, muffled boom beneath a floor full of equipment.

"Underwater detonation!"

"Damage?"

"No immediate damage reported by the convoy."

"Source?"

"Unknown. Weapon contact lost."

We had barely begun to establish that no one had been struck when an alarm arrived from engineering. The port propulsion system had suffered a major casualty. Fire indications appeared in an associated machinery area. Smoke was reported in a cable passage.

And four people could not be accounted for.

One of them was Jacob Rios.

# CHAPTER NINE — FIRE BELOW THE WATERLINE

**1102 hours**

A warship on fire at sea is a terrifying thing.

The sea surrounds you with more water than any city could imagine, yet none of that changes the danger of smoke moving down a narrow passageway. Fire spreads through cableways and equipment spaces. The same steel that protects you can trap heat. The ship rolls, ladders shift beneath you, and even a familiar compartment becomes strange when the lights are obscured.

Engineering Central reported a failure in a port machinery area and a rapidly developing secondary fire. The affected systems had been isolated. Starboard propulsion was available, but our maneuvering flexibility was degraded at exactly the wrong moment.

"Names of the unaccounted for," I demanded.

Lieutenant Commander Luis Navarro, our chief engineer, read them to me.

"Chief Petty Officer Elena Ramirez. Petty Officer Second Class Ben Ochoa. Fireman Apprentice Jacob Rios. Fireman Lucas Price."

I knew them all.

Ramirez had a nine-year-old daughter named Sofia. Ochoa had twin daughters and complained that no amount of coffee could compensate for being outnumbered at home. Price played guitar badly and enthusiastically. Rios was the boy from Kansas who had asked whether he would see combat.

It is harder to keep your voice steady when casualties become names.

"Keep the boundaries secure," I told Navarro. "No uncontrolled entries. Rescue only when conditions permit."

"Aye, Captain. Teams are moving."

Mara leaned over the damage-control display. "If this gets into the adjoining equipment area, we may lose more than port propulsion."

"I know."

"Daniel, we need you commanding, not down there."

She was right. My instinct was to run to the smoke, to look for them myself. But a commanding officer who abandons the wider situation during a submarine threat may multiply the danger to everyone aboard.

We divided responsibilities. Navarro directed engineering and damage control, Mara coordinated the broader shipboard response, and Idris maintained the battle watch. I kept the overall tactical picture and listened to the reports arrive.

{{scene:53-the-door-with-no-handle.md}}

At 1116 we established communication with Ramirez.

Her voice came through a damaged circuit with a hard edge of static.

"Captain, Ramirez."

"Chief. Tell me your situation."

"We've got smoke and heat. Ochoa is injured. Rios and Price are with me. We have an obstructed access route."

"Can you move?"

"Working on it. We've isolated what we can."

I could hear coughing in the background. Someone was striking metal with a tool.

"Chief, the rescue team is coming from the safer side. Don't risk moving into an unknown area."

"Understood."

"Elena, I want you alive when we get to Iceland."

A faint laugh. "That makes two of us."

Then her voice changed.

"Captain, Rios wants you to know the remaining engines are still good. He's worried we're letting you down."

I closed my eyes. A nineteen-year-old boy trapped in smoke, concerned about the ship's propulsion.

"Tell him he is not letting anybody down. Tell all of them their orders are to survive."

"Will do."

The transmission broke.

For the next few minutes, every second took an entire minute to pass.

Our rescue teams found Price first. His ankle was broken and his lungs hurt, but he was breathing. Then they reached a section of passageway where Rios was helping Ochoa move despite burns on both hands.

They were recovered at 1128.

Ramirez remained inside.

The rescue team went back.

The fire had begun to weaken behind controlled boundaries. Temperatures were falling. At 1142, sailors emerged carrying Ramirez on a stretcher. She was unconscious and not breathing.

A medical team took her.

I listened to the attempt to resuscitate her over a casualty circuit that seemed to occupy every part of my mind. One of our medical personnel reported progress, then a setback. They continued treatment. I asked for no more information than they could reliably give.

At 1151 the fire was contained.

At 1204 Navarro reported the surviving propulsion plant stable.

At 1212 medical told me Chief Ramirez was dead.

The deck was still moving. The tactical display still showed three civilian ships. There were still reports to make, orders to give, threats to assess.

But for one long moment I saw nothing except a photograph from a command picnic. Ramirez, her wife, and their daughter Sofia standing beside a table with chocolate cake. The little girl had eaten three slices. Rebecca, my wife, had laughed about it for weeks.

Now I would someday have to look that child in the eyes.

Mara waited beside me.

"Elena?" she asked.

I nodded.

She placed one hand on the back of my shoulder. It was the smallest possible gesture, a way of acknowledging grief without permitting it to overtake the ship.

"She's gone," I said.

"I know."

We stood like that for a few seconds.

Then Idris called my name.

The watch continued.

# CHAPTER TEN — THE MEN WHO GO BELOW

**1230 hours**

Medical was crowded with damaged lives.

Ochoa lay unconscious, attended by a corpsman who had not left his side for nearly an hour. Price was propped up with his leg stabilized, trying to make jokes that nobody found funny. Rios sat on a treatment table with an oxygen mask and both hands wrapped in dressings.

He looked at me as I entered.

"Captain."

"As you were, Jacob."

His face was black with soot except where tears had cleared tracks down his cheeks. He looked older than the boy I had met before dawn, and terribly young at the same time.

"How are you?"

"Like somebody put me in a dryer with a toolbox."

"Sounds about right."

He smiled weakly. Then the smile disappeared.

"Chief Ramirez?"

I pulled up a chair.

He already knew. He wanted me to say it anyway.

"She didn't make it."

His eyes filled. He turned his head toward the bulkhead and tried to breathe through the pain.

"She came back for us," he whispered.

I waited.

"Ochoa couldn't walk. There was smoke everywhere. I lost the ladder. Chief could've gone out when the others did, but she came back and found us."

He wiped at his face with a bandaged wrist.

"She told me to hold Ochoa up and keep moving. Then she couldn't stand herself."

I listened to the sound of his breathing.

"I should've carried her."

"Jacob."

"I should have."

"You were nineteen years old, injured and trying to move another wounded sailor through a burning ship. You did not cause what happened."

He shook his head. Tears fell onto the blanket.

"The chief died."

"Yes," I said. "She did."

I did not tell him that everything was all right. It wasn't. I didn't tell him he had nothing to regret. Survivors often have regrets, even when they did everything anyone could ask.

Instead I said, "When you're ready, I want you to remember her for how she lived, not only how she died. And I want you to live the kind of life she would have been proud to see."

He stared at me.

Then nodded.

As I stood to leave, he asked, "Did we save the ship?"

I thought of Navarro and his engineers, the firefighters holding the boundaries, the medics, and the sailors who had entered the damaged spaces.

"Yes, Jacob. You all did."

I moved on to Ochoa. His condition was still critical, but there were hopeful signs. The corpsman briefed me without false comfort. Outside medical, a young sailor sat alone on the deck with her knees drawn up, staring at a pair of smoke-stained gloves. I recognized her as one of Ramirez's damage-control team.

I sat beside her for a minute.

Neither of us spoke.

Sometimes a commanding officer must give orders. Sometimes all he can do is refuse to let somebody grieve alone.

Back in CIC, I reviewed the day's events. The enemy had struck in layers: distraction, underwater attack, airborne missiles, information interference, surface pressure, and another suspected torpedo. Our ship had answered each threat, but the true achievement belonged to sailors in spaces the tactical plots never showed.

The damage-control crews did not see missiles on a display. They saw smoke. The engineers did not see the grand strategy. They saw alarms, pressure gauges and damaged equipment. The medics did not know whether we were winning. They saw a wounded man and tried to save him.

I had spent a career studying naval warfare. That afternoon I understood with fresh clarity that a warship survives not through one grand decision, but through hundreds of people making small correct decisions while afraid.

# CHAPTER ELEVEN — THE NIGHT THAT WOULD NOT END

**1546 hours**

By midafternoon the sea was rising.

A wall of weather moved across our route, heavy with low cloud, freezing spray, and long swells. Satellite updates were inconsistent, but our navigators had enough verified information to avoid known ice hazards and guide the merchants through a safer portion of the northern diversion.

We could not outrun the enemy in a conventional contest of speed. The merchant ships were too slow. We could, however, complicate the enemy's attempt to find and attack us.

The sea would do its part without favor.

Meridian struggled to maintain convoy speed. Atlas Mariner rolled heavily. Aurora reported more injuries from the violent movement, none then life-threatening. Cutlass kept moving under reduced capability while her crew restored enough equipment to remain useful.

I went to the bridge. Wind shook the windows. The sky was the color of bruised iron.

Lieutenant Aaron Bishop had the watch.

"Sir, visibility is now very poor."

"Keep us within safe maneuvering limits."

"Aye, Captain. We'll advise the merchants on speed adjustments."

I stayed longer than I needed to. The bridge was familiar. It smelled of hot electronics and wet wool. There was comfort in looking out at the sea, even one that had done so much to hurt us.

{{scene:11-the-weather-has-no-allies.md}}

Mara found me there.

"Daniel, when did you last sleep?"

"Recently."

"That's not a time."

"I had a nap yesterday."

"That's not an answer either."

I looked at her. "We're fighting a war."

"Yes. And I would prefer that the person commanding the ship still have enough judgment to recognize the fact."

"I can manage."

"You have been awake for more than thirty hours."

She waited until I looked at her.

"You trusted Chief Ramirez to do her job. You trusted Navarro. You trusted Idris and Bell and the bridge teams. Why won't you trust me?"

There was no answer that would have made me sound sensible.

"Forty-five minutes," I said.

"An hour."

"Forty-five."

"Done."

I left her in command and went to my cabin. It contained a narrow bunk, a desk, and photographs of my wife Rebecca and our son Ethan. He was sixteen when I deployed. Before I left, he had asked why I couldn't take a shore assignment.

I had answered with duty and service and all the words that sounded appropriate. Sitting in that cabin, I wished I had admitted how much I loved the sea, and how impossible it was to explain that love to somebody who only understood what the Navy took away.

I lay down and, for the first time since the battle began, cried quietly into my pillow.

I was not crying only for Ramirez. I was crying because I had nearly lost hundreds of people and could not be certain I would not lose them in the next hour. I was crying for Caledonia's missing sailors and for the unknown men aboard the enemy ship we had attacked. I was crying because I was exhausted and the responsibility had become heavier than my body could carry.

Then I slept.

Thirty-seven minutes later, Mara called.

"We have an update on Hawthorne."

I was already reaching for my boots. "Tell me."

"She's afloat. Badly damaged. Forty-one confirmed dead."

I sat back down.

"Hayes?"

"Alive. Wounded, but alive."

I closed my eyes.

Thank God, I thought. Then I thought of the forty-one sailors who had not survived and felt ashamed that gratitude and sorrow could arrive at the same time.

When I returned to CIC, the convoy was still moving through the storm. Friendly forces were organizing support west of us, delayed by the same weather that had complicated the enemy's surveillance.

We had not been forgotten.

Help was simply far away.

{{scene:27-the-cold-hours.md}}

**2218 hours**

Bell reported a weak submerged contact astern.

It appeared, disappeared, and returned on a slightly altered bearing. She could not confirm it was one of the submarines from the morning attacks, but the possibility was strong enough to maintain heightened vigilance.

"Why haven't they fired again?" I asked.

"Could be the conditions. Could be they're conserving weapons. Could be they've lost track of us and we're hearing only pieces of the picture."

"Could be waiting."

"Could be, Captain."

She studied me for a moment.

"Sir, do you ever get used to not knowing?"

"Not if you're paying attention."

She nodded as though that was the answer she wanted.

{{scene:19-a-longer-kind-of-dark.md}}

At 2311 the acoustic contact faded. We did not announce that the submarine had gone. We had no way to know. We maintained the required watches and moved west through black water while the sea pounded the hull.

For the first time in many hours, no one attacked us.

That silence felt like a gift I did not quite trust.

**19 November 2037 — 1832 hours**

The friendly aircraft appeared to the west just before evening.

Its radio voice was clear, confident and almost impossibly ordinary.

"Northstar, Guardian Two-One. We have your convoy."

I lifted the microphone.

"Guardian Two-One, Northstar Actual. Good to hear from you."

"Likewise, Captain. We've been looking forward to finding you. Additional friendlies are inbound."

The aviator's accent sounded American. After nearly two days of damaged circuits, hostile emissions and strained emergency calls, I had forgotten how comforting an ordinary American voice could be.

I looked at Mara.

She was smiling, and the sight seemed to surprise both of us.

{{scene:12-voices-from-the-west.md}}

Later, the American destroyer USS Jefferson and a Norwegian frigate approached from the secured corridor. Relief was finally arriving, though there were still hours of open water ahead of us.

{{scene:34-the-corridor-of-lights.md}}

With the escort responsibilities carefully handed over, I checked that all surviving civilian ships were accounted for and allowed the crew to move from sustained combat readiness toward appropriate transit watches. We had lost Caledonia and Ramirez. We had also brought hundreds of people this far. Both truths belonged in the same account.

Some sailors laughed. Others cried. A few simply stared at their hands, as though they had forgotten what stillness felt like.

At 2241 the lights of Iceland appeared.

Land began as a dull glow through cloud and spray. Then came scattered points along the shore, the black silhouette of a mountain, the faint shape of a harbor beyond the darkness.

I stood on the bridge wing with Mercer. Her injured arm was supported in a sling. She should have been sleeping, but she had asked to see the approach.

"Beautiful, isn't it?" she said.

"The most beautiful thing I've seen in a very long time."

She watched the lights.

"Captain, will people remember what happened?"

"Some."

"And the others?"

I thought of naval histories: battle arrows across maps, tonnage, missile counts, loss tables, bold-faced names. They would tell part of the truth and leave most of it out.

"Most people won't know what this felt like," I said. "But we will. And the people behind us might remember too."

A radio call arrived from Aurora.

Captain Lindholm requested permission to address our crew.

I gave it.

"United States Ship Northstar," he began. "We are approaching safety. Six hundred and twelve civilians aboard this ferry can see your ship."

I stared across the water. Aurora's windows were lit in long rows.

"Many of us lost our homes," Lindholm continued. "Some lost family members. Our children have spent days asking whether we would live to see land again."

I closed my eyes.

"They watched your vessel fight to keep them alive. They watched you stay with us when you could have sailed away."

His voice shook.

"Commander Vale, please tell your sailors that our children are alive because of them."

I had to take a breath before answering.

"I will, Captain."

Then he said, "Thank you for bringing us home."

I looked toward Aurora and saw movement at the windows. People were standing there, waving. A few held up small lights against the glass.

"It's been our honor," I said.

For a long time afterward, no one on the bridge spoke.

# CHAPTER THIRTEEN — THE EMPTY PLACE AT THE TABLE

**20 November 2037 — 0915 hours**

Northstar secured alongside in Reykjavík after sunrise.

The harbor was crowded with allied warships, merchant vessels, rescue craft, temporary medical facilities and sailors who had been at sea for too long. For the first time in days, the deck remained still beneath my boots.

I felt almost dizzy without the motion.

Medical teams waited on the pier. Ochoa went ashore first among our wounded. Price followed. Rios walked slowly, supported by another sailor, his bandaged hands held awkwardly in front of him.

Halfway down the gangway, Rios stopped and looked up toward the bridge.

He saluted.

I returned it.

He disappeared into the waiting crowd.

I watched until I could no longer see him.

Then I went below to prepare for the memorial service.

We gathered in the largest space available. Some sailors attended in person; others remained at necessary watches. A folded American flag rested beside a photograph of Chief Petty Officer Elena Ramirez. In the picture she was smiling, unaware that a photograph taken on a sunny afternoon would one day become the center of a ship's grief.

Our chaplain stood beside me.

I had prepared remarks. They were formal, correct, and useless.

I folded the paper.

"Shipmates," I began, "we came through something difficult. Some of our people have wounds that can be seen. Others will carry injuries no one else knows about."

I looked across the room.

"Chief Ramirez gave her life saving her shipmates. We should remember her courage. But we should also remember her laughter, the way she taught junior sailors, the pride she took in doing the small things correctly, and the family she loved."

Rios stood near the front. Tears ran silently down his cheeks.

"Courage is not an absence of fear," I said. "It's the choice to protect somebody else while fear is telling you to run."

I turned toward the photograph.

"Elena made that choice. We are alive to tell the story because of her."

A bell sounded.

The chaplain prayed.

We stood in silence.

Later, I wrote a message to my wife.

*Rebecca, I'm safe. The ship made it. The crew was extraordinary. We lost someone. You met Elena at the picnic—the chief whose little girl loved the chocolate cake. She saved our sailors. I wish I could tell you she survived.*

I stopped typing.

Then I added: *I miss you. I miss Ethan. I want to come home. I think I've had enough war for a lifetime.*

I sent the message and sat at my desk until the room grew dark.

{{scene:35-the-first-ledger.md}}

Outside, Iceland went on with its life. Cars moved through streets. People bought bread. Children went to school. It was a relief to see ordinary civilization carrying on, indifferent to our private catastrophe.

The Navy repaired our ship. Engineers examined the damaged machinery. Investigators recorded what could be learned. Reports were written and reviewed and reviewed again. Somewhere at headquarters, people counted missiles, measured response times, debated whether different maneuvers might have saved Caledonia.

Some of those questions were important.

Others could never be answered.

I was responsible for the decisions I had made. I would live with them. What I would not allow was for the story of that night to become only a table of weapons and probabilities.

There had been people inside those numbers.

# EPILOGUE — WHAT THE OCEAN KEPT

**Five years later — Norfolk, Virginia**

They ask me whether we won.

I tell them we completed our mission.

Sometimes the person asking is disappointed. They want a clean answer. They want the moment the enemy was defeated and a flag rose triumphantly against the sunrise.

War rarely offers that kind of ending.

Caledonia never returned. Her survivors carried memories that no port inspection could erase. Some of Cutlass's sailors lived with injuries long after her hull was repaired. Families on both sides buried the dead. The world eventually signed agreements, reopened trade routes and decided which names to put on monuments.

The ocean kept the rest.

After the war I lectured at the Naval War College. I showed maps, explained what we could confirm about the coordinated attacks, and described the difficulties of making decisions with incomplete information.

At the end of every lecture I turned off the projector.

I told the young officers about Jacob Rios, who thought he had failed because he could not carry everyone from the fire. I told them about Chief Ramirez, who went back for her shipmates. I told them about Captain Ward, who stayed aboard a sinking freighter until she had searched every accessible space. I told them about Edward Mallory, whose wounded crew kept fighting to save the other ships. And I told them about Anders Nygaard, the fisherman who went into a war zone to rescue sailors wearing the wrong flag.

Then I told them a captain's rank does not make him important. A ship's weapons do not make its crew brave. Authority is not a prize, and command is not a stage.

A commanding officer exists to carry responsibility that cannot be passed to anyone else.

Three years after the battle, I retired.

Northstar was alongside in Norfolk for my final ceremony. Her hull had been repaired, her equipment updated, her paint renewed. She looked better than she had when I first took command.

I asked to visit engineering one last time.

A young lieutenant commander proudly showed me the upgraded systems. Fresh cable runs, new monitoring equipment, immaculate decks. We stopped near the place where the fire had burned.

On the bulkhead was a brass plaque.

**CHIEF PETTY OFFICER ELENA RAMIREZ, UNITED STATES NAVY**

**WHO GAVE HER LIFE SAVING HER SHIPMATES**

**18 NOVEMBER 2037**

Beneath her name were four words:

**WE KEEP THE WATCH.**

I stood at attention and saluted.

The engineer waited quietly behind me. He had not been there that day. He knew the story, but he did not know the smell of the smoke, the sound of the damaged radio, or how Elena had managed a weak laugh when I asked her to get out.

I hoped he never would.

That evening I sat on our back porch with Ethan. He was twenty-one, studying engineering, and taller than me.

"Dad," he said, "I've been thinking about working on emergency power systems for ships."

I stared out at the yard.

"Not what I expected to hear when you decided against the Navy."

"I'm still not joining. I want to build things that people need, without making the decisions you had to make."

"That's a worthy ambition."

He looked toward me.

"Did you ever think you wouldn't come back?"

"Every day of that crossing."

He studied my hands.

"Were you scared?"

"Every second."

He looked surprised. "Really?"

"Really."

"But you kept going."

"So did everybody else."

He watched a bird settle on the fence.

"I want to design something that gives people a better chance when everything goes wrong."

I put a hand on his shoulder.

"Then make sure you understand why you're doing it. Engineering doesn't make the choices for you. People do."

He nodded.

We stayed outside until the light faded.

Sometimes I still dream of the ship.

I hear machinery humming through the bulkheads. I smell coffee and hot metal. I walk down the passageway and see Ramirez standing at the end of it, clean uniform, mug in hand, smiling like she has a hundred times before.

"Everything all right, Captain?" she asks.

I want to tell her that Ochoa recovered. That Rios became a sailor whose courage I would never forget. That the ferry reached land and the children grew up. That her daughter grew older, loved building things, and kept a small brass model of Northstar in her room. That thousands of ordinary days followed the night we thought might never end.

In the dream I can never quite say it.

So I answer the way a captain does.

"Everything's running, Chief."

She smiles.

"Good, sir."

And she walks away.

When I wake, I remember the lights of Aurora coming toward Iceland, and children waving through the windows.

A woman once wrote to me about her son, a little boy who had hidden under a table during the attacks. She told me that he wanted to become a sailor, not to fight, but to protect people who were frightened.

I keep that letter near my desk.

It reminds me that the meaning of a war is not found only in the ships sunk, weapons fired, or orders carried out. It is found in the people who remain alive when the fighting stops.

My name is Daniel Vale. I commanded the USS Northstar through the Black Atlantic. I saw ships burn. I saw sailors die. I saw ordinary men and women do things that still leave me humbled.

And I learned this:

A warship is not great because it can destroy.

It is great when its crew has the courage and discipline to protect life.

Somewhere tonight, another destroyer is underway. Another commanding officer stands on a bridge. Another young fireman walks into an engineering space wondering whether the world will ask more of him than he knows how to give.

I hope the seas remain peaceful.

I hope every sailor comes home.

But if the darkness returns, if a convoy calls for help, and if somebody must stand between innocent people and a threat beyond the horizon, I hope there will always be sailors who answer.

Because the ocean is vast and indifferent, and the watch can be lonely.

But behind that gray ship may be someone who cannot defend themselves.

That is why we sail.

That is why we serve.

**And that is why the watch never ends.**
